### Executive Summary: Pricing Experiment Interpretation

The observed "zero sensitivity" in conversion rates is a **lagging indicator** and likely a false signal of price inelasticity. In enterprise sales, price resistance rarely manifests as immediate deal loss; it manifests as **friction**. The 12-day elongation in deal cycles is the true leading indicator. Your hypothesis that "feature fit drives decisions" is partially correct, but price acts as the friction coefficient in the approval process. A 15% increase likely triggered additional internal approval layers (e.g., requiring VP instead of Director sign-off), causing the delay without stopping the deal *yet*.

Approving a permanent global increase based solely on close-rate stability risks optimizing for short-term ARPU while degrading long-term revenue velocity and increasing future churn risk due to buyer remorse or strained stakeholder relationships.

### Framework: Evaluating the Hidden Costs of Sales Friction

To present to the CFO, use the **Revenue Velocity & Friction Cost Model**. Do not look at conversion rates in isolation. Evaluate the trade-off using these three dimensions:

#### 1. The Velocity Tax (Immediate Impact)
Calculate the revenue tied up in the elongated cycle.
*   **Formula:** `(Average Deal Value × 15% Increase) vs. (Capital Cost of 12 Days Delay)`
*   **Analysis:** If your cost of capital or sales commission accrual during the extra 12 days exceeds the margin gain from the price hike, the increase is net-negative immediately.
*   **Metric to Track:** **Sales Velocity** = `(Number of Opportunities × Average Deal Value × Win Rate) / Sales Cycle Length`. If Velocity drops despite higher Deal Value, the strategy is failing.

#### 2. The Approval Friction Index (Leading Indicator)
The 12-day delay suggests deals are hitting higher approval thresholds.
*   **Action:** Segment the closed deals by "Number of Approvers" and "Time in Legal/Procurement."
*   **Hypothesis:** The price hike pushed deals into a more rigorous procurement workflow.
*   **Risk:** Deals with higher friction have higher implementation failure rates and lower initial satisfaction.

#### 3. The "Buyer's Remorse" Cohort (6-Month Predictor)
Price sensitivity often appears post-purchase during renewal or expansion.
*   **Action:** Tag all deals closed during the experiment period as "Price-Hike Cohort."
*   **Prediction:** Monitor this cohort for **Time-to-First-Value** and **Support Ticket Volume** in the first 90 days. High friction at sale often correlates with delayed onboarding and early churn.

### Specific Leading Indicators to Track Now

To predict churn or down-sells six months out, track these leading indicators immediately rather than waiting for renewal data:

1.  **Procurement Escalation Rate:** The percentage of deals requiring legal or C-level review *after* the initial proposal. A spike here confirms the price is triggering structural friction.
2.  **Time-to-First-Value (TTFV):** Measure the days from contract signature to the customer's first "aha" moment or core feature adoption.
    *   *Why:* Elongated sales cycles often create internal skepticism. If the customer doesn't see value quickly post-sale, churn probability skyrockets.
3.  **Initial Expansion Resistance:** Track the acceptance rate of add-ons or seat expansions within the first 90 days.
    *   *Why:* Customers who felt "priced in" often freeze account growth initially, signaling a lack of commitment.
4.  **NPS/CSAT at Day 30 vs. Day 90:** Specifically survey the "Price-Hike Cohort" on "Ease of Buying." A low score here is a direct predictor of renewal churn, even if the deal closed.

### Recommendation to CFO

**Do not approve a permanent global adjustment yet.** The 12-day delay is a significant efficiency loss that may outweigh the 15% price gain when factoring in sales team capacity and cash flow timing.

**Next Step:** Run a **regional holdout test**. Apply the increase in 50% of regions and keep pricing static in the other 50%. Compare the *fully loaded* revenue velocity (including sales costs and delay impacts) over the next two quarters. If the "friction cost" of the delay exceeds the price premium in the holdout analysis, the price increase is value-destructive.
