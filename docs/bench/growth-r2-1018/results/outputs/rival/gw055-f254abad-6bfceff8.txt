### Recommended Retention Metrics for MelodyStream Social Feature Test

To determine if the social sharing feature drives sustainable growth rather than a fleeting novelty effect, you must move beyond daily active user (DAU) counts and average session duration. The executive board needs evidence of **habit formation** and **long-term value**, not just initial engagement spikes.

For a three-month evaluation period starting November 10th, implement the following specific time-based and frequency-based measurements:

#### 1. Cohort-Based Retention Curves (The Primary Signal)
Do not aggregate all users. Segment the 10,000 participants into weekly cohorts based on their first exposure to the feature.
*   **Measurement:** Plot the percentage of users from each cohort who return to the app at Day 1, Day 7, Day 14, Day 30, Day 60, and Day 90.
*   **Why it matters:** A novelty effect shows a sharp spike in Day 1–7 retention followed by a steep drop-off that never recovers. Sustainable growth shows a "flattening" of the curve where a core group stabilizes at a higher baseline than the control group. If the social feature users stabilize at a higher retention rate than non-social users by Day 30, the trade-off is acceptable.

#### 2. Frequency of Use (Sessions per User per Week)
Session duration is dropping, but frequency may be rising. Users might be engaging in shorter, more frequent bursts due to social notifications.
*   **Measurement:** Calculate the average number of sessions per user per week for the test group vs. control group, tracked weekly over the 12-week period.
*   **Why it matters:** If total weekly listening time remains stable or grows because frequency increased (even with shorter sessions), the feature is successfully integrating into daily habits. If both frequency and duration drop, the feature is cannibalizing core value.

#### 3. "Stickiness" Ratio (DAU/MAU)
*   **Measurement:** Divide Daily Active Users by Monthly Active Users for the test cohort weekly.
*   **Why it matters:** This ratio indicates how often monthly users return within a given month. A rising stickiness ratio (e.g., moving from 20% to 25%) suggests the feature is becoming a daily habit. A declining ratio despite higher DAU suggests users are popping in briefly but not establishing a routine.

#### 4. Feature-Specific Retention (The "Power User" Curve)
*   **Measurement:** Track retention specifically for users who interact with the social feature at least once in their first week vs. those who do not.
*   **Why it matters:** This isolates whether the social mechanic itself drives loyalty. If users who touch the social feature have significantly higher 60-day and 90-day retention than those who ignore it, the feature is a valid retention lever, even if it distracts casual users.

#### 5. Churn Rate by Cohort Week
*   **Measurement:** Calculate the percentage of users who completely stop using the app (zero sessions) for 14 consecutive days, measured weekly.
*   **Why it matters:** Monitor if churn accelerates after the initial 2–3 weeks. A delayed churn spike indicates the novelty wore off and the distraction factor drove users away.

### Executive Decision Framework
Present these metrics to the board with a clear threshold for success:
*   **Green Light:** Day 30 and Day 60 retention curves flatten higher than control; frequency increases enough to offset duration loss; stickiness ratio rises.
*   **Yellow Light:** Retention is flat, but frequency is up. Recommend optimizing the social interface to reduce distraction while keeping engagement high.
*   **Red Light:** Retention curves converge with or fall below control by Day 30; frequency does not compensate for duration loss. The novelty effect has faded, and the feature harms core value.

Focus the board's attention on the **Day 30 and Day 60 inflection points**. If the curves haven't separated positively by then, the behavior is likely transient.

*Note: Ensure your data pipeline excludes users who joined after November 10th from this specific test cohort to maintain statistical validity. Do not rely on averages alone; inspect the distribution to ensure a small group of super-users isn't skewing the frequency data.*
