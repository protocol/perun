**Recommendation: Prioritize Retention (Fix the Leaky Bucket)**

For Q1, your product decisions must be driven by **retention metrics**, specifically reversing the Day-7 decline from 30% to 22%. While the 25% surge in downloads is encouraging, pouring engineering resources into activation now would be inefficient and potentially damaging to long-term growth.

**The Core Argument**
Your assumption is likely correct: the download spike has diluted your user base with casual trialists, causing the retention slip. However, the solution is not to optimize for activation (getting users to the "aha" moment faster) if that "aha" moment does not lead to long-term value. If you improve activation without fixing the underlying retention issue, you will simply help casual users realize faster that the app isn't for them, potentially increasing your churn rate or attracting even lower-quality users who drop off after Day 1.

In your current stage, **retention is the constraint on growth**. A 22% Day-7 retention rate means 78% of your new acquisition budget and engineering effort is wasted within a week. Fixing this "leak" ensures that any future activation improvements or marketing spends compound rather than evaporate. Dedicated creators (your target) are defined by their return behavior, not just their initial signup. Therefore, the engineering budget should focus on identifying *why* users leave between Day 1 and Day 7 and solving those specific friction points.

**Historical Patterns and Data Comparisons Needed**
To validate this direction for the CEO, present the following data comparisons immediately:

1.  **Cohort Retention Curves (Pre- vs. Post-Sept 15):**
    Plot the Day 1, Day 3, and Day 7 retention curves for users acquired before September 15 against those acquired after.
    *   *What to look for:* If the post-Sept 15 curve drops sharply between Day 1 and Day 3 while the pre-Sept 15 curve stays flat, the issue is immediate value mismatch (casual users). If both curves look similar but the new cohort is just larger, the issue might be server load or diluted support, not user quality.

2.  **Activation vs. Retention Correlation:**
    Analyze users who completed your current "activation" events (e.g., first edit, first export) in the new cohort. Did they retain at Day 7?
    *   *What to look for:* If users who "activate" are *still* churning by Day 7, your current definition of activation is flawed. Improving the flow to get more people to this step will not solve retention. You need to find the behavior that *actually* correlates with Day 30 retention (e.g., creating 3+ projects) and make that the new north star.

3.  **Source/Medium Quality Split:**
    Break down the 50,000 installs by source. Did a specific new channel (e.g., a viral TikTok campaign or a new ad network) drive the 25% increase?
    *   *What to look for:* Compare Day-7 retention by source. If one specific channel has 10% retention while organic remains at 30%, the problem is acquisition targeting, not the product. In this case, the "engineering" fix might be pausing that channel rather than changing the app.

4.  **Feature Usage of Retained vs. Churned:**
    Compare the feature usage of the 22% who stayed vs. the 78% who left during the first week.
    *   *What to look for:* Identify the "sticky" features used exclusively by the retained group. This tells you exactly what to highlight in the onboarding flow later, ensuring you optimize for *quality* activation, not just speed.

**Conclusion for the CEO**
Allocating resources to retention now protects the lifetime value of the 50,000 new users. Once Day-7 retention stabilizes back toward 30%, *then* we can safely invest in activation to widen the funnel, knowing that the users we acquire will stick.

*Note: Ensure your analytics events for "first edit" and "export" are firing correctly before drawing final conclusions, as tracking errors can mimic retention drops.*
*Do not run A/B tests on onboarding flows until the baseline retention leak is plugged.*
