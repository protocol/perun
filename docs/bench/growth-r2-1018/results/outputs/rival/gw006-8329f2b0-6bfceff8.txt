**Recommended Metric: 60-Day Active Cooking Rate (ACR)**

**Definition**
The 60-Day Active Cooking Rate is the percentage of users in a specific cohort (Referred vs. Organic) who log into the platform to perform a "meal management action" (skipping a week, swapping a recipe, or rating a delivered meal) at least once every 30 days during their first two months.

Unlike simple login rates or delivery acceptance, this metric isolates *intent*. A user who accepts a delivery but never logs in to manage it is passive; a user who logs in to skip or swap is actively engaging with the service's core value proposition: flexibility and customization.

**Measurement Strategy**
To validate your assumption about commitment, you must segment this metric by acquisition source and compare the curves:

1.  **Data Source:** Pull event-level data from your analytics platform for `login`, `recipe_swap`, `week_skip`, and `meal_rating` events.
2.  **Cohort Construction:** Create two distinct cohorts starting from their first delivery date:
    *   **Cohort A:** Users acquired via the March 15 referral program.
    *   **Cohort B:** Organic users acquired in the same period (to control for seasonality).
3.  **Calculation:**
    *   *Numerator:* Count of users in the cohort who performed at least one "meal management action" in Days 1–30 AND at least one in Days 31–60.
    *   *Denominator:* Total users in the cohort who received at least one delivery.
    *   *Formula:* `(Users Active in Month 1 AND Month 2) / Total Delivered Users`

**Behavioral Narrative for the CFO**
The current 25% higher churn rate suggests a financial leak, but it does not explain *why* users leave. The 60-Day ACR provides the behavioral context needed to decide on funding:

*   **Scenario A: Low ACR in Referred Cohort.** If referred users show significantly lower engagement depth (e.g., 15% ACR vs. 45% for organic), your assumption is correct. These users are "discount hunters" who lack product commitment. They accept the free/discounted box, do not engage with the customization features, and churn when the price normalizes.
    *   *Recommendation:* Do not increase funding. Instead, restructure the incentive to reward *retention* (e.g., "$20 off your 3rd box") rather than acquisition, filtering for users willing to engage long-term.

*   **Scenario B: Comparable ACR, High Churn.** If referred users engage with the product at similar rates to organic users (e.g., 40% ACR vs. 45%) but still churn at higher rates, the issue is not commitment or product value. The friction is likely economic or operational (e.g., they forget to cancel because the billing cycle is unclear, or the price jump after the promo is too steep).
    *   *Recommendation:* Continue funding but optimize the conversion flow. Implement proactive retention emails before the first paid cycle or offer a graduated pricing tier rather than a hard cliff.

This metric shifts the conversation from "referrals are low quality" to "referrals require a different engagement strategy," allowing the CFO to allocate budget based on fixable behavioral gaps rather than assumed user flaws.

*Ensure your analytics team validates that "meal management actions" are tracked as distinct events and not lumped into generic pageviews.*
