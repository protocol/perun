### Interpretation of Conflicting Signals

The data suggests a classic **volume-quality trade-off**. The 40% increase in invitations confirms the $5 incentive effectively motivates existing users to share. However, the drop in repeat order rate (25% → 18%) indicates the *new* referred users are less valuable long-term than those acquired under the $3 model. This is likely due to **adverse selection**: the higher reward attracts "reward hunters" (users motivated by the credit rather than food quality) or encourages existing users to refer lower-intent friends just to claim the bonus.

The hypothesis that the reward "cannibalized first order value" is less likely to explain the *repeat* rate drop unless the $5 credit made the first order free, removing the financial commitment that often correlates with retention. If the first order was heavily discounted or free, users may have less psychological investment in returning.

### Critical Data Points & Segmentation Needed

To resolve whether this is a user quality issue or a structural flaw in the reward, request the following breakdowns immediately:

1.  **Referrer Segmentation**: Split the referred users by the *type* of referrer.
    *   *High-Value Referrers*: Did users who normally order frequently refer different quality friends under the $5 offer compared to the $3 offer?
    *   *Low-Value/Inactive Referrers*: Did the $5 offer activate dormant users to refer friends? If so, these new referrals are likely low-quality regardless of the reward amount. If the drop in quality is concentrated here, the issue is *who* is referring, not necessarily the reward amount itself.

2.  **First Order Economics**: Analyze the **Net First Order Value** (Order Value minus Discount/Credit).
    *   Did the average order value of referred users drop significantly? If the $5 credit covered the entire cost of the first meal, you attracted users with zero skin in the game.
    *   Compare the **Cost Per Acquired Retained User (CPARU)**. Even with a lower retention rate, does the higher volume of referrals at $5 result in more *total* retained users per dollar spent than the $3 model?
    *   *Calculation*: If 100 referrals @ $3 = 25 retained users (Cost: $75 total / 25 users = $3 CPA). If 140 referrals @ $5 = 25.2 retained users (Cost: $700 total / 25.2 users = $27.7 CPA). The volume gain must outweigh the efficiency loss.

3.  **Time-to-Second-Order Cohorts**:
    *   Plot the days between Order 1 and Order 2 for both groups. If the $5 group orders again but significantly slower, they are still valuable, just slower to convert. If they simply never return, they are truly low quality.

4.  **Geographic/Restaurant Overlap**:
    *   Ensure the drop isn't due to the new referrals clustering in areas with poor delivery times or limited restaurant options, which would be an operational issue, not a referral incentive issue.

### Decision Framework for Friday

Do not roll out globally yet. The 28% relative drop in retention (25% to 18%) is severe. Unless the **total number of retained users** has increased significantly enough to justify a potentially 9x increase in acquisition cost (as illustrated in the CPARU example above), the $5 model is likely burning budget on low-LTV users.

**Recommendation**: Pause global rollout. Run a targeted test where the $5 credit is unlocked *only after* the friend's second order, or restrict the higher incentive to referrers with a minimum historical order value. This aligns the incentive with quality rather than just volume.
