### Q4 Budget Allocation Recommendation: Prioritize Validated LTV Over Speculative CAC

**Executive Summary**
Do not optimize bidding strategies for the finance team's assumed 18-month repeat purchase cycle. With a current repeat rate of only 8%, building your Q4 strategy on the expectation of matching chair sales is financially dangerous. Optimizing for low Customer Acquisition Cost (CAC) alone will likely attract one-time bargain hunters, while optimizing for unproven Lifetime Value (LTV) will cause you to overbid and burn cash on customers who never return.

**The Core Trade-off Analysis**
Your decision rests on the gap between *assumed* behavior and *actual* behavior.

1.  **The Risk of CAC Optimization:** If you bid strictly for lowest CAC, algorithms will find users likely to make a single $950 purchase but unlikely to return. Given your 8% repeat rate, 92% of customers are currently one-time buyers. Minimizing CAC reinforces this behavior, maximizing short-term efficiency but capping long-term growth.
2.  **The Risk of LTV Optimization:** If you bid based on the finance team's assumption (high LTV via chair purchases), you will bid aggressively to acquire customers. If the 18-month return rate remains at 8% rather than the projected ~40-50% needed to justify sofa margins, you will acquire customers at a cost higher than their actual lifetime value, resulting in significant losses.

**Recommendation:** Adopt a **"Verified First-Order Profitability"** strategy for Q4. Bid to acquire customers where the first transaction (sofa/dining set) is nearly breakeven or slightly profitable *without* relying on repeat purchases. Treat any repeat revenue as pure upside bonus, not a requirement for media efficiency. This protects your $300k budget from over-extension while still allowing growth.

**Critical Data Points to Gather Before the Meeting**
To validate or refute the repeat purchase assumption immediately, request these four specific data cuts from your analytics team tonight:

1.  **Cohort-Based Repeat Rate by Product:** Do not look at the aggregate 8%. Segment by "Sofa Buyers" vs. "Dining Set Buyers." Is the 8% dragged down by dining sets, while sofa buyers actually return at 25%? If sofa buyers are also <10%, the cross-sell hypothesis is dead.
2.  **Time-to-Second-Purchase Distribution:** Of the 8% who *did* return, how many bought within 6 months vs. 12-18 months? If most repeats happen within 6 months, the "18-month" assumption is masking a faster decay curve. If no one has bought yet in the 12-18 month window (because those cohorts are too new), the assumption is purely theoretical.
3.  **Category Affinity Analysis:** What are the actual second purchases? Are sofa buyers buying chairs, or are they buying rugs and throws (lower AOV)? If they aren't buying matching chairs, the specific "matching chair" LTV model is invalid.
4.  **Attribution of Second Purchase:** Did returning customers come back via organic search/brand recall, or did they require paid retargeting? If they require paid ads to return, your "organic LTV" calculation is inflated by hidden reacquisition costs.

**Strategic Argument for the CEO**
"Optimizing for a repeat rate that doesn't exist yet is a bet, not a strategy. We should allocate the $300k to acquire customers who make economic sense on day one. We will test the 'matching chair' hypothesis with a dedicated email/SMS flow to recent buyers rather than betting our entire media budget on it. If the data shows sofa buyers naturally return at >20% within 6 months, we can shift bidding to LTV in Q1."

*Note: Ensure your analytics tracking distinguishes between "cross-sell" (different category) and "repeat" (same category) to accurately measure the chair hypothesis.*
*Do not commit to LTV bidding until the cohort data confirms the 18-month window contains actual completed purchases, not just projections.*
