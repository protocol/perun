**Recommendation: Do Not Roll Out Any Variant Yet**

Based on the data provided, you cannot make a statistically valid go/no-go decision for a full market rollout today. The conflicting signals between conversion volume and Average Order Value (AOV), combined with device-specific divergence, indicate that no single variant is universally superior. Rolling out one version risks alienating 50% of your user base or sacrificing total revenue for higher booking counts.

**How to Interpret the Data and Resolve the Contradiction**

To present this to your Product Director, frame the analysis around **Total Revenue Impact** rather than isolated conversion rates, and acknowledge the statistical limitations of your current sample.

1.  **Shift the Primary Metric to Revenue Per Visitor (RPV)**
    Conversion rate is a vanity metric if it comes at the cost of ticket size. Variant B may drive more bookings on Android, but if those bookings are for cheaper rides, total revenue may be lower than the Control.
    *   **Action:** Calculate RPV for each variant *within* each device segment.
    *   *Formula:* `(Total Bookings × AOV) / Total Visitors in Segment`.
    *   If Variant B has a 12% higher conversion rate but a significantly lower AOV, its RPV might be flat or negative compared to Control. The "winner" is the variant with the highest RPV, not the highest conversion rate.

2.  **Address the Statistical Significance Gap**
    You mentioned 4,000 users per variant. With a multi-variant test (Control + B + C), you have split your traffic three ways. When you further slice this by device (Android vs. iOS), your sample size per cell drops drastically (likely ~2,000 per device per variant).
    *   **The Risk:** A 12% lift on a small sample often falls within the margin of error. Without a calculated p-value or confidence interval, you cannot rule out random noise.
    *   **The Argument:** Tell your director that declaring a winner now creates a high risk of a "false positive." If you roll out Variant B and it turns out to be noise, you may degrade the iOS experience (where it likely underperformed) without actually gaining the Android lift.

3.  **Diagnose the Interaction Effect**
    The fact that B wins on Android and C wins on iOS suggests an **interaction effect**, not a main effect. The layout changes are interacting with device-specific UI constraints (e.g., thumb reach on Android vs. screen real estate on iOS).
    *   **Conclusion:** A single global rollout is the wrong solution. The data suggests you need a **device-specific implementation** *if* the results hold up, not a one-size-fits-all change.

**Immediate Next Steps for the Director**

*   **Decision:** Extend the test for 7–10 more days to gather sufficient sample size for device-segmented significance, or implement a **conditional rollout** (Variant B for Android, Variant C for iOS) *only* if the RPV calculation confirms both beat the Control in their respective segments.
*   **Guardrail Check:** Verify if the drop in AOV for the high-conversion variants is due to users selecting cheaper ride options (behavioral change) or just random variance. If users are downgrading rides to complete booking faster, Variant B might be cannibalizing revenue.

Do not roll out the Control either; while it has the highest AOV, it may be leaving booking volume on the table. The correct path is a segmented approach pending revenue validation.
