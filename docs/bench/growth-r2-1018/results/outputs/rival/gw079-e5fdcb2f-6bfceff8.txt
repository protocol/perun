**Recommendation: Track "Percentage of Orders Delivered Hot" as your Primary KPI.**

Do not use "Average Total Delivery Time" as your primary success metric for the Guadalajara launch. While speed is easier to measure, it is a proxy that fails to capture the actual customer experience in this specific context. Your goal is customer satisfaction and retention, not just logistical velocity. If you optimize strictly for speed, you risk incentivizing drivers to rush through dangerous traffic conditions or pressuring restaurants to cut corners on food quality, neither of which sustains a business.

**Why "Delivered Hot" Wins for Actionability**

The core unknown in your launch is the local definition of quality. In many Mexican markets, food temperature and freshness often outweigh marginal speed differences. A taco delivered in 20 minutes but lukewarm is a failed product; a taco delivered in 38 minutes but piping hot is a satisfied customer.

By tracking "Percentage of Orders Delivered Hot," you force the operations team to solve the *real* bottleneck, whether it is traffic or preparation:

1.  **If the metric fails due to Traffic:** Drivers will naturally report that they picked up hot food but lost heat in transit. This signals a need for better insulation bags, optimized routing that prioritizes direct paths over shortest distance, or dynamic delivery radii that shrink during peak congestion.
2.  **If the metric fails due to Preparation:** Drivers will report picking up food that was already cooling down. This immediately identifies specific restaurants with slow kitchen workflows, allowing you to target training or adjust quoted prep times for those specific partners.

"Average Delivery Time" masks these distinctions. An average of 32 minutes could hide a reality where half your customers receive cold food because the other half got incredibly fast deliveries, skewing the data and leaving you blind to the root cause of dissatisfaction.

**Implementation Strategy for the Operations Manager**

Since "hot" is subjective, you must operationalize this metric immediately:

*   **Define the Proxy:** Do not rely on sensors yet. Define "Delivered Hot" as "Orders completed within the target window AND receiving a 4+ star rating on food temperature/quality." Alternatively, use a strict time-from-restaurant-arrival threshold (e.g., must reach customer within 15 minutes of pickup) as a temporary proxy until survey data validates the specific heat retention curve for local cuisine.
*   **The 35-Minute Rule:** Treat your 35-minute assumption as a guardrail, not the goal. Aim for the highest percentage of "hot" deliveries, even if the average time creeps to 38 minutes initially.
*   **Driver Training:** Train drivers to prioritize food integrity over shaving off two minutes. Incentivize them on the "Hot Delivery" rate, not just trips per hour. This aligns their behavior with customer retention.
*   **Restaurant Onboarding:** Use the data to identify which of the 500 restaurants consistently cause "cold" deliveries. Is it their packaging? Their cook time? This allows you to segment your 500 partners into "ready" vs. "needs support" buckets within the first week.

**Conclusion**

Optimizing for "Hot" automatically optimizes for speed where it matters, but optimizing for speed does not guarantee quality. For a Regional Operations Manager hiring and training staff, "Percentage Delivered Hot" provides a clear, binary signal for every failed order: Was it the drive or the kitchen? This clarity is essential for rapid iteration in a new market.

*Note: Validate the specific temperature expectation via a one-question post-order survey in week one to refine your definition of "hot."*
*Ensure your driver app allows a simple "food temp at pickup" check-box to help distinguish kitchen delays from transit cooling.*
