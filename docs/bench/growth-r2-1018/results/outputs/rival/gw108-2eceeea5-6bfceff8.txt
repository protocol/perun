**Executive Summary: Default Privacy Settings A/B Test**

The test reveals a classic tension between long-term value (retention) and immediate usability (breakage). While a 2% retention lift is strategically valuable, a 15% surge in breakage reports represents an acute trust violation that often precedes churn. You cannot currently recommend a global rollout because the "net impact" is obscured by the unknown distribution of the breakage.

**Interpreting the Trade-offs**

*   **The Retention Gain (2%):** In a browser with 1M users, a 2% retention increase compounds significantly over a year. This validates your hypothesis that users value privacy long-term. However, retention is a lagging indicator; users may stay despite breakage simply because switching browsers is high-friction, not because they are satisfied.
*   **The Breakage Cost (15%):** This is the critical risk. If breakage is concentrated on "high-frequency" sites (e.g., banking, email, major news), users experience daily friction, leading to eventual churn that the 2% gain cannot offset. If breakage is spread across obscure sites, the impact is negligible for most users.

**Recommendation: Do Not Roll Out Globally Yet**

The current data suggests a "false positive" on safety. Users might be retaining *despite* the breakage, not because of the privacy settings. Rolling out globally risks alienating users who hit broken sites on Day 1, before they ever realize the privacy benefit.

**Required Data Slices for Decision Making**

To make a confident recommendation to the CPO, you must segment the breakage data immediately:

1.  **Breakage by Site Frequency (The "Top 100" Test):**
    *   Cross-reference breakage reports against your internal telemetry of the top 1,000 most-visited domains.
    *   *Decision Rule:* If >20% of breakage comes from the top 100 sites, **do not roll out**. The friction is too visible. If breakage is concentrated in the "long tail" (low-traffic sites), the risk is manageable.

2.  **Breakage by User Segment:**
    *   Are breakage reports coming from power users (who visit many sites) or casual users?
    *   Are specific geographies or device types (mobile vs. desktop) disproportionately affected? Mobile breakage is often more frustrating due to smaller screens and inability to easily "view source" or debug.

3.  **Correlation of Breakage to Churn:**
    *   Did users who filed a breakage report show lower retention than those who didn't?
    *   *Hypothesis:* If users experiencing breakage have *lower* retention than the control group, the 2% aggregate gain is being dragged down by severe usability issues. The "true" retention gain for users *without* breakage might be 5%+, suggesting a targeted rollout is better than a global one.

4.  **Severity Classification:**
    *   Distinguish between "hard breaks" (site won't load) and "soft breaks" (login fails, layout shifted). Hard breaks on core utilities are unacceptable; soft breaks might be tolerable if communicated well.

**Strategic Path Forward**

Propose a **"Smart Default"** strategy instead of a blanket rollout:
*   Enable strict protection by default *except* for a whitelist of known problematic high-traffic sites (based on the data slice above).
*   Implement a one-time interstitial for users hitting a broken site: "WebSafe blocked a tracker that broke this site. Would you like to disable protection for this site?" This turns a frustration into a trust-building moment of transparency.

**Note:** The 2% retention gain is promising but fragile if usability suffers on daily-use sites. Prioritize the "Top 100" site analysis before any Q2 decisions.
