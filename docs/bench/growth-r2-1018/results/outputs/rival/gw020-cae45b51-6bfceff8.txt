**Recommendation: Do Not Implement Site-Wide Yet**

Based on the data provided, implementing these changes site-wide is not recommended. While the 12% increase in add-to-cart actions indicates successful top-of-funnel engagement, the statistically insignificant 2% lift in final checkout conversion fails to validate the core business hypothesis: that social proof drives revenue for high-ticket items.

**Interpreting the Disconnect**
The divergence between mid-funnel (add-to-cart) and bottom-funnel (purchase) metrics suggests the "bestseller" badges and review counts effectively reduce the friction of *consideration* but do not resolve the friction of *commitment*. In the luxury jewelry category, a $2,000 purchase involves a different psychological threshold than lower-ticket items. Social proof validates popularity, but it does not necessarily address specific luxury purchase barriers such as authenticity verification, return policy confidence, or personalized service expectations.

The data supports your hypothesis that users are utilizing the cart as a wishlist. The surge in add-to-cart actions without a corresponding revenue lift indicates that while the badges encourage users to "save" the item for later consideration, they do not provide the final nudge required to complete the transaction. If the cart is functioning as a wishlist, the metric "add-to-cart" has shifted from a high-intent signal to a low-intent browsing behavior in this test context.

**Strategic Next Steps**
1.  **Re-evaluate the Primary Metric:** For future tests on high-ticket items, "add-to-cart" is a vanity metric if it does not correlate with revenue. Focus exclusively on checkout completion or initiated checkout as the primary success metric.
2.  **Investigate the "Wishlist" Behavior:** Before gathering more reviews, validate the wishlist hypothesis. Segment the test data to see if the variant drove more returning visitors to the cart without purchasing, or if session duration increased without conversion. If the cart is being used as a wishlist, consider adding a distinct "Save for Later" button to clean up your funnel data and distinguish between browsing and buying intent.
3.  **Refine the Hypothesis:** Gathering more reviews is not a wasted effort, but the *presentation* of social proof may need adjustment. For luxury goods, try testing specific, detailed testimonials regarding quality and service rather than aggregate counts or "bestseller" labels, which may feel too mass-market for high-end buyers.

**Conclusion for the Marketing Director**
The current test proves that social proof increases product interest but fails to drive incremental revenue. Investing heavily in gathering more reviews based on this specific implementation would likely yield diminishing returns. We should pause site-wide implementation, further investigate the cart-as-wishlist behavior, and redesign the social proof elements to specifically address high-ticket purchase anxieties rather than general popularity.

*Note: Ensure future tests run long enough to capture the longer consideration cycles typical of luxury purchases, as a 21-day window may miss delayed conversions.*
