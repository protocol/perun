**Analysis of Time-Dependent Frequency Effects**

Your data reveals a classic engagement-fatigue curve where short-term vanity metrics (open rates) masked long-term value erosion (retention). The 10% initial open rate lift was likely driven by novelty or increased surface area, not deeper user value. The critical failure signal is the 3.1x increase in unsubscribe rates (2.5% vs. 0.8%) by week four. In growth analytics, retention is the primary constraint; acquiring attention only to lose the user entirely is a net negative lifetime value event. The "delayed" effect confirms that fatigue is cumulative, not immediate, validating your hypothesis that the fourth week represents a tipping point for this cohort.

**Strategic Recommendation: Dynamic Frequency Capping**

Do not adopt a static "five emails per week" or "three emails per week" rule. Instead, propose a **Dynamic Frequency Strategy** tied to a user-specific fatigue threshold:

1.  **Phase 1 (Acquisition/Re-engagement):** Allow up to 5 emails/week for the first 21 days of a user's journey or re-engagement window to capture the initial 10% open rate lift.
2.  **Phase 2 (Retention Guardrail):** Automatically cap frequency to 3 emails/week immediately after day 21, or sooner if a user skips two consecutive emails. This preemptively avoids the week-four churn cliff observed in your test.
3.  **Metric Shift:** Stop optimizing for "Open Rate" as the primary success metric for frequency tests. It is a vanity metric in this context. Shift the North Star to **Net Subscriber Growth** (New Subs – Unsubscribes) or **Long-term Retention Rate** (users active after 60 days). A higher open rate that drives unsubscribes is a failure, not a win.
