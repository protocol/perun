**Recommendation: Prioritize Retention Metrics Immediately**

Do not allocate engineering resources to widen the top of the funnel. Your current data indicates a critical "leaky bucket" scenario where acquisition efficiency is collapsing. Pouring more users into a product with declining retention is the most expensive mistake a growth-stage company can make.

**The Argument for the CEO**
Your downloads increased by 25% (to 50,000), yet Day-7 retention dropped from 30% to 22%. This is not a trade-off between "casual" and "dedicated" users; it is a signal that the product value proposition is failing to stick for a growing segment of your audience.

If you prioritize activation to boost top-of-funnel numbers without fixing retention, you will inflate your Customer Acquisition Cost (CAC) while lowering Lifetime Value (LTV). Mathematically, a 22% retention rate means you are losing 78% of your new users within a week. Acquiring more users to compensate for this churn creates a "treadmill effect" where growth requires exponentially higher spend just to maintain flat active user counts.

Your assumption that better onboarding might lower user quality is a false dichotomy. Effective onboarding does not "trick" users into staying; it ensures that users who *can* derive value from your core editing features actually find them. If onboarding improvements cause "casual" users to leave faster, that is a success—it stops you from wasting server costs and support resources on users who were never going to convert. The goal is not to maximize *installs*; it is to maximize *active creators*.

**Growth Stage Context**
You are past the `prototype` phase (where talking to users is the only metric) and firmly in the `growth` phase. In this stage, the primary directive is to secure the base before scaling. A North Star metric based on "Weekly Active Editors" (a value-based action) will flatline or decline if Day-7 retention continues to drop, regardless of how many new downloads you buy. Retention is the foundation; activation is the lever; acquisition is the multiplier. You cannot multiply a broken foundation.

**Required Data Comparisons to Validate the Decision**
To confirm this direction and define the specific engineering initiative, pull these three data slices from your analytics immediately:

1.  **Cohort Overlap Analysis:** Segment the post-September 15th cohort by traffic source. Did the retention drop occur across *all* channels, or only in specific new acquisition campaigns? If the drop is universal, the issue is likely a product change or bug introduced in the update. If it is isolated to specific channels, you are buying low-quality traffic, and the fix is marketing, not product.
2.  **Activation vs. Retention Correlation:** Identify the specific "Aha!" moment (e.g., "exported first edited photo"). Compare the percentage of users hitting this moment in the old cohort (30% retention) vs. the new cohort (22% retention). If activation rates dropped proportionally with retention, your onboarding flow is broken. If activation is stable but retention still fell, the issue lies in ongoing engagement or feature utility after the first use.
3.  **The "Leak" Point:** Map the drop-off days. Did users leave on Day 1 (onboarding failure), Day 3 (feature discovery failure), or Day 7 (lack of recurring value)? Engineering effort must target the specific day where the steepest drop occurs.

**Immediate Next Step**
Pause all new user acquisition spending increases. Direct the single available engineering initiative toward diagnosing and fixing the specific drop-off point identified in the "Leak Point" analysis above. Do not consider the quarter successful until Day-7 retention returns to at least 28%.

*Note: Do not fabricate industry benchmarks for "good" retention; compare only against your own pre-September 15th baseline. Ensure any user tracking uses pseudonymous IDs to comply with privacy standards.*
