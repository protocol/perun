**Decision: Do not roll out the $5 offer globally yet; revert to the $3 baseline or pause the experiment until specific segmentation data is analyzed.**

The 40% increase in invitations coupled with a 7-point drop in repeat order rate (25% → 18%) indicates a classic "quantity over quality" failure. The higher incentive likely attracted referrers who spammed low-intent users or referred friends who only converted for the credit, not the product value. Before Friday's budget decision, you must isolate whether the drop in retention is caused by **who** was referred (user quality) or **how** they were treated (cannibalization).

Request the following three specific data cuts from your analytics team immediately:

1.  **Referrer Segmentation:** Split the referred users into two cohorts: those referred by "power users" (high historical order frequency) vs. "casual users." If the retention drop is concentrated entirely among referrals from casual users, the $5 incentive triggered spammy behavior from low-quality referrers. If the drop is uniform across all referrer types, the incentive itself is attracting low-intent new users regardless of the source.
2.  **First-Order Value vs. Subsequent Behavior:** Compare the average order value (AOV) of the first order for the $5 cohort against the $3 cohort. If the $5 cohort has a significantly lower first-order AOV, the reward is cannibalizing revenue immediately. Crucially, check if the *second* order value normalizes. If the first order is low but the second matches the control group, the issue is purely acquisition cost efficiency. If the second order remains low or non-existent, the user quality is fundamentally broken.
3.  **Time-to-Second-Order Cohort:** Analyze the days between first and second order. A shift from a 7-day average to a 20-day average suggests the users are still interested but less urgent, whereas a complete drop-off suggests they were purely mercenary. This distinguishes between a "delayed retention" problem and a "churned immediately" problem.

**This Week's Step:** Pull the retention curve for the $5 cohort split by referrer activity level by Thursday EOD. If the retention of referrals from top-tier users remains near 25%, limit the $5 rollout to a targeted campaign for power users only. If retention collapses across all segments, kill the $5 test and investigate non-monetary referral incentives.

*Note: The 7-point retention drop represents a 28% relative decline in user lifetime value potential, which likely outweighs the marginal gain in acquisition volume unless the cost per acquisition dropped drastically.*
