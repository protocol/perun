The claim is that acquiring the dominant but unprofitable video platform for $1.65 billion creates sufficient strategic value to justify extreme financial and legal risk. The steelman posits that owning the de facto standard for online video secures the buyer's long-term relevance in a shifting media landscape, neutralizing a competitor while acquiring massive user engagement data that cannot be organically grown fast enough to matter.

However, the skepticism required here is severe. The target has no meaningful revenue, exploding bandwidth costs, and an existential threat from copyright litigation that could result in statutory damages far exceeding the purchase price. The "better way" analysis suggests continuing to build the buyer's existing, compliant video service or waiting for the target to collapse under legal pressure or operational costs, potentially acquiring assets for pennies later. The pre-mortem is clear: the acquisition closes, media companies sue immediately, courts rule against fair use, and the buyer inherits billions in liabilities while struggling to monetize content that advertisers fear due to brand safety concerns. The cost of delay is losing user mindshare, but the cost of action could be insolvency or a decade of litigation.

The kill criteria are missing. There is no defined metric for successful integration, no cap on legal liability exposure, and no proven path to profitability given the current bandwidth-to-revenue ratio. The proposal relies on the assumption that copyright law will bend to accommodate user-generated content at scale, a legal theory untested in 2006. Furthermore, paying in stock at a high valuation dilutes existing shareholders for an asset with negative cash flow and undefined legal standing.

While the strategic logic of "owning the category" is compelling, the financial and legal premises are currently unsupported by facts. The risk profile is not just high; it is unquantifiable. Without a concrete plan to resolve the copyright overhang or a proven ad model that covers bandwidth, this is a speculative bet disguised as a strategic necessity. The failure scenario (massive lawsuits + continued losses) is plausible and catastrophic.

DECISION: NO-GO
