The claim is that a $699 Wi-Fi-connected juicer with a proprietary subscription model will succeed in the 2016 consumer hardware market. The steelman posits that convenience and freshness verification justify a massive premium over standard juicing, creating a recurring revenue moat.

However, the attack reveals fatal flaws in unit economics and consumer behavior. First, the $699 entry price is prohibitive; successful kitchen appliances typically penetrate the market at under $200 before scaling to premium tiers. Second, the "proprietary bag" model forces a per-serving cost significantly higher than fresh produce, violating the core value proposition of juicing (cost-effective health). Third, requiring Wi-Fi for a mechanical pressing function introduces unnecessary friction and failure points without delivering commensurate value; freshness checks can be achieved via simple packaging indicators, not cloud dependency.

The "better way" analysis shows that a non-connected device using standard produce or reusable containers would lower the barrier to entry and increase total addressable market, even if it sacrifices subscription lock-in. The current proposal bets on a "razor-and-blades" model where the razor is too expensive and the blades are too costly for the average consumer.

Pre-mortem: The device launches, early adopters buy it, but churn is immediate because the ongoing cost per juice exceeds buying a cold-pressed juice at a retail store. The hardware becomes e-waste, and the subscription base never reaches the scale required to sustain the custom engineering and logistics of pre-packed bags. The market for high-end juicers exists, but not for one that restricts input to expensive, single-use proprietary bags while demanding an internet connection.

The strongest attack survived is the potential for a luxury niche; however, even luxury buyers resent being locked into inflated consumable costs without superior quality justification. The proposal fails the "fit" test against basic consumer hardware economics of 2016. The kill criterion is clear: if the cost per serving exceeds $8, adoption will stall. Given the likely pricing of proprietary bags to recoup R&D, this threshold will be breached.

DECISION: NO-GO
