Steelman: Acquiring the target secures a sticky, high-margin enterprise segment (construction/business) that the buyer cannot currently serve, creating immediate revenue synergy despite technical friction.

However, the attack on this claim reveals fatal flaws in the 2005 context. First, the "different, incompatible technologies" constraint is not merely an integration cost; it is a structural barrier to the primary value driver of modern wireless: seamless data. Running a legacy network indefinitely traps capital in depreciating assets while diverting engineering focus from next-generation data rollout. The proposal admits the target is "limited for data," yet the industry trajectory in 2005 demands data expansion, not legacy voice maintenance.

Second, the $36 billion valuation against a target with limited data capacity suggests the buyer is paying a premium for a shrinking asset class (specialized voice) while inheriting massive technical debt. The "large goodwill" risk indicates the price exceeds tangible assets, relying on synergies that the technical incompatibility actively prevents. You cannot easily merge incompatible networks without degrading service for the very high-value customers you aim to retain, or spending exorbitantly to rebuild their infrastructure from scratch.

Third, the "do nothing" alternative or a "partnership" model offers access to the customer base without the balance sheet toxicity of a full merger. A commercial agreement to resell the push-to-talk service avoids the $36 billion outlay and the integration nightmare. The proposal fails to demonstrate why ownership is required when the technology cannot be unified. The cost of delay in fixing the network mismatch likely exceeds the revenue gained from the acquired users, turning the deal into a value-destroying anchor.

The strongest attack survived is the revenue potential of the business base, but only if the service remains uninterrupted. Given the admitted incompatibility, service disruption is highly probable during integration, threatening the exact retention needed to justify the price. The proposal lacks a credible, funded path to migrate the legacy tech without churn.

DECISION: NO-GO
