The claim is that splitting the brand, accounts, and billing will liberate streaming strategy while isolating a declining DVD business. The steelman: distinct brands prevent the legacy DVD decline from dragging down streaming valuation and allow focused management for each trajectory.

However, the attack reveals fatal flaws in timing and execution. The proposal follows immediately after a 60% price hike for combined users. Executing a split now—forcing separate logins, queues, and bills—compounds the friction of the price increase with the hassle of fragmentation. Customers perceiving a "price hike plus service breakup" will likely interpret this as a hostile exit strategy rather than strategic focus, triggering massive churn.

The "better way" analysis shows the status quo (absorbing the pain of the price hike while maintaining unified convenience) or a gradual decoupling without brand fragmentation is superior. Splitting ratings and recommendations destroys the unique value proposition of a unified library, reducing overall user engagement. The risk of customer confusion is not merely operational; it is existential when trust is already fractured by the recent pricing shock.

The pre-mortem indicates that launching two bills and logins right after a 60% price jump will be viewed as double punishment. The loss of cross-service data blindsides the algorithm, degrading the product for both segments. The cost of delay (waiting until pricing anger subsides) is lower than the cost of immediate brand trauma. There is no evidence that separate branding solves the management distraction issue more effectively than internal organizational separation without customer-facing fragmentation.

The strongest attack survived is the need for distinct management focus, but the proposed mechanism (total customer-facing split) is disproportionate and ill-timed. The kill criterion is clear: if the implementation increases support tickets regarding billing access by >20% in week one, the strategy fails. Given the current volatility, the probability of exceeding this threshold is near certain. The proposal ignores the compounding negative sentiment of price hikes followed by service degradation (splitting accounts).

DECISION: NO-GO
