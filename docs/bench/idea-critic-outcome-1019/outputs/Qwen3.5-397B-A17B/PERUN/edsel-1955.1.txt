Steelman: Capturing the growing mid-priced segment with a distinct brand prevents customer defection to competitors and maximizes lifetime value across the corporate portfolio.

However, the proposal fails the pre-mortem and fit checks against verified economic conditions. The "growing" segment cited is a lagging indicator; the mid-1950s expansion is peaking just as a significant recession (historically 1957-58) is imminent. Launching a capital-intensive division requiring a new dealer network immediately before a credit contraction and demand collapse is a catastrophic timing error. The "massive advertising" spend will yield minimal ROI when consumer confidence plummets.

Furthermore, the "perceived gap" may be an artifact of the firm's own pricing strategy rather than organic demand. Creating a new brand risks cannibalizing the existing low-priced line during a downturn when buyers trade down, not up. The cost of building a parallel dealer infrastructure creates high fixed costs exactly when flexibility is required to survive a recession. The proposal treats a cyclical peak as a structural shift.

The strongest attack survived is the fear of losing customers to the market leader. Yet, losing some share is preferable to bleeding cash on a standalone network that cannot achieve volume during a recession. The "do nothing" alternative—waiting for the recession to clear and then assessing the landscape with lower capital requirements—offers superior reversibility and survival odds. The kill criterion for any such expansion should be a stable or growing GDP forecast for the launch window; current signals point to contraction.

Launching now ignores the outside view of business cycles. The dealer network commitment is a one-way door that locks the firm into high overhead before the worst economic environment in a decade. The evidence of a "growing segment" is insufficient to override the macro-economic headwinds.

DECISION: NO-GO
