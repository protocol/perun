**Steelman:** A premium, convenience-first juicing ecosystem where hardware subsidizes recurring high-margin revenue, justified by freshness verification and ease of use for affluent early adopters.

**Attack:** The unit economics and friction points are fatal. At $699, the device targets luxury buyers, yet the proprietary bag subscription likely costs significantly more per ounce than fresh produce, creating a "double premium" that limits the total addressable market to a tiny fraction of health enthusiasts. Unlike razors, where blades are cheap, here the "blade" (produce) is a perishable commodity available cheaply elsewhere. Requiring Wi-Fi for a mechanical pressing function introduces unnecessary failure modes (connectivity loss, server shutdowns) and privacy concerns for a simple kitchen task, offering no tangible user benefit that justifies the complexity. The "freshness check" via an app solves a problem users rarely have (they can smell or see spoiled fruit) while adding a gate to consumption. In 2016, the smart home market was nascent; consumers were not yet conditioned to accept internet-dependent appliances for basic food prep, making adoption friction immense. The combination of high upfront cost, locked-in expensive consumables, and forced connectivity creates a perfect storm for rapid churn after the trial period.

**Better Way:** Sell a high-quality mechanical press without connectivity or proprietary locks, allowing users to input any produce. If a subscription is desired, offer it as an optional convenience, not a requirement. This lowers the price point, removes the "internet of things" risk, and expands the market to all juicers, not just those willing to enter a walled garden.

**Kill Criteria:** If the attachment rate for the subscription drops below 40% within three months, or if the device return rate exceeds 15% due to connectivity issues, the model fails. The cost of delay is low; waiting for smart-home maturity reduces risk.

**Verdict:** The proposal forces a complex, expensive solution onto a simple need, relying on a subscription model that fights against basic commodity pricing logic.

DECISION: NO-GO
