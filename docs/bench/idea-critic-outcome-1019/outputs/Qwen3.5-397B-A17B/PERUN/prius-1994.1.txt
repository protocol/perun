Steelman: Committing now secures a decade-long technological moat, defining the segment before regulators force a panicked, inferior rush later. However, the proposal fails the "fit" and "evidence" gates. With fuel cheap and no regulatory mandate, the market signal is absent; launching a premium, unproven technology into a price-sensitive mass market without a demand trigger is speculative gambling, not strategy.

The "better-way" analysis reveals a fatal flaw in the chosen path: a bold public schedule for 1997 mass production. This creates a one-way door with massive warranty exposure if the nickel-metal-hydride durability proves insufficient in real-world cycles—a high probability given the unproven state of the tech. Competitors are waiting for others to burn cash solving these teething issues. The superior alternative is a "two-door" approach: complete the technical validation and limited fleet testing immediately, but delay the *mass production commitment* and public schedule until durability data is empirical and regulatory signals clarify. This captures the learning curve without the catastrophic risk of a premature, high-volume recall event that could bankrupt the division.

The pre-mortem is clear: The program launches in 1997, battery degradation exceeds projections by 40% within three years due to thermal management gaps in the novel transmission, and warranty costs exceed the vehicle's margin by 3x. With no regulatory penalty for non-compliance and cheap gas, consumers reject the higher sticker price, leaving the company with unsold inventory and a tarnished reliability reputation. The cost of delay is negligible compared to the cost of failure; being second to market with a reliable product is vastly superior to being first with a defective one.

The proposal lacks a verified demand signal and treats unproven battery durability as a solved variable. It commits to irreversible high-volume tooling before the "kill criteria" (proven cycle life) are met.
