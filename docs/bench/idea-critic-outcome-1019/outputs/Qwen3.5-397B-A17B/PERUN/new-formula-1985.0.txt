The claim is that replacing the flagship formula with a sweeter alternative, validated by blind taste tests, will reverse market share losses. The steelman: sensory superiority drives purchase frequency, and removing the old formula forces migration to the superior product, maximizing efficiency.

However, the attack reveals a fatal flaw in the evidence. The taste tests isolated flavor from brand identity, creating a false positive. Consumers do not buy cola in a vacuum; they buy heritage, nostalgia, and identity. By withholding the fact that the original would be removed, the testing failed to measure the "endowment effect"—people value what they might lose more than a marginally better replacement. The data proves the new liquid tastes better in isolation, but it does not prove the *brand* survives the removal of its anchor.

The "do nothing" alternative (keeping the old formula while optionally launching the new one as a distinct product) carries lower risk. It captures the sweetness-seeking segment without alienating the core base. The proposed plan is a one-way door with catastrophic reversibility costs; once the original is withdrawn, trust is broken, and reintroducing it later appears as a capitulation rather than a strategy.

The pre-mortem is clear: launching this causes a public backlash framed as corporate arrogance erasing history. The emotional attachment risk is not a minor variable; it is the primary driver of loyalty for a century-old brand. The testing methodology ignored the product's context, rendering the "superiority" claim invalid for the actual business decision. There is no fallback, meaning any error is immediately fatal to the core revenue stream.

The proposal fails because it optimizes for the liquid while destroying the brand. The evidence cited (taste tests) actively contradicts the real-world constraint (emotional ownership) because the test design excluded that variable. Without a phased approach or a dual-product strategy, the risk of permanent brand damage outweighs the potential gain in taste preference.

DECISION: NO-GO
