The claim assumes open-source software alone can displace entrenched mobile OS vendors and overcome carrier control without a hardware business or revenue model. In 2005, handset makers prioritize differentiation and carrier relationships over adopting a free, unproven OS from a search advertiser. The "free" value proposition ignores the massive integration, support, and customization costs manufacturers would incur, which currently exceed the license fees of established players.

Alternatives include licensing an existing embedded OS, building a lightweight mobile search app for current platforms, or waiting for the market to mature. Doing nothing preserves capital while the mobile web ecosystem clarifies. The proposed path requires betting $50M+ on a six-person team with no product, betting against carriers who control distribution, and hoping for adoption that contradicts current manufacturer incentives.

The pre-mortem reveals failure via: (1) handset makers rejecting the OS to protect existing partnerships; (2) carriers blocking non-certified devices; (3) the open-source community fragmenting the effort; or (4) the multi-year timeline exhausting capital before search revenue materializes. This is a one-way door with irreversible capital loss and high opportunity cost. The cost of delay is low; the mobile web is nascent, and waiting 12–18 months reduces technical and market uncertainty significantly.

Evidence is limited to the proposal's own admission of "no revenue model" and "carrier resistance." The $50M valuation for six people lacks comparables in 2005. The assumption that "free software pushes mobile web use" conflates availability with adoption; distribution bottlenecks (carriers) remain unsolved. No kill criteria exist for stopping development if handset partners do not sign within 12 months.

This proposal fails the fit check: it diverts massive resources from the core search-ad business into a hardware-adjacent gamble with no clear path to monetization. The strongest attack—carrier and manufacturer resistance—remains unaddressed by the "open source" lever alone.

DECISION: NO-GO
