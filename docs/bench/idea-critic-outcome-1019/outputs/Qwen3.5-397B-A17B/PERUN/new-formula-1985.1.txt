The claim is that replacing the flagship formula with a sweeter alternative, validated by blind taste tests, will reverse market share loss. The steelman: sensory superiority drives purchase, and removing the old formula forces migration to the superior product, maximizing efficiency.

However, the attack reveals a fatal flaw in the evidence. The blind taste tests isolate flavor from brand identity, measuring only immediate sensory pleasure (sips) rather than long-term satisfaction (cases). By withholding the fact that the original would be removed, the research failed to measure the psychological reactance and sense of loss consumers would feel. A product's value is not just its taste but its heritage and consistency; removing the original transforms the launch from an innovation into a confiscation of a cultural asset.

The "better way" is clear: introduce the sweeter formula as a new line extension while retaining the original. This captures the taste-preference segment without alienating the core base. The proposed "all-in" swap bets the entire company on a single metric that ignores emotional attachment, a risk explicitly flagged in the proposal itself. The lack of a fallback product creates a one-way door with catastrophic potential if the emotional backlash outweighs the taste preference.

The pre-mortem is straightforward: loyalists revolt, perceiving the change as corporate arrogance, leading to boycotts and a forced, humiliating reintroduction of the old formula at a higher cost. The cost of delay is negligible compared to the risk of brand destruction. The testing methodology is fundamentally broken for this specific strategic decision because it removed the primary variable driving loyalty: the brand's history.

Without a phased rollout or a retained legacy option, the risk of irreversible brand damage is unacceptably high. The data supports a new product, not a replacement.

DECISION: NO-GO
