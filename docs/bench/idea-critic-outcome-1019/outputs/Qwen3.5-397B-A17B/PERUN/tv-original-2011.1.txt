The claim is that a data-driven, all-at-once release of two seasons without a pilot will succeed where traditional broadcasting models require testing. The steelman: proprietary viewing data reduces pilot necessity, and a bold release strategy creates immediate cultural impact to drive subscriptions.

However, critical failures loom. First, **data validity**: viewing data reflects past consumption of licensed content, not demand for original prestige drama. Assuming correlation is a fatal leap; the audience for other companies' content may not follow the service's brand. Second, **financial concentration**: committing a large share of the budget to one unproven asset violates diversification principles. If the show fails, the service lacks funds for recovery. Third, **distribution risk**: releasing all episodes at once removes the weekly retention hook that sustains subscriber engagement over months, potentially causing churn immediately after binge-watching.

Better ways exist. A single-season order with a pilot or shortened first run limits downside. Releasing weekly builds sustained marketing momentum and allows mid-course correction. Licensing a completed series from another market proves the concept before commissioning new production.

The pre-mortem reveals total budget exhaustion on a flop with no backup content. This is a one-way door; once spent, the capital is gone. The cost of delay is low; waiting for a pilot or smaller test preserves cash while gathering specific original-content data.

Evidence is missing: no proof that viewing data predicts original hit potential, nor that binge-model retains subscribers long-term. The "large share of budget" constraint makes the risk of ruin unacceptable without a smaller initial test. The kill criterion is clear: if a smaller test (one season or pilot) does not show retention metrics exceeding licensed content by 20%, do not proceed. Without this step, the proposal gambles the company's solvency on an unverified assumption.

DECISION: NO-GO
