The claim is that investing heavily in general-purpose programmability for mass-market graphics chips (2004–2006), despite no existing large market and increased unit costs, will create a dominant new revenue stream.

Steelman: By embedding general-purpose compute capabilities into every consumer chip now, the company can seed a software ecosystem that locks in developers before rivals do, turning a niche academic curiosity into a massive scientific and data-processing market, eventually diversifying revenue beyond the volatile gaming sector.

However, the skepticism is severe. The proposal requires raising unit costs on every chip sold to subsidize a market with zero committed customers. In a hyper-competitive duopoly focused on gaming performance, diverting engineering resources and silicon area to unproven features risks losing the core gaming market—the only current source of revenue. If the gaming segment shrinks due to distraction, the company collapses before the scientific market matures. The "better way" is to wait for clear demand signals or develop specialized professional cards first, rather than bloating every consumer unit. The cost of delay is low compared to the risk of bankruptcy from misallocating capital during a critical architectural race.

The pre-mortem reveals failure: the company loses the gaming generation war due to divided focus, stock plummets, and the scientific toolchain is abandoned because the hardware install base was too weak to support developer interest. The kill criterion is clear: without at least one major non-gaming industry partner committing to volume purchases within 12 months, the program drains cash without return.

While the potential upside is transformative, the immediate threat to the core business is existential. Betting the entire company on a hypothetical future market while ignoring the present lethal competition violates basic survival strategy. The proposal lacks a phased approach or a safeguard for the core gaming business.

DECISION: NO-GO
