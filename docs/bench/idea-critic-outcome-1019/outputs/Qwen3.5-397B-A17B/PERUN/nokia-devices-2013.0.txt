The claim is that acquiring a failing handset manufacturer for €5.4 billion will secure the ecosystem and prevent defection to a rival. The steelman posits that without vertical integration, the platform loses its primary hardware advocate, accelerating a death spiral where developers abandon the OS due to low market share.

However, the "better way" analysis reveals a fatal flaw: the proposal attempts to solve an app-ecosystem deficit (a software/network problem) by purchasing a declining hardware asset. The market leader dominates because of developer momentum, not just device count. Owning a shrinking handset division does not automatically attract developers; it merely adds the burden of a losing hardware business to the software company's balance sheet. The "do nothing" or "partner-only" alternative avoids the massive integration risk of a large, demoralized workforce while allowing other manufacturers to remain neutral rather than becoming hostile competitors forced to back the rival exclusively.

The pre-mortem is clear: The acquisition fails because the hardware division continues to lose money, draining resources needed for software innovation, while other manufacturers, fearing competition from the owner, immediately abandon the platform for the rival. The cost of delay is low compared to the cost of a botched integration; waiting allows the market to clarify whether hardware ownership actually moves the developer needle.

The strongest attack survived is the fear of immediate defection. Yet, if the handset maker is already failing, their switch to the rival might happen regardless of ownership, or their decline is too steep to save the ecosystem. Buying a sinking ship to keep it flying your flag is a vanity metric if the passengers (developers) have already left. The proposal lacks a specific kill criterion for when the hardware drag outweighs the ecosystem protection. Without proof that owning this specific failing asset reverses developer churn, the €5.4 billion is a high-risk bet on a backward-looking strategy.

DECISION: NO-GO
