Steelman: Vertical integration secures the only remaining hardware partner for a struggling OS, preventing an immediate switch to the dominant rival and buying time to fix the app ecosystem.

However, the claim that ownership fixes the ecosystem problem is unsupported. The core failure mode is not hardware supply but developer abandonment; the market leader already possesses the apps. Buying a declining handset division with falling sales for €5.4 billion consumes capital needed for developer incentives while inheriting integration risks and a large, potentially misaligned workforce. Competitors may react by accelerating their own consolidation or excluding the OS entirely, viewing the move as hostile rather than protective.

The "do nothing" alternative preserves cash but risks the manufacturer defecting immediately. A cheaper, lower-risk alternative is a strategic partnership or subsidy agreement that locks the manufacturer to the OS without the burden of owning a losing hardware business. This avoids integration chaos and preserves flexibility.

Pre-mortem: Two years post-acquisition, the handset division continues to lose market share due to the lack of apps. The massive integration effort distracts the software team, slowing OS updates. The €5.4 billion is gone, leaving no funds for a developer bailout. The company is forced to exit the mobile market entirely, having failed to solve the root cause: the app gap. Hardware ownership cannot manufacture developer interest if the user base is shrinking.

The cost of delay is the potential loss of the manufacturer to the rival platform. However, the cost of action is likely total capital depletion without solving the ecosystem issue. The proposal conflates controlling the pipe with fixing the water; owning the faucet does not help if no one wants the water. The risks of integration and competitor retaliation outweigh the temporary assurance of hardware supply, especially since the supply itself is becoming less valuable as sales fall.

DECISION: NO-GO
