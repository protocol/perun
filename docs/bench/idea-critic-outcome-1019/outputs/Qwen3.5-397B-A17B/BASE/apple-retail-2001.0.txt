The proposal suggests a high-risk pivot for a struggling PC manufacturer with only 3–5% market share and recent losses. The plan involves opening company-run retail stores in prime shopping malls during a recessionary period in 2001. While management argues these locations are essential to demonstrate products and a new digital-music strategy, the financial fundamentals are deeply concerning.

The computer retail sector is characterized by notoriously low margins. Combining this with high lease costs in prime locations creates a fragile cost structure that leaves little room for error. For a company already posting losses, adding significant fixed overhead through long-term leases introduces severe liquidity risk. If sales do not immediately surge, the burden of rent and inventory could accelerate financial distress.

Furthermore, the proposal ignores substantial channel conflict. Current sales rely on third-party electronics retailers where staff already favor cheaper competitors. Establishing direct competition via owned stores will likely alienate these existing partners, potentially causing an immediate drop in the volume sold through established channels before the new stores can compensate. Given the industry's poor track record with dedicated retail chains and the specific timing of a recession, the probability of failure is high.

The argument for better product demonstration does not outweigh the existential threat posed by fixed costs and partner alienation. A company with limited market share and no recent profitability cannot afford to gamble its remaining capital on an unproven, capital-intensive distribution model during an economic downturn. The risks of inventory obsolescence, lease defaults, and retaliatory actions from current resellers create a scenario where the downside far exceeds any potential strategic benefit. Approval would jeopardize the firm's survival without a guaranteed path to profitability.

DECISION: NO-GO
