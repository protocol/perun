The proposal involves a struggling PC manufacturer with 3–5% market share and recent losses attempting to launch a proprietary retail chain in 2001. The core argument is that third-party retailers fail to adequately demonstrate the product or its new digital-music strategy due to staff pushing cheaper competitors. However, the financial and operational risks are severe. The computer retail sector is characterized by notoriously low margins, which this company cannot afford given its current loss trajectory. Adding high lease costs in prime shopping malls creates a heavy fixed-cost burden precisely when the economy faces recessionary pressures.

Furthermore, the industry has a documented poor track record for manufacturer-owned retail stores, suggesting a structural flaw in this approach rather than an execution issue specific to one firm. Opening direct stores will inevitably create channel conflict with existing third-party resellers. Since these partners currently drive the majority of sales, alienating them could cause an immediate collapse in volume that the new stores cannot replace quickly enough to offset losses. The inventory risk associated with stocking physical locations adds another layer of potential financial drain.

While the need for better product demonstration is valid, the proposed solution threatens the company's remaining liquidity. The combination of high fixed costs, low industry margins, existing financial losses, and the danger of destroying current distribution channels makes this initiative excessively dangerous at this time. The company lacks the financial cushion to absorb the inevitable initial losses of a new retail venture, especially during a recession. A less capital-intensive approach to demonstration, such as dedicated kiosks within existing partner stores or revised incentive programs for retail staff, would address the core problem without exposing the firm to existential risk. Proceeding as proposed invites catastrophic failure.

DECISION: NO-GO
