The proposal to launch a free streaming service in 2007, despite significant limitations in catalog size, video quality, and broadband penetration, represents a critical defensive maneuver rather than a mere feature addition. While the risks of high licensing costs, potential cannibalization of the profitable DVD segment, and competition from larger technology entrants are valid, ignoring the shift toward digital distribution poses a far greater existential threat.

The current profitability and seven-million-subscriber base provide a unique financial cushion and an installed user base that competitors lack. Offering streaming at no extra charge leverages this existing loyalty, creating an immediate barrier to entry for rivals who must acquire customers from scratch. Although the streaming catalog is currently small and skewed toward older titles, establishing the infrastructure and user habit now is essential. Waiting for perfect conditions, universal broadband, or better licensing deals allows competitors to define the market standards and capture consumer mindshare first.

Cannibalization of the DVD business is inevitable as technology evolves; the choice is not whether it will happen, but whether this company will lead the transition or be displaced by others. By controlling the migration path, the company can manage the decline of physical media while building the data and technical capabilities required for a digital future. The poor video quality and limited selection are temporary constraints that will improve as bandwidth expands and licensing models mature. However, the strategic window to establish a foothold before massive tech and retail giants dominate the space is narrow. Rejecting this proposal to protect short-term DVD margins risks obsolescence in the medium term. The company must use its current strength to fund the future, even if the initial offering is imperfect.
