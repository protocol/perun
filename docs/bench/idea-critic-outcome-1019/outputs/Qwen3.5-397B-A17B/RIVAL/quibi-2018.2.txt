This proposal fails the fundamental test of product-market fit in its specific historical context. The core premise—charging a subscription fee for short-form, premium video in 2018—contradicts established consumer behavior and market dynamics.

First, the value proposition is misaligned. In 2018, "short-form" was synonymous with free, ad-supported, user-generated content (YouTube, emerging TikTok). Consumers did not associate "short" with "premium." Attempting to monetize this format via subscription ignores the zero-price anchor already set by competitors. The "subscription fatigue" noted in the proposal is a critical warning sign, not a hurdle to jump over with more content.

Second, the cost structure is unsustainable. High production budgets and A-list talent are designed for long-form retention and cinematic spectacle, which drives theatrical or SVOD (Subscription Video on Demand) value. Applying these costs to under-10-minute episodes creates a unit economics disaster. The return on investment for a $20 million pilot that lasts six minutes is mathematically inferior to a feature film or a 10-hour series, yet the revenue potential per user is capped by the lower perceived value of the format.

Third, the technological differentiator (vertical/horizontal optimization) is a feature, not a business. While usability is important, it does not justify a new subscription tier when incumbents can replicate this functionality instantly. The "mobile-only" constraint further limits the total addressable market by excluding living-room viewing, which was becoming the dominant mode for premium video consumption even on mobile devices via casting.

The risk of "no proven demand" is not a risk; it is a certainty of non-existence. There is no evidence that users wanted *premium* short-form content enough to pay for it, especially when free alternatives occupied the same attention span. This proposal attempts to force a square peg (Hollywood economics) into a round hole (mobile micro-content) without solving the fundamental equation of why a user would open a new app and pay a monthly fee for it.

The capital requirement of $1 billion suggests a "build it and they will come" strategy that ignores the brutal reality of customer acquisition costs in a saturated market. Without a clear path to profitability or a unique content library that cannot be found elsewhere, this venture is destined to burn cash with no retention.

DECISION: NO-GO
