This proposal represents a pivotal strategic pivot from a "walled garden" web-only approach to a controlled native ecosystem. In 2008, the smartphone market is transitioning from feature phones to computing platforms; without native apps, the device risks becoming a premium browser rather than a versatile tool.

The 30% revenue share creates an immediate, scalable business model that aligns developer incentives with platform success while funding the review infrastructure. A single company-run store directly addresses the primary executive concerns: it mitigates security and stability risks through mandatory vetting, ensures product polish by enforcing design guidelines, and provides a negotiation lever with carriers by controlling which network-bypassing apps reach users.

While rival platforms offer open distribution, their fragmentation often results in inconsistent user experiences and security vulnerabilities. This proposal trades maximum developer freedom for maximum user trust and brand coherence. The review workload is a valid operational challenge, but it is a necessary cost of maintaining a premium reputation. Delaying native app support to avoid this workload would cede the emerging platform war to competitors who solve the quality control problem later, if ever.

The CEO's previous stance on web apps was logical for a first-generation launch focused on speed to market and stability. However, clinging to that constraint now would limit the device's utility as user expectations shift toward specialized, high-performance tools that web technologies of this era cannot deliver reliably. The proposal does not abandon the web; it supplements it with a higher-performance tier under strict governance.

Rejecting this plan preserves short-term operational ease but guarantees long-term irrelevance in a software-defined mobile future. Approving it establishes the device as a legitimate computing platform, creates a new high-margin revenue stream, and leverages the company's design reputation as a competitive moat against fragmented rivals. The risks are manageable; the risk of inaction is existential.

DECISION: GO
