This proposal represents a classic "adjacent expansion" into infrastructure-as-a-service, leveraging existing sunk costs in hardware and data center operations. The strategic logic is sound: transform fixed capital expenditure into variable revenue by monetizing idle or over-provisioned capacity during non-peak retail cycles. The "spiky" nature of retail traffic (e.g., holiday seasons) creates natural periods of underutilization that can be rented out, effectively lowering the blended cost of infrastructure for the core retail business.

However, the risks are existential. Running a developer platform requires a fundamentally different operational muscle than retail e-commerce. Retail tolerates brief outages; developers building mission-critical applications demand "five nines" reliability and immediate support. The company lacks institutional knowledge in API design, developer relations, and enterprise sales cycles. Entering a market with unproven demand while facing entrenched incumbents (hardware/software vendors) who may pivot to compete is dangerous. Furthermore, investor skepticism regarding heavy capex will likely intensify if margins dilute due to the high support costs of a nascent platform business.

The "distraction" risk is the most critical. If engineering leadership splits focus between keeping the retail site online during peak seasons and building a reliable cloud platform, both could fail. The margin profile of low-end compute rental is notoriously thin without massive scale, potentially dragging down the company's overall profitability just as investors are questioning spending.

Despite these severe risks, the potential upside justifies a controlled attempt. If the market for on-demand compute proves real (a big "if" in 2006), being an early mover with existing infrastructure provides a unique cost advantage that pure-play startups cannot match. The key is isolation: the new business must operate as a distinct unit with its own P&L, engineering leadership, and reliability standards, preventing cultural and operational bleed into the core retail engine. The proposal should proceed, but only with strict firewalls between the two businesses and a clear mandate to validate market demand before further massive capital allocation. The cost of *not* exploring this, should the cloud model succeed, is far higher than the cost of a contained failure.

DECISION: GO
