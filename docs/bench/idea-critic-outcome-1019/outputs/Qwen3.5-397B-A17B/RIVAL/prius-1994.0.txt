This proposal represents a high-stakes gambit that conflates technological leadership with commercial viability. While the ambition to define a new category is admirable, the underlying premises are fragile. The market signal is absent: fuel is cheap, removing the primary economic incentive for adoption, and regulators have not created compliance pressure. Without external drivers, consumer demand for a premium-priced, unproven technology is speculative at best.

The technical risks are substantial and potentially catastrophic for a volume manufacturer. The novel power-split transmission and nickel-metal-hydride battery pack lack field validation. Committing to a rigid 1997 mass-production schedule ignores the high probability of teething issues that could result in massive warranty exposure and brand damage. In the automotive sector, reliability is the cornerstone of reputation; a flawed launch could poison the well for future electrification efforts.

Furthermore, the cost structure is misaligned with the value proposition. Developing a complex, first-of-its-kind powertrain for a market that does not yet exist forces the company to subsidize early adopters heavily. Competitors are wisely waiting on the sidelines to learn from any pioneer's mistakes. By locking into a bold public schedule, this automaker removes its own flexibility to iterate or delay if technical hurdles arise, turning a strategic bet into a reputational trap.

True innovation requires timing as much as invention. The "first mover" advantage here is illusory without a supporting ecosystem or regulatory mandate. The prudent path is to continue development as a limited feasibility program or technology demonstrator, gathering real-world data on battery durability and customer reception without the existential risk of a full mass-production commitment. Launching a volume product now is premature.

DECISION: NO-GO
