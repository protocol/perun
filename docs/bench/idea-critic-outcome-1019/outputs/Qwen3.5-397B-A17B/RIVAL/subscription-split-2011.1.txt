This proposal attempts to solve a pricing backlash with structural separation, but it misdiagnoses the core problem. Customers are reacting to a 60% price hike, not brand confusion. Splitting the services into two distinct brands with separate logins, bills, and queues does not lower the price; it merely formalizes the unbundling that the price increase already enacted.

The execution risks are severe. Forcing existing users to migrate to a new brand for DVD rentals creates immediate friction. Losing shared ratings and recommendations degrades the product value for both services, making each less useful than the combined whole. The operational complexity of maintaining two separate technology stacks, customer support lines, and marketing campaigns will drain resources from the very innovation the streaming side claims to need.

Most critically, this move signals to the market that the company is retreating from its integrated vision. It validates competitor narratives that the DVD business is a dying anchor. While the DVD segment is indeed declining, milking it through a confusing spin-off during a pricing crisis will likely accelerate churn rather than stabilize revenue.

The rationale that streaming needs freedom to pursue its own strategy is valid, but it does not require a full brand split, separate billing, and broken data synergy right now. A better approach would be to keep the unified brand and account system while clearly itemizing the costs, or to absorb the short-term revenue hit to smooth the transition.

This proposal adds massive customer friction and operational overhead without solving the price sensitivity issue. It trades long-term data advantages for short-term managerial convenience during a crisis where customer trust is already fragile. The loss of cross-service intelligence alone is a strategic error that will compound over time.

DECISION: NO-GO
