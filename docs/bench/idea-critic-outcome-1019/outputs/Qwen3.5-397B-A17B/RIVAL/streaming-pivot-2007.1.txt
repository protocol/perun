This proposal represents a classic innovator's dilemma, but the strategic imperative to act is absolute. In 2007, the physical media market is at its zenith, yet the trajectory of broadband adoption and digital distribution is undeniable. Waiting for perfect conditions—universal high-speed internet, HD quality, or a vast library—is a strategy for obsolescence.

The risks cited are real but manageable compared to the existential threat of inaction. Licensing costs are high now but will stabilize as the market matures; entering early secures crucial data on viewing habits and streaming infrastructure experience that competitors lack. Cannibalization of the DVD business is inevitable, whether by this company or by external entrants like the anticipated big technology firms. It is far better to cannibalize your own profitable revenue stream than to have it stolen by a rival who offers the future while you cling to the past.

The small, older catalogue is actually a strategic advantage for a initial launch. It minimizes upfront licensing risk while allowing the company to test the technology, gauge user adoption, and refine the streaming interface without the pressure of blockbuster expectations. Offering it at no extra charge to existing subscribers leverages the seven-million-strong base to drive immediate adoption and collect valuable behavioral data. This turns the user base into a beta testing group that competitors cannot match.

The "poor" video quality and lack of universal broadband are temporary constraints that will resolve naturally with time and infrastructure investment. The strategic risk is not in launching with these limitations, but in waiting until the limitations disappear, by which point the market will likely be dominated by the very large entrants the proposal fears.

This move is not about immediate profit from streaming; it is about securing the company's relevance in the next decade. It signals to the market, investors, and content partners that the company is evolving. It builds the muscle memory for a digital future while the cash cow of DVD rentals still funds the transition. To reject this proposal is to bet against the direction of technology itself.
