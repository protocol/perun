This proposal asks a graphics-chip maker to pivot its core mass-market hardware toward general-purpose scientific computing before a commercial market exists. The strategy involves increasing unit costs on every consumer chip to fund general-purpose features, a full compiler toolchain, and university education programs.

**Strategic Assessment:**
The primary flaw is economic timing. In 2004–2006, the company's revenue relies almost entirely on gaming and workstation graphics, a sector engaged in a fierce performance war with a main rival. Adding non-revenue-generating silicon area to every unit raises costs without an immediate return, directly threatening margin and competitive pricing in the core business.

While the vision of programmable graphics for science is technically sound, the "build it and they will come" approach ignores the adoption curve. Academic users are currently hacking existing APIs, proving interest but not willingness to pay. Funding university teaching is a long-term play that yields no revenue within the proposal's timeframe. The engineering distraction risk is critical; diverting focus from the core graphics race could allow the rival to capture the gaming market, destroying the cash cow needed to fund any future compute initiative.

A "GO" decision here requires betting the company's current profitability on a niche, unproven market. Without committed customers or a clear path to monetization beyond academic goodwill, the financial exposure is too high. The proposal lacks a phased approach, such as developing the toolchain on existing architecture first or securing anchor tenants in high-performance computing before altering silicon design.

The risk of engineering distraction combined with increased unit costs in a hyper-competitive core market makes this proposal untenable in its current form. The company should validate demand with software-only solutions before committing to expensive hardware changes across its entire product line.

DECISION: NO-GO
