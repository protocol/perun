The proposal to open the device to third-party native apps via a curated store is a high-stakes pivot from the current "web-only" doctrine, but it is strategically necessary. The primary risk—untrusted code destabilizing the device or violating carrier agreements—is mitigated by the proposed single-company review gate. This walled garden approach balances openness with control, directly addressing security and stability concerns while preventing carrier backlash from unauthorized network bypasses.

The 30% revenue share is standard for the era and creates a sustainable economic engine for the platform without requiring upfront investment from the hardware maker. While review workload is a valid operational concern, it is a solvable scaling problem; the greater risk is irrelevance. Rival platforms already possess app ecosystems. Maintaining a web-only stance in 2008 cedes the definition of "smartphone" to competitors who understand that native code unlocks hardware capabilities (GPS, accelerometer, multi-touch) that browsers cannot yet access reliably.

The fear that third-party apps will dilute product polish is valid but manageable through strict interface guidelines and the approval process. The alternative—keeping the device closed—guarantees a ceiling on utility that competitors will quickly surpass. The "web apps are sufficient" argument was a useful constraint for the first generation to ensure launch stability, but it becomes a liability for the second. This proposal transforms the phone from a static appliance into a dynamic platform. The 30% cut funds the review infrastructure required to maintain quality. The carrier objections are neutralized by the review gate ensuring no unauthorized network usage.

The timing is critical. Waiting another year to see how native apps evolve on rival devices would allow them to lock in developer mindshare and user expectations permanently. The operational burden of review is the price of admission for platform status. The risk of shipping a few bad apps is lower than the risk of shipping no apps at all while competitors flourish.

DECISION: GO
