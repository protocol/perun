This $36 billion merger presents a classic "buy vs. build" dilemma wrapped in massive technical debt. The strategic logic is sound: acquiring a dominant position in the high-value construction and business push-to-talk (PTT) segment is faster than attempting to displace an entrenched competitor with a homegrown solution. The target's customer base offers immediate revenue stability and high switching costs, which are valuable assets.

However, the operational risks are severe. Integrating two incompatible network technologies in 2005 is not a simple software patch; it requires maintaining parallel infrastructure, doubling operational complexity, and delaying the realization of synergies. The "large goodwill" indicates the purchase price significantly exceeds the tangible asset value, betting heavily on future integration success. If the technical migration fails or takes too long, the carrier faces stranded assets, inflated maintenance costs for legacy equipment, and potential churn if service quality dips during the transition.

The core question is whether the revenue from the business PTT segment justifies the multi-year burden of running dual networks and the massive integration bill. Given the era's technological constraints, network convergence is likely to be slower and more expensive than projected. While the market share gain is attractive, the execution risk threatens to erode the deal's value before it can be realized. The incompatibility isn't just a technical hurdle; it's a continuous drain on capital and engineering focus that could distract from broader data network improvements needed for the emerging smartphone era.

Approving this deal as proposed ignores the probability of integration delays and cost overruns inherent in merging incompatible legacy systems at this scale. The financial exposure from the goodwill impairment alone, should the integration stumble, is substantial. A more prudent path involves restructuring the deal to account for integration risks or demanding a concrete, funded migration plan before approval. As it stands, the proposal underestimates the friction of technological incompatibility.

DECISION: NO-GO
