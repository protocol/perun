This proposal represents a classic disruptor's dilemma: sacrificing guaranteed, high-margin licensing revenue today for the possibility of owning the customer relationship tomorrow. The strategic logic holds water only if the long-term value of a direct subscriber base exceeds the net present value of the licensing fees being abandoned. In the streaming era, owning the interface and the data is the ultimate moat; licensing to rivals essentially funds your own eventual obsolescence.

However, the execution risks are severe. The company has no history of running a consumer technology platform at scale, suggesting a high probability of technical debt, poor user experience, and operational blunders during the critical launch window. Furthermore, the assumption that a lower price point alone will drive tens of millions of subscribers ignores the massive marketing spend required to acquire them and the churn inherent in a crowded market. The "multi-year operating loss" is not just a funding line; it is a vote of confidence from shareholders that may waver if subscriber growth lags even slightly.

The cannibalization of cable and theatrical revenue is not a bug, but a feature—it is the necessary transition from legacy distribution to direct-to-consumer. Waiting to protect legacy revenue streams usually results in dying with them. The real question is whether the company can endure the cash burn long enough to reach scale before the market saturates or capital dries up.

Given the trajectory of the industry in 2019, where the shift to streaming is inevitable, the cost of inaction is higher than the cost of this risky pivot. While the execution risks are substantial, they are manageable with the right leadership and capital allocation. The alternative—remaining a content supplier to your future competitors—is a slow death sentence. The potential upside of owning a global subscriber base justifies the short-term pain and the substantial investment required.

DECISION: GO
