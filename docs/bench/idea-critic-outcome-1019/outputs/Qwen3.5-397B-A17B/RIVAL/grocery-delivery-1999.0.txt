This proposal represents a catastrophic misalignment of strategy, timing, and capital allocation. The core failure is the decision to scale infrastructure before validating unit economics. In any retail business, especially one with the thin margins of groceries, the path to profitability relies on optimizing density, reducing waste, and refining logistics. This plan explicitly bypasses the learning phase required to achieve those optimizations.

Spending $1 billion on highly automated warehouses across a dozen markets assumes the current operational model is correct and scalable. It is not. The company has been operating for only one month in a single city. There is zero data on customer retention, average order value, delivery costs per drop, or spoilage rates. Automation locks in processes that may prove inefficient or obsolete within a year. If the unit economics in the first city reveal that 30-minute slots are unsustainable due to routing inefficiencies or low basket size, the company will be left with a dozen expensive, specialized facilities designed for a broken model.

The capital intensity here creates an existential risk. Unlike a software business, where scaling often involves marginal server costs, this model requires massive upfront fixed costs. If customer density targets are not met immediately—which is statistically unlikely without years of market penetration—the cost per delivery will dwarf the margin on the goods. The "land grab" strategy only works if you can survive long enough to dominate. Burning $1 billion before proving the model works in one city ensures insolvency before dominance is possible.

The risks identified in the proposal (capital intensity, thin margins, density needs) are not just warnings; they are disqualifiers for this specific approach. A prudent strategy would involve leasing smaller, flexible fulfillment centers, manually validating delivery routes, and proving positive unit economics in the initial market before expanding to a second. To proceed with this plan is to gamble the entire venture on unproven assumptions. The board has a fiduciary duty to reject this level of exposure without evidence of viability.

DECISION: NO-GO
