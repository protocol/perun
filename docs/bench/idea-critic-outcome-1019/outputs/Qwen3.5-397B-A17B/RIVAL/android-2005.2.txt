This proposal represents a high-conviction, asymmetric bet on the future of mobile computing. The core thesis is sound: search advertising revenue is inextricably linked to user attention, and attention is migrating from desktop to mobile. If the mobile internet remains walled off by proprietary, fragmented operating systems, the search engine's growth ceiling lowers dramatically.

Acquiring this six-person team for $50 million is not a purchase of current revenue, but an insurance policy against obsolescence and a lever to unlock the next platform. The open-source model is the only viable wedge against entrenched incumbents; it neutralizes handset makers' fear of vendor lock-in and lowers the barrier to entry. While carrier resistance is a legitimate hurdle, history shows that compelling consumer devices eventually force carrier compliance, not the other way around.

The risks are substantial but manageable. The lack of a direct revenue model for the OS is irrelevant; the OS is a loss leader for the core search business. Handset adoption will be slow initially, requiring patience and likely further subsidies or partnerships, but the alternative—having no seat at the table when the mobile web explodes—is far costlier. The multi-year payback aligns with the typical infrastructure shift timeline; expecting quick returns on a platform play demonstrates a misunderstanding of the market dynamics.

The primary danger is execution risk: can a search culture successfully manage an OS development team? Can they ship a product that rivals established players? However, doing nothing guarantees dependency on others' roadmaps. $50 million is a rounding error for a company of this scale compared to the potential loss of the mobile search market. The timing (2005) is prescient, just before the smartphone explosion. Waiting for "proof" of the market means entering too late to dominate.

This is a classic "barbell" strategy: limited downside (the acquisition cost) against unlimited upside (owning the gateway to mobile search). The strategic fit is perfect, even if the tactical path is fraught. The board must approve this to secure the company's next decade of growth.
