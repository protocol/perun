The acquisition price of $7.4 B for a studio that produces only one film annually implies a valuation far above the cash‑flow and earnings multiples typical for the animation sector. Even with strong box‑office and critical track records, the studio’s limited output creates high earnings volatility, making the deal financially risky for shareholders.

Placing the studio’s creative leaders in charge of the conglomerate’s broader animation pipeline could generate fresh ideas, but it also raises a cultural integration problem. The conglomerate’s hand‑drawn unit has recently under‑performed, suggesting systemic issues that may not be solved merely by importing external leadership. Moreover, the reliance on a handful of creative executives concentrates risk; any departure or disagreement could destabilise the combined operation.

The founder‑shareholder becoming a major owner of the buyer introduces governance complexities. Their influence could clash with existing board dynamics, potentially slowing decision‑making or steering the business toward projects that favour the founder’s legacy rather than the conglomerate’s strategic priorities.

Alternative paths—such as renewing the partnership on more favorable terms, forming a joint venture, or investing in new talent pipelines—could secure the creative benefits without the outsized financial exposure and integration hazards.

Given the disproportionate price, limited production cadence, cultural and governance risks, and the availability of lower‑risk partnership models, the proposal should be revisited before proceeding.

DECISION: NO-GO
