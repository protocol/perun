The proposal aims to turn a pure‑gaming GPU vendor into a general‑purpose compute platform by adding programmable hardware, a compiler stack, and university outreach to every consumer chip. While visionary, the timing and market realities make it premature for a full‑scale rollout.

**Market risk** – In 2004‑2006 no sizable paying market for GPU computing exists. Academic labs are still hacking existing graphics APIs; they cannot provide the volume or revenue needed to offset the per‑chip cost increase. Without committed customers, the added silicon and software engineering budget become an unrecouped expense.

**Cost impact** – Adding general‑purpose units to each mass‑market die raises the bill‑of‑materials for every consumer product. That cost will be passed to gamers and workstation users, who have no incentive to pay for compute features they never use, potentially eroding price competitiveness against the main rival.

**Engineering distraction** – The core business is a race for performance, power efficiency, and feature leadership in gaming graphics. Diverting engineering talent to build a compiler, runtime, and teaching program risks slowing the flagship GPU roadmap, weakening the company’s position in its primary market.

**Strategic alternatives** – A more disciplined approach would be to:
1. Run a limited pilot program targeting a subset of high‑end cards where the cost penalty is tolerable and early adopters (research labs, scientific supercomputing centers) can be engaged.
2. Secure anchor customers (e.g., large research institutions or early‑stage HPC vendors) before committing to mass‑market integration.
3. Develop the software stack in parallel but keep it optional, allowing the hardware to remain unchanged for the bulk of consumer shipments.

By validating demand and recouping costs on a smaller scale first, the company can avoid a broad‑based price increase while still positioning itself for future compute markets.

Given the high financial and competitive risk, the proposal should be revised to a staged, market‑validated approach before committing to a multi‑year, full‑scale program.

DECISION: NO-GO
