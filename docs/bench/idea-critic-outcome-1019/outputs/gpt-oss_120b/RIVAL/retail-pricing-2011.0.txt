The department‑store chain’s core revenue has long depended on deep discounts and coupon‑driven buying habits. Customers have been conditioned to wait for promotions, and the bulk of sales come from items sold at a discount. Shifting instantly to everyday low pricing removes the primary value proposition that drives traffic and volume.

Key concerns:

1. **Customer behavior mismatch** – Long‑time shoppers will likely perceive “everyday pricing” as higher prices, reducing footfall and basket size. Without a transition period or clear communication, brand trust could erode quickly.

2. **Revenue risk** – Discount‑driven sales currently subsidize other categories. Eliminating coupons in one step could cause an abrupt drop in overall revenue, jeopardizing cash flow and inventory turnover.

3. **Operational shock** – Re‑branding all stores simultaneously demands massive supply‑chain, merchandising, and staff retraining efforts. Any misalignment could cause stockouts, pricing errors, and poor in‑store experience.

4. **No pilot** – Testing the model in a limited set of stores would provide data on customer response, pricing elasticity, and operational challenges. Skipping this step removes a critical feedback loop and forces the organization to learn only after full rollout.

5. **Competitive pressure** – Competitors that continue to offer coupons may capture price‑sensitive shoppers during the transition, further amplifying sales loss.

Given these risks, the proposal should be refined to include a controlled pilot (e.g., 5‑10 stores) with clear metrics, a phased communication plan, and contingency pricing adjustments. Only after validating the model’s impact on traffic, conversion, and profitability should the chain commit to a full‑scale rollout.

**Recommendation:** Hold the full rollout until a pilot validates the everyday‑pricing concept and mitigates the identified risks.

DECISION: NO-GO
