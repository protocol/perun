The proposal aims to create a new social network in 2011 by embedding a profile and sharing layer across the company’s existing search, mail, video, and photo services, then opening it via an invite‑only beta. Its only stated differentiators—friend‑grouping circles and video hangouts—do not constitute a compelling value proposition against a market leader that already commands hundreds of millions of users.

Three prior social‑networking attempts have failed, indicating a pattern of mis‑reading user demand or execution challenges. Network effects are a fundamental barrier: the incumbent’s large, entrenched user base makes it extremely difficult for a newcomer to achieve critical mass, especially when users must adopt a new platform that offers only marginally distinct features.

Integrating the social layer into core products carries significant risk of alienating existing users who rely on those services for search, mail, and media. Forced integration can degrade the core experience, increase support load, and erode trust in the brand’s flagship offerings. Moreover, tying executive bonuses to adoption creates a perverse incentive structure that may pressure teams to prioritize short‑term user counts over sustainable product quality and long‑term retention.

Given the high‑stakes nature of social‑network launches, the limited differentiation, the historical record of failure, and the potential for negative spill‑over into core services, the proposal does not meet the threshold for a prudent, high‑impact investment at this time. A more viable path would be to conduct focused experiments on the differentiating features (circles, video hangouts) within existing products, gather concrete user adoption data, and reassess the full‑scale launch only after demonstrable demand and a clear competitive moat emerge.

DECISION: NO-GO
