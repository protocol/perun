The merger would create the nation’s largest carrier on paper, but the core technical realities make the deal hazardous. The two networks run fundamentally different, non‑interoperable standards; one is a legacy push‑to‑talk system optimized for voice, the other is a more modern data‑capable architecture. Integrating them would require massive capital spending to either:

1. **Maintain dual networks** – keeping the legacy system alive indefinitely, which adds ongoing operational cost, complexity, and limits the combined entity’s ability to compete in a data‑driven market.
2. **Replace one network** – a full rebuild of the target’s infrastructure, a multi‑year, multi‑billion‑dollar effort that erodes the value of the acquisition and delays any revenue synergies.

The target’s business‑customer base is valuable, yet those customers increasingly demand data services that the legacy network cannot support. Without a clear, funded roadmap to upgrade the target’s technology, the buyer risks losing those customers after the merger.

Goodwill on the balance sheet also raises concerns. The buyer would inherit significant intangible assets that may be impaired if integration stalls, further hurting earnings and shareholder confidence.

Given the magnitude of integration cost, the technology mismatch, and the looming data‑usage trends of the mid‑2000s, the proposal as presented carries too much uncertainty to justify approval. A more prudent approach would be to:

* Negotiate a lower price reflecting integration risk, or
* Pursue a strategic partnership that grants access to the business customer base without full network integration, or
* Require a detailed, funded plan for network harmonization before proceeding.

Until such mitigations are in place, the merger should not move forward.

DECISION: NO-GO
