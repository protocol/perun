The concept targets a premium market segment, yet the $699 price point for a countertop juicer is substantially higher than comparable appliances (e.g., high‑end blenders and traditional juicers typically range $200‑$400). This creates a steep barrier to entry for most consumers, especially when the device also requires a continuous subscription for proprietary pre‑packed bags.

The subscription model adds ongoing cost that can quickly exceed the price of buying fresh produce and juicing it at home. A typical bag of chopped fruit/vegetable (≈1 kg) might cost $5‑$7; at two bags per week the monthly expense reaches $40‑$60, far above the cost of purchasing raw ingredients and using a conventional juicer. The value proposition hinges on convenience, but convenience alone rarely justifies such a premium total cost of ownership.

Mandating Wi‑Fi connectivity introduces reliability and privacy concerns. Users in regions with unstable internet or those who prefer offline appliances would be excluded. Moreover, tying freshness checks to an app adds software maintenance overhead and potential points of failure that could render the device unusable if the service is discontinued.

Market size for a $699 connected juicer is limited. Even among affluent early adopters, demand for niche kitchen gadgets is modest, and the subscription lock‑in further narrows the addressable audience. Competing alternatives—affordable juicers, personal blenders, and ready‑to‑drink cold‑pressed juices—already satisfy the convenience need without the high upfront and recurring costs.

Given the high price, ongoing subscription expense, connectivity requirement, and uncertain market demand, the proposal carries significant commercial risk. It would be prudent to revisit pricing, explore a lower‑cost hardware version, or consider a more flexible, non‑subscription consumable model before moving forward.

DECISION: NO-GO
