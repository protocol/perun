The proposal tackles a clear market gap: competitors already host thriving native‑app stores, and without one the phone will quickly appear stale. Opening the platform creates a new revenue stream (30 % cut) and a developer community that can extend the device’s value far beyond the original web‑app promise.  

Security and stability can be controlled through a mandatory review process and sandboxing, as the company already plans to approve every submission. A well‑defined policy—rejecting code that accesses privileged APIs, enforcing resource limits, and requiring signed packages—will keep untrusted code from compromising the OS.  

Carrier concerns are addressable by disallowing apps that directly replace carrier‑provided services or that bypass carrier billing, and by exposing a “carrier‑approved” tag in the store. This preserves carrier revenue while still allowing innovative utilities.  

The review workload will rise, but it is a finite, scalable operation: a small team can triage submissions using automated static analysis followed by manual checks for the most complex cases. The 30 % revenue share is comparable to rival platforms and provides sufficient budget to staff this effort.  

Finally, the risk to product polish is mitigated by the same review gate: only apps meeting strict UI/UX guidelines will be listed, and the store can feature “certified” apps that meet higher standards.  

By moving early, the company secures a strategic advantage, captures a new market, and can evolve its ecosystem under tight quality controls. The concerns are real but solvable with clear policies and modest investment.
