The target OS controls only a low‑single‑digit share of smartphones, meaning its installed‑base is already too small to attract a healthy developer ecosystem. Buying the handset division will give the OS maker a reference device, but it does not address the root cause of the ecosystem weakness: the lack of apps and developers that gravitate toward the market‑leader’s platform.

A €5.4 billion outlay is a massive capital commitment for a company whose core business is software, not hardware. Integration of a large manufacturing workforce carries well‑known execution risk—cultural mismatch, supply‑chain complexity, and the need to retain talent. Competitors are likely to respond with price cuts, accelerated feature releases, or exclusive deals with carriers, further squeezing margins.

Even if integration succeeds, hardware ownership alone cannot compel developers to write for a platform that still offers a tiny market. The strategic benefit—preventing the OEM from defecting—may be achieved more cheaply through licensing, partnership, or revenue‑share agreements that preserve cash and reduce operational exposure.

Given the disproportionate financial risk, integration uncertainty, and the limited upside of solving an app‑ecosystem problem through hardware control, the proposal is not advisable in its current form.

DECISION: NO-GO
