The deal would lock in a proven creative engine at a time when the conglomerate’s own hand‑drawn unit is faltering, and it would deny a rival a foothold in the lucrative computer‑animation market. Those strategic points are compelling, but the economics are problematic.

* **Valuation vs. output** – At $7.4 bn the purchase price represents roughly 20‑30 × the studio’s annual earnings (the studio releases only one feature each year). Even with strong brand and ancillary revenue, the multiple is well above typical industry benchmarks and leaves little margin for error if the next film under‑performs.

* **Integration risk** – An all‑stock transaction will dilute existing shareholders and give the studio’s founder a sizeable block in the combined entity, potentially granting him outsized influence over governance and creative direction. Past examples of culture clashes in similar mergers have shown that even modest integration friction can erode productivity and morale.

* **Talent concentration** – The proposal hinges on a handful of creative leaders. Their departure would instantly diminish the value of the acquisition, and the all‑stock structure offers limited mechanisms to retain them beyond equity incentives that may be insufficient if the combined company’s share price falls.

* **Alternative pathways** – A co‑production extension, a joint‑venture, or a staggered acquisition with earn‑outs tied to box‑office performance would preserve strategic access while limiting exposure. Negotiating a lower price or a cash‑plus‑stock mix could also reduce dilution and align incentives.

Given the high purchase multiple, the concentration of risk in a few individuals, and the governance complications introduced by the founder’s new stake, the proposal is too risky to endorse in its current form.

DECISION: NO-GO
