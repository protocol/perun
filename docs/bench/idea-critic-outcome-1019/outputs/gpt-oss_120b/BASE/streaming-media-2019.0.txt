The conglomerate’s core cash engine is its licensing business, which currently delivers billions in annual profit. Pulling that content away from rivals instantly eliminates a predictable, high‑margin revenue stream. The proposal assumes that a subscription service will replace those dollars, yet the target of “tens of millions” of subscribers over five years is modest compared with the market leader’s >100 million base. Even at a low price point, the revenue needed to offset the lost licensing income would require rapid, large‑scale adoption that the company has not demonstrated it can achieve.

Funding the rollout with a multi‑year operating loss is standard for new entrants, but the burn rate will be amplified by the need to build a proprietary technology platform from scratch. The firm has no prior experience delivering a consumer‑facing streaming stack, raising execution risk in areas such as content delivery network, recommendation algorithms, billing, and device integration. Delays or performance problems would accelerate subscriber churn and damage the brand.

Cannibalisation of existing cable and theatrical windows further erodes cash flow. While a direct‑to‑consumer model can eventually reduce reliance on third‑party distributors, the transition period will likely see overlapping revenue loss without an immediate offsetting subscriber base.

Given the magnitude of the licensing revenue at stake, the unproven technology capability, and the uncertainty around achieving sufficient subscriber scale, the proposal carries a high probability of net negative financial impact in the near to medium term. A more prudent path would be to pilot a limited‑scope offering, retain key licensing contracts, and invest in platform expertise before a full‑scale launch.

DECISION: NO-GO
