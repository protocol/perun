The proposal aims to launch a first‑generation petrol‑electric hybrid for mass production within two years. While the market context—cheap fuel, no regulatory mandate, and no competitive offering—creates a window for a technological lead, the technical and commercial uncertainties are substantial.

**Technical risk** – The power‑split transmission and nickel‑metal‑hydride (NiMH) pack have not yet proven durability in high‑volume use. Battery life cycles, thermal management, and integration with the drivetrain will likely require extensive validation. Early‑stage reliability problems could trigger costly warranty claims and damage brand reputation.

**Cost risk** – Novel components and low‑volume tooling will drive unit costs well above conventional models. Without a clear premium price tolerance from buyers, the program could erode margins or require deep subsidies.

**Demand risk** – No explicit consumer demand signal exists; the market has not yet expressed willingness to pay for hybrid efficiency when fuel is inexpensive. Launching a premium‑priced, untested product could lead to poor sales, inventory buildup, and sunk‑cost exposure.

**Strategic timing** – A two‑year horizon is aggressive for a technology that has not yet cleared reliability and cost hurdles. A staged approach—initial limited‑run pilot, extensive field testing, and incremental scaling—would allow the company to gather real‑world data, refine the power‑train, and build a credible value proposition before committing to full volume production.

**Recommendation** – Defer the full‑scale 1997 launch. Instead, allocate resources to a controlled pilot program with clear go/no‑go checkpoints based on battery durability, cost reduction targets, and verified consumer interest. This mitigates warranty exposure, preserves brand equity, and positions the company to capture the first‑mover advantage when the market and technology are ready.

DECISION: NO-GO
