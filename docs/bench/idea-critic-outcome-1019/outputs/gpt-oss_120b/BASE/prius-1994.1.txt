**Executive Summary**  
The proposal calls for a corporate‑level launch of a mass‑produced petrol‑electric hybrid passenger car by 1997, using an untested power‑split transmission and a nickel‑metal‑hydride (NiMH) battery pack. The market context includes cheap fuel in the primary export market, no regulatory mandate for hybrids, and the absence of any competing production hybrid. Battery durability is unproven, and the schedule is aggressive. The principal risks identified are high cost, reliability and warranty exposure, and the lack of a near‑term demand signal.

**Key Risk Assessment**

| Risk | Impact | Likelihood (given facts) | Comments |
|------|--------|--------------------------|----------|
| High unit cost (novel drivetrain, NiMH pack) | Margin compression or price premium beyond market tolerance | High | No demand signal and cheap fuel limit willingness to pay extra. |
| Reliability & warranty exposure | Potential large warranty claims, brand damage | High | Power‑split transmission and NiMH durability are untested at volume. |
| Lack of demand | Low sales, excess inventory, sunk R&D cost | High | Cheap fuel removes economic incentive; no regulation or competitor pressure to create buyer interest. |
| Schedule pressure (1997 launch) | Rushed development could exacerbate cost & reliability issues | High | 2‑year window is tight for new drivetrain integration and durability testing. |

**Strategic Considerations**

1. **First‑Mover Advantage vs. Market Pull** – While being the first volume hybrid could generate brand prestige, the absence of regulatory or economic drivers weakens the commercial case. Prestige alone rarely offsets high upfront costs and warranty risk.
2. **Technology Validation** – NiMH battery durability and power‑split transmission reliability must be proven before large‑scale production. The current timeline does not allow for extensive field validation.
3. **Financial Exposure** – Committing corporate resources to a program with uncertain revenue creates opportunity cost for other, potentially higher‑return projects.

**Recommended Course of Action**

- **Pause** the mass‑production commitment.  
- Initiate a limited‑run pilot program (e.g., 1,000–2,000 units) to gather real‑world durability data and gauge consumer response in a market segment willing to pay a premium for fuel‑efficiency.  
- Conduct a cost‑benefit analysis based on pilot outcomes before scaling.  
- Re‑evaluate schedule to allow at least 12‑18 months of durability testing and warranty modeling.

This approach preserves the strategic intent to explore hybrid technology while mitigating financial, reliability, and market‑demand risks.

DECISION: NO-GO
