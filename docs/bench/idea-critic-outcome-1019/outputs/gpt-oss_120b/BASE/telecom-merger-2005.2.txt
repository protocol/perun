The proposed $36 billion merger would give the buyer immediate access to a lucrative business‑customer segment that values the target’s push‑to‑talk walkie‑talkie service. That market is attractive, but it is built on a network that is technologically mismatched with the buyer’s platform and is not well suited for data traffic—a capability that is rapidly becoming essential for business customers.

Key considerations:

* **Technology incompatibility** – The two networks use different, non‑interoperable standards. Integrating them will require either a costly dual‑run of legacy equipment or a wholesale migration of the target’s customers to the buyer’s technology, both of which entail substantial capital outlay and operational risk.

* **Integration cost and timeline** – Aligning network operations, support systems, and billing platforms for two disparate infrastructures will likely exceed typical merger integration budgets. The need to maintain legacy equipment prolongs the integration window and exposes the combined firm to ongoing maintenance expenses.

* **Goodwill exposure** – The buyer must absorb a large goodwill balance, reflecting the premium paid for the target’s brand and customer relationships. If integration challenges erode the expected revenue upside, that goodwill could be impaired, harming earnings and share price.

* **Strategic fit** – While the target’s business‑customer base is valuable, its limited data capability means the buyer would need to invest heavily to upgrade that segment to meet future demand. The faster‑growing data market may be better served by organic network enhancement or a lighter‑weight partnership rather than a full merger that ties up capital in legacy assets.

Given the high integration risk, the substantial goodwill burden, and the mismatch between the target’s legacy, low‑data network and the buyer’s growth strategy focused on broadband services, the proposal presents more downside than upside at this time.

DECISION: NO‑GO
