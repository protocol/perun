The target OS currently commands only a low‑single‑digit share of the global smartphone market. Its ecosystem is already lagging because the dominant rival platform supplies the majority of applications and developers. The handset maker whose division is being considered for acquisition is experiencing declining smartphone sales, indicating that its products are not resonating with consumers despite the shared OS.

The proposed €5.4 billion purchase aims to lock a key device partner into the weak platform and to gain vertical control. However, the primary strategic problem is not lack of hardware ownership but the thin app ecosystem that drives user adoption. Owning the handset division does not automatically generate the missing apps, nor does it guarantee that developers will shift their focus. Without a compelling app portfolio, even a fully integrated hardware‑software offering is unlikely to attract a broader consumer base.

Integration risk is significant: the acquiring company must absorb a large workforce and manufacturing operation, a capability it has not demonstrated. Missteps could disrupt both existing product lines and the nascent OS effort. Moreover, other handset manufacturers may view the move as an attempt to monopolize the platform and could respond by abandoning the OS entirely, further eroding its market relevance.

Given these considerations, the acquisition does not address the core deficiency—an attractive, developer‑rich ecosystem—and introduces substantial operational and competitive risks. The proposal should be reconsidered until a clear, viable path to building or acquiring a robust app ecosystem is identified, and until integration plans are more concrete.

DECISION: NO-GO
