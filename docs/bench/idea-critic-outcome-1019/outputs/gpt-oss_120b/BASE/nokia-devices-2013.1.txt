The target’s handset division is the only major OEM that ships the software company’s OS, yet its smartphone sales are already in decline. Buying the unit for €5.4 billion would give the software firm control of the reference hardware, but it does not address the core weakness of the platform: an app ecosystem that is far smaller than that of the market‑leader. Developers choose platforms based on user reach, not on who manufactures the device, so ownership of hardware will not automatically attract the missing apps or reverse the “app‑gap” that drives consumers toward the rival platform.

Financially, the price tag is sizable relative to the current revenue generated by the handset business, whose sales trajectory is negative. Even if the acquisition eliminates the risk of the OEM switching platforms, the software company would inherit a loss‑making operation that will require sustained investment to reverse. Integration of a large workforce, supply‑chain complexities, and the need to align engineering, marketing, and sales cultures present additional cost and execution risk.

Strategically, the move could provoke a hostile response from other OEMs, who may accelerate migration to the dominant platform to avoid dependence on a single, now vertically integrated, partner. This could shrink the software company’s remaining third‑party device base, further eroding market share and reducing the potential return on the acquisition.

Alternative approaches—such as partnering with multiple OEMs, investing in developer incentives, or licensing the OS—target the real problem (app ecosystem) without the heavy financial and integration burden of an outright purchase.

Given the mismatch between the acquisition cost and the modest, declining revenue of the handset division, the high integration risk, and the low probability that hardware control will close the app gap, the proposal is not justified in its current form.

DECISION: NO-GO
