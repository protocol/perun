The claim is that a $35 single-board computer sold by a charity in 2012 will successfully rekindle student interest in programming despite significant execution risks. The steelman is that removing the cost barrier of the core logic unit unlocks education for masses who cannot afford even "cheap" laptops, provided the ecosystem gap (power, display, input) can be bridged by schools or scavenged parts.

However, the skeptic identifies a fatal flaw in the "low-cost" premise: the hidden costs of peripherals. A board without a display, keyboard, or power supply is useless to a student unless they already own these items, which correlates strongly with already having access to a computer. If the school must provide them, the total cost per station likely exceeds that of a bulk-purchased used laptop or tablet, which offers a complete, supported experience. The charity lacks manufacturing and support experience; when inevitable hardware failures or compatibility issues arise, there is no infrastructure to handle returns or technical queries, risking brand damage and wasted school funds.

The "better way" analysis favors the status quo of refining existing low-cost laptop programs or tablet grants. These provide an integrated device with known support channels. The proposed path requires the user to become an integrator, a role schools are ill-equipped to fill at scale. The risk of a small first batch selling out creates artificial scarcity that hinders adoption momentum, while a large batch risks unsold inventory the charity cannot support.

The pre-mortem reveals a scenario where schools buy the boards, fail to source compatible power supplies or displays within budget, and the units sit unused in boxes. The "rekindling" never happens because the barrier to entry was shifted, not removed. The lack of a defined kill criterion or support plan makes this an irresponsible gamble for a charity. The proposal assumes a level of technical self-sufficiency in the target demographic that contradicts the need for "rekindling interest." Without a verified plan to solve the peripheral and support gaps, the project is likely to fail its mission and waste resources.

DECISION: NO-GO
