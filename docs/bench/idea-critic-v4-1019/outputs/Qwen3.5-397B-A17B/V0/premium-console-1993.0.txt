The claim is that licensing a high-end console design at a $700 price point in 1993 will establish a new industry standard. The steelman argues that leveraging multiple electronics manufacturers accelerates market penetration and shifts hardware risk to partners while securing royalty revenue.

However, the skeptic hat identifies a fatal contradiction. The proposal requires licensees to sell hardware at a profit, yet the $700 price tag is significantly higher than competitors who sell at a loss to subsidize game sales. In this market, hardware is a razor; profit comes from blades (games). If licensees must profit on the razor, the console becomes too expensive for the mass market, ensuring low adoption. Low adoption means few game sales, rendering the royalty model worthless. Licensees, facing unsold inventory at a premium price against subsidized competitors, will likely abandon the platform.

The "better-way" analysis shows the incumbent strategy (subsidized hardware) is dominant because it aligns incentives: cheap hardware drives the installed base necessary for software monetization. The proposed model misaligns incentives; manufacturers bear high costs with no guarantee of volume, while the startup bets on volume that the high price prevents.

The pre-mortem reveals the most likely failure mode: the console launches as a niche luxury item for early adopters but fails to reach critical mass. Competitors undercut the price, capturing the developer ecosystem. Without developers, the game library shrinks, further reducing hardware appeal. The kill criterion is clear: if the hardware cannot reach a price point competitive with subsidized rivals within the first year, the platform dies. Since the proposal explicitly mandates licensee profit on hardware, it structurally prevents the necessary price war.

The strongest attack survived is the assumption that "high-end graphics" alone justify a 2x-3x price premium in a market driven by software libraries. This fails because gamers prioritize game availability and total cost of ownership over raw specs when the price delta is this extreme. The proposal ignores the fundamental economics of the console business model established by prior generations.

DECISION: NO-GO
