The claim is that releasing two versioned variants of a creature-collecting game will drive sales via mandatory social trading, despite hardware decline and publisher skepticism.

**Steelman:** The mechanic transforms a single-player limitation (incomplete collection) into a viral social loop, leveraging the existing install base's desire for completionism to offset declining hardware sales.

**Attack & Alternatives:**
1.  **Declining Hardware:** If the console is dying, the addressable market for *any* version is shrinking. Two versions split an already fracturing user base, potentially ensuring neither version reaches critical mass for trading.
2.  **Perceived Greed:** Selling "half a game" twice risks brand toxicity. If players view this as a price grab rather than a feature, word-of-mouth turns negative, killing the second wave of sales.
3.  **Friction:** Trading requires a second player, a second copy, and a physical cable. This high friction kills adoption if the initial player density is low.
4.  **Alternatives:** A single version with time-exclusive events or DLC (if technically feasible) achieves replayability without the "cash grab" stigma or the requirement of immediate dual-purchase. Doing nothing avoids the reputational risk of a failed dual-launch.

**Pre-mortem:** The launch fails because the installed base is too small to sustain the trading economy. Players buy one version, cannot find trading partners locally, feel cheated by the missing creatures, and the game dies as "incomplete." The publisher's uncertainty is validated; the split development costs double the break-even point while halving the potential sales per SKU.

**Kill Criteria:** This strategy only works if the social pressure to "catch them all" outweighs the cost of buying two cartridges. Without evidence of a dense, active community ready to trade on day one, the friction of the link cable becomes a barrier, not a feature. The proposal assumes a network effect that the "aging console" context explicitly undermines.

The risk of alienating a shrinking customer base with a perceived money grab, combined with the high friction of requiring physical proximity for core gameplay completion on a dying platform, makes this approach too volatile without a guaranteed dense install base.

DECISION: NO-GO
