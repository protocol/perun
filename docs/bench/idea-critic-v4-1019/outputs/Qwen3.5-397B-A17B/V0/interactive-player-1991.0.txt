The claim is that a $1,000 hybrid disc player positioned between consoles and PCs will succeed in 1991 by delivering interactive multimedia to the living room.

Steelman: This device offers a dedicated, high-fidelity experience unavailable on current consoles, capturing early adopters before PCs become user-friendly enough for the couch.

However, the attack on this position is severe. First, the price point is fatal. At $1,000, the unit costs roughly 3–4 times more than contemporary game consoles. In 1991, this places it in the luxury tier without the productivity utility of a PC to justify the cost. Second, the "middle" positioning is a strategic void. Consoles win on price and games; PCs win on versatility and expanding multimedia capabilities. A device doing both poorly at a premium price satisfies neither segment. Third, the content library risk is circular: developers will not invest in interactive titles for a niche, expensive install base, and buyers will not pay $1,000 for a device with few discs. Finally, consumer confusion is a primary blocker; marketing a vague "interactive" category against established "gaming" and "computing" categories requires immense education spend with no guarantee of clarity.

The "do nothing" alternative is stronger: wait for PC prices to drop and console multimedia to evolve, avoiding the capital risk of manufacturing a likely obsolete bridge technology. A cheaper alternative would be a software-only approach or an add-on to existing consoles, though the proposal rejects this.

The pre-mortem is clear: the product launches, sales stall immediately due to price and confusion, third-party support never materializes, and the platform is discontinued within two years, resulting in significant sunk costs. The cost of delay is negligible compared to the cost of failure; waiting allows the market to define the standard rather than forcing a proprietary one.

There is no evidence provided that the target market has demonstrated willingness to pay $1,000 for non-productivity entertainment. The risks outweigh the potential first-mover advantage.

DECISION: NO-GO
