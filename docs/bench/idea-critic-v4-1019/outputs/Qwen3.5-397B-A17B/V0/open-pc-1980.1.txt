The proposal correctly identifies an existential threat: small-computer makers are growing fast while the mainframe giant remains static. The "steelman" argument is that speed to market via off-the-shelf parts and independent distribution is the only way to capture this segment before it solidifies, outweighing the risks of lower margins and copycats.

However, the risks are severe. Relying on outside software creates a dependency that could cripple the product if suppliers prioritize competitors or fail to deliver. Lower margins threaten the company's financial engine, and lack of retail experience invites channel conflict and brand dilution. Most critically, an open design with standard parts offers no defensible moat; rivals can indeed copy it instantly, turning the venture into a race to the bottom on price.

The "better way" analysis suggests that doing nothing preserves current high-margin dominance but cedes the future. A cheaper, simpler alternative might be acquiring an existing small-computer maker to gain immediate technology, software, and retail channels, rather than building from scratch in a year. Yet, acquisition carries integration risks and cultural clash.

The pre-mortem reveals a likely failure mode: the internal mainframe culture strangles the small team, delaying launch past the market window, or the open architecture allows clones to undercut prices before scale is achieved. The cost of delay is total market irrelevance in the personal computing sector.

Despite the risks, the status quo is fatal. The market shift is undeniable. While the specific tactics (open design, outside software) are risky, they are necessary constraints to achieve the one-year timeline. The risk of copying is real, but first-mover advantage in branding and distribution might suffice initially. The dependency on outside software is a strategic vulnerability that must be managed, perhaps by fostering multiple suppliers or planning an eventual internal takeover of the OS layer.

The proposal represents a necessary, high-stakes pivot. The risks are manageable compared to the certainty of decline if the company ignores the small-computer revolution. The team must be walled off culturally to succeed.
