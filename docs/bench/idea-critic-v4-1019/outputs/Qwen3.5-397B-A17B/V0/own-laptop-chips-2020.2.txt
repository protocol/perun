The claim is that shifting laptop production to in-house designed processors, mirroring the successful phone architecture, will yield net benefits despite transition risks. The steelman: this move secures long-term differentiation, margin control, and unified ecosystem performance, leveraging proven mobile silicon expertise to eventually outperform generic supplier units.

However, critical failures loom. First, software compatibility is not guaranteed; mobile architectures often lack the instruction sets or thermal headroom for legacy laptop workloads, risking a "broken promise" to professional users. Second, the "unproven" nature of these chips in high-performance computing means initial generations may underperform established supplier flagships, damaging brand reputation before maturity. Third, ending the supplier relationship removes a capacity buffer; if in-house production hits yield issues, there is no backup, potentially halting sales entirely.

The "do nothing" option retains immediate stability and leverages the supplier's R&D amortization. A "hybrid" alternative—introducing in-house chips only in lower-volume, specific models first—reduces blast radius while testing the waters. The proposal lacks a concrete kill criterion: what specific performance metric or market share drop triggers a reversion to the supplier? Without a defined rollback plan or a phased rollout strategy that isolates risk, the "all-in" transition is reckless.

The strongest attack survived is the assumption that mobile design success translates directly to laptop viability. While the engineering approach is similar, the thermal and power constraints differ vastly. If the company cannot demonstrate a prototype matching current supplier performance on key professional benchmarks within six months, the project must halt. Yet, the proposal as presented commits to the full switch without this validation gate.

Because the proposal ignores the immediate risk of software incompatibility, lacks a phased deployment strategy to protect the core business, and fails to define specific tripwires for abandoning the effort if initial chips underperform, it cannot be approved in its current form. The potential for catastrophic supply chain disruption and brand erosion outweighs the theoretical long-term gains without stricter safeguards.

DECISION: NO-GO
