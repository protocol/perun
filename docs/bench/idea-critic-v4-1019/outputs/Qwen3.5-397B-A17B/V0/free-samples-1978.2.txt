The proposal attempts to rescue a failing product by changing its name and relying on self-reported intent from free samples. This approach contains fatal flaws in evidence and logic.

First, the "disappointing sales" in four cities indicate a fundamental lack of product-market fit, not a branding issue. Renaming a product that users have already rejected in a test launch is a cosmetic fix for a structural problem. The core assumption—that the name is the barrier rather than the utility of a "re-positionable adhesive" itself—is unverified and likely false.

Second, the validation method is dangerously weak. Asking recipients if they "would buy" the product after receiving it for free measures politeness and curiosity, not economic commitment. History shows a massive gap between stated intent and actual purchasing behavior, especially for office supplies where procurement processes are rigid. Basing a national rollout on this vanity metric invites a catastrophic loss of capital.

Third, the "better way" is ignored. Before spending on a city-wide giveaway and national marketing, the company should have investigated *why* the initial test failed. Was it price, distribution, or genuine lack of need? A cheaper, safer alternative is to conduct paid pilot tests in a single office park where real transactions occur, proving willingness to pay before any free distribution.

The risks outweigh the potential rewards. The cost of free samples plus a national marketing budget, driven by unreliable data, threatens significant financial loss. The proposal fails to address the primary risk: that the product simply solves a problem nobody is willing to pay to fix. The lack of a concrete, monetary kill criterion (e.g., "X% of sampled offices place a paid order within 30 days") means the rollout could proceed despite continued lack of real demand.

This plan substitutes hope for evidence. It ignores the negative signal from the first test, relies on a biased validation metric, and exposes the company to high costs with no guarantee of revenue. The prudent path is to halt the current plan, investigate the root cause of the initial failure, and validate demand with real money, not free giveaways.

DECISION: NO-GO
