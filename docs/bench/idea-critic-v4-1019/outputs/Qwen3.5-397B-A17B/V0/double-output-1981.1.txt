The claim is that doubling production amidst a recession, rising interest rates, and massive unsold inventory will improve the company's position. The steelman suggests this is a desperate gamble to achieve economies of scale or secure market presence before competitors, betting that future demand will absorb the glut once rates fall.

However, the skeptic hat reveals fatal flaws. First, the company has sold only 3,000 of 7,500 units; demand is clearly insufficient. Doubling output exacerbates the inventory overhang, turning cash into depreciating metal sitting on lots. Second, high interest rates increase the cost of financing both production and dealer inventory. Dealers explicitly cannot finance more stock; forcing more vehicles onto them risks breaking the distribution network. Third, with limited cash, increasing burn rate without a corresponding revenue spike accelerates insolvency.

The "better way" analysis shows that doing nothing or reducing production preserves cash, the most critical asset during a recession. Alternatives like incentivizing sales or restructuring debt address the root cause (lack of demand/liquidity) rather than compounding it with supply.

The pre-mortem is clear: if we double production, we run out of cash within months as financing costs spike and storage fills up. The company becomes insolvent before the economy recovers. This is a one-way door to bankruptcy. The cost of delay in fixing the sales channel is high, but the cost of this specific action is immediate financial collapse. There is no evidence that doubling supply creates its own demand in a macroeconomic downturn, especially for big-ticket items requiring financing.

The strongest attack is the cash flow mismatch: production costs are immediate, while sales are stalled by macro factors. Doubling production widens this gap lethally. No kill criteria can save this; the premise violates basic liquidity constraints.

DECISION: NO-GO
