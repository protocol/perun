The claim is that a robotics company should bypass uninterested industry partners and directly launch a low-cost, randomized-motion vacuum cleaner in 2002, despite lacking consumer experience.

**Steelman:** The market rejects expensive, complex mapping robots. A "good enough," affordable device using randomized coverage solves the core pain point (cleaning floors) without the cost/complexity barrier, creating a new mass market where incumbents see none.

**Attack & Alternatives:**
1.  **Skeptic:** The lack of consumer experience is a fatal operational risk, not just a footnote. Manufacturing, distribution, and support for millions of units differ vastly from B2B prototypes. If the unit fails or jams frequently, brand reputation dies instantly.
2.  **Better Way:** Licensing the technology to an established toy or appliance manufacturer mitigates the distribution/experience risk while capturing royalties, rather than bearing full P&L risk alone.
3.  **Fit/Pre-mortem:** If the random motion leaves visible dirty patches in large rooms, early adopters (the most vocal segment) will label it "broken," killing word-of-mouth. The "dumb" approach must be framed as "robust," not "cheap."

**Verification of Premises:** The proposal assumes randomized movement is sufficient for customer satisfaction. Without mapping, the robot may miss spots repeatedly, leading to perceived incompetence. However, if the price is low enough, expectations shift from "perfect clean" to "maintenance clean." The two floor-care companies' disinterest likely stems from channel conflict or margin expectations, not necessarily product viability.

**Kill Criteria:**
*   **Metric:** Return rate < 15% in the first 6 months.
*   **Threshold:** If returns exceed 20% due to "poor cleaning," halt production.
*   **Delay Cost:** Waiting for mapping tech to mature cedes the "affordable" segment to potential copycats.

**Decision:** The risk of no consumer experience is severe but surmountable by focusing strictly on a single, simple SKU and leveraging existing electronics supply chains. The "randomized" approach is a feature (cost/reliability), not a bug, provided marketing manages expectations. The rejection by floor-care firms validates the need for a direct-to-consumer disruptor model. The opportunity to define a new category outweighs the execution risks if the team remains focused on simplicity.

DECISION: GO
