The claim is that licensing a computer platform to a toy company for a low-cost console in 1995 will successfully establish a consumer presence despite market dominance by rivals. The steelman posits that leveraging an existing toy distributor's reach offers the only viable, low-capital entry into a saturated market, trading margin for volume and brand visibility.

However, the attack reveals fatal flaws. First, the "small royalty" model creates misaligned incentives: the licensee bears the marketing risk and may under-invest to protect their own margins, while the licensor lacks control over brand positioning. Second, entering a market dominated by established players with a "low-cost" device reliant on a toy company's distribution channels risks brand dilution rather than enhancement; consumers may associate the computer maker with cheap toys rather than serious computing. Third, the proposal assumes the licensee will aggressively market a product where their primary profit driver (volume) conflicts with the licensor's need for brand prestige. The "do nothing" or "direct entry" alternatives, while riskier financially, preserve brand control and strategic focus.

The pre-mortem shows the product launching quietly, failing to gain traction against incumbents, and confusing consumers about the licensor's core identity. The cost of delay is minimal compared to the reputational damage of a failed, cheapened brand extension. There is no evidence provided that the toy licensee has the capability or incentive to compete with dedicated console makers. The kill criteria are absent; there is no defined metric for success or a stop-loss mechanism if brand sentiment turns negative.

This plan relies on unverified assumptions about licensee motivation and market reception without a mechanism to enforce brand standards or marketing spend. The risks of brand erosion and passive failure outweigh the potential for low-margin volume.

DECISION: NO-GO
