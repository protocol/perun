The claim is that migrating laptops to in-house silicon, mirroring the phone architecture, creates sufficient strategic advantage to outweigh transition risks. The steelman: this move unlocks deeper hardware-software integration, improves margins long-term, and differentiates the product line beyond what generic suppliers allow.

However, critical assumptions remain unverified. First, the "same design approach" assumes phone workloads (power-efficient, bursty) map cleanly to laptop workloads (sustained performance, thermal constraints); if the architecture cannot sustain laptop-class thermals without throttling, the product fails. Second, the software compatibility risk is understated: "keeping existing software running" usually requires emulation or a dual-architecture transition period, which historically degrades performance and confuses buyers, potentially shrinking the install base during the switch. Third, the cost of engineering time is presented as a one-time hit, but maintaining two distinct high-performance silicon families (phone and laptop) may permanently fracture R&D focus, slowing innovation in both.

The "do nothing" option retains proven performance and supplier scale, avoiding the "unproven" label that enterprise buyers often reject. A better way might be a hybrid approach: co-design with the supplier to customize off-the-shelf parts rather than building from scratch, capturing some differentiation with lower risk.

The pre-mortem reveals a likely failure mode: the new chips launch with 20% lower peak performance than the competitor's offering due to thermal limits, causing pro users to defect. The kill criterion is clear: if benchmark simulations do not match or exceed the supplier's current flagship within 6 months, the project must pivot back to external sourcing. Without evidence that the thermal envelope and performance targets are already met in simulation, proceeding now risks a public product failure.

The strongest attack survived is the strategic necessity of control; however, the execution risk regarding thermal performance and software emulation overhead remains too high to approve *as proposed* without a validated prototype showing parity. Since no evidence of such a prototype exists in the proposal, the risk of market confusion and performance regression is unacceptable at this stage.

DECISION: NO-GO
