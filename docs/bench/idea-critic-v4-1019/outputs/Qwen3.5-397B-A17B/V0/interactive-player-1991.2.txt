The claim is that a $1,000 hybrid multimedia player will succeed in 1991 by occupying a niche between consoles and PCs. The steelman: this device offers unique interactive capabilities unavailable elsewhere, justifying a premium price for early adopters seeking a dedicated living room hub.

However, the attack reveals fatal flaws in this positioning. First, the price point is prohibitive; at $1,000, it costs significantly more than dedicated game consoles and competes directly with full-featured personal computers that are rapidly adding multimedia capabilities. Second, the "hybrid" positioning creates consumer confusion: it is too expensive for gamers who only want games, and too limited for computer buyers who need productivity software. Third, the small library of interactive titles creates a vicious cycle: without users, developers won't create content; without content, users won't buy the hardware.

The "do nothing" alternative is superior: wait for PC prices to drop and multimedia standards to mature, or license the technology to existing console manufacturers to lower the entry barrier. A "cheaper way" exists by focusing on a single use case (e.g., gaming only) at a lower price point rather than attempting a costly all-in-one solution.

The pre-mortem is clear: the product launches, sales are tepid due to price and confusion, third-party support fails to materialize, and the platform is discontinued within two years, resulting in massive sunk costs. The cost of delay is minimal compared to the risk of launching a flawed product; waiting allows the market to define itself and hardware costs to decrease.

There is no evidence provided that the target market understands or demands this specific hybrid form factor at this price. The risks (price, confusion, lack of content) outweigh the unproven benefits. The proposal fails to beat the alternatives of waiting or narrowing the scope.

DECISION: NO-GO
