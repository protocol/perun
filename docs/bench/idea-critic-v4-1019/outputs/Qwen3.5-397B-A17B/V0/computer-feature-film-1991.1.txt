The claim is that a short-form computer graphics studio should immediately accept a 1991 production deal to create the first-ever fully computer-animated feature film with a major studio partner.

Steelman: This is a unique, once-in-a-generation opportunity to leverage proprietary technology and creative talent to define a new medium before competitors, securing market leadership despite inexperience with long-form production.

However, critical failures loom. First, the "no one has done it" risk is not just technical but economic; rendering costs for 90 minutes in 1991 could easily bankrupt a small company if the schedule slips, which is highly probable given their lack of feature-scale pipeline experience. Second, the power imbalance is dangerous: a small studio signing with a giant publisher without a finished product or proven long-form workflow invites creative interference, loss of IP control, or a buyout that dissolves the original team—the very outcome the lead animator previously tried to avoid by staying.

Better ways exist. Instead of a full feature, the studio could produce a high-profile short or a series of specials to prove the long-form viability and negotiate from a position of strength later. Alternatively, they could demand a co-production structure with shared control rather than a simple publisher deal, mitigating the "stronger hand" risk.

The pre-mortem is clear: the project drags on for four years, costs triple the budget due to rendering inefficiencies, and the studio partner exercises contractual rights to take over production, firing the original creators. The door is one-way; once signed, the small studio loses its independence and likely its existence if the film fails.

There is no evidence provided that the small company has secured financing caps, creative control clauses, or a technical pipeline capable of scaling 100x from shorts to features. Accepting now based solely on the offer's existence ignores the fatal mismatch in capacity and leverage.

DECISION: NO-GO
