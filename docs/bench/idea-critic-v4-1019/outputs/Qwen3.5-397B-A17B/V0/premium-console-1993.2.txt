The claim is that licensing a high-priced ($700), high-end console design to multiple manufacturers in 1993 will establish a new industry standard. The steelman posits that premium graphics justify a premium price for early adopters, and that spreading manufacturing risk across licensees accelerates market penetration without capital expenditure.

However, the attack reveals fatal structural flaws. First, the price point is disconnected from reality: existing consoles are significantly cheaper, and the proposed model requires licensees to profit on hardware, whereas competitors sell at a loss to subsidize a lucrative software ecosystem. At $700, the addressable market shrinks to a niche too small to sustain a "standard." Second, the incentive alignment is broken. Established competitors can undercut this price aggressively, knowing they recoup losses on game sales. Licensees, forced to sell at a profit, cannot match this pricing war and will likely abandon the platform when sales stall. Third, fragmentation risk is high; multiple manufacturers may produce incompatible variations or fail to coordinate marketing, diluting the brand before it gains traction.

The "better way" analysis shows the industry standard is actually the razor-and-blades model (loss-leading hardware, profitable software), which this proposal explicitly rejects. Doing nothing or adopting the competitor's pricing strategy is superior. The pre-mortem is clear: the console launches, sells few units due to price, licensees lose interest, developers ignore the tiny install base, and the platform dies within 18 months.

The evidence provided highlights that the market already has strong competitors using a proven, opposing economic model. There is no verified demand for a $700 console in 1993, and the reliance on licensee profits creates an insurmountable cost disadvantage. The kill criterion—market share growth—will never be met because the unit economics prevent competitive pricing. This is a one-way door to failure; once the brand is associated with an overpriced, failed format, recovery is impossible. The proposal ignores the fundamental driver of console success: installed base size, which is maximized by low hardware prices, not high margins.

DECISION: NO-GO
