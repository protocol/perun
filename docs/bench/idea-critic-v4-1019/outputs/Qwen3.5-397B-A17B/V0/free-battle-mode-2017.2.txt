The claim assumes asset reuse guarantees a two-month delivery for a complex last-player-standing mode, ignoring that network architecture, anti-cheat, and matchmaking for battle royale differ fundamentally from cooperative play. This is a false equivalence; the "two months" estimate likely covers only content creation, not the requisite backend infrastructure overhaul.

The "do nothing" alternative preserves the paid game's value proposition. The proposed free-to-play model introduces direct cannibalization risk without verified retention mechanics. Entering a genre dominated by an incumbent rival in 2017 requires a distinct competitive advantage, yet the proposal relies solely on existing assets, which may feel dated or mechanically misaligned with genre expectations.

The studio lacks operational experience with free-to-play economies. Launching a cosmetic-only revenue stream requires live-ops expertise, item shop balancing, and player acquisition budgets not mentioned in the plan. Without these, the mode may launch to zero monetization while fracturing the existing community.

The strongest attack is the timeline versus complexity mismatch. If the backend cannot support hundreds of concurrent players per match within eight weeks, the product fails at launch. The proposal treats this as a content update rather than a platform shift.

However, the market signal (rival's popularity) validates demand. The risk is execution, not concept. A "GO" decision is only viable if the two-month window is explicitly redefined as a closed-beta technical test, not a full commercial launch. The proposal as written promises a full release, which is unsupported by the evidence of the studio's current capabilities and the technical debt of adapting cooperative assets for competitive multiplayer.

The lack of a phased approach or specific kill criteria for the backend performance makes the current plan reckless. The studio cannot afford a public failure in a crowded market.

DECISION: NO-GO
