The claim is that selling an unfinished 2009 sandbox alpha immediately will succeed via community feedback. The steelman: early revenue funds development while loyalists shape the roadmap, bypassing traditional publishing gatekeepers.

However, the attack reveals fatal flaws in this specific context. First, the "feedback loop" assumes a tolerant audience that did not exist in 2009. The "Early Access" model was not culturally normalized or platform-supported (Steam Greenlight launched in 2012); releasing a broken product then signals "scam" or "abandonware," not "collaborative development." Second, the solo developer explicitly lacks support capacity. Selling to a "large audience" without infrastructure guarantees a flood of negative reviews and refund demands that will drown out constructive feedback, killing momentum before features can be added. Third, without a marketing budget, visibility relies entirely on word-of-mouth, which spreads negativity faster than novelty for rough products.

The "do nothing" alternative (finish a vertical slice first) or "seek publisher" path carries lower reputational risk. The proposal fails the "what must be true" test: it requires a 2009 market ready to pay for bugs and a solo dev capable of instant customer service, both unsupported by the facts. The risk of permanent brand damage from a premature, unsupported launch outweighs the potential cash flow. The lack of a kill criterion or phased rollout makes this an all-or-nothing gamble with high probability of total failure.

Strongest attack survived? None. The premise that buyers in 2009 would tolerate an unfinished state without platform-mediated expectations is historically false. The constraint of "no support capacity" directly contradicts the requirement to manage a paying public.

DECISION: NO-GO
