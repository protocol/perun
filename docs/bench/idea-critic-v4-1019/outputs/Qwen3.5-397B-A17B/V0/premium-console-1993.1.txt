The claim is that licensing a high-priced console design to third-party manufacturers in 1993 will establish a new industry standard. The steelman posits that leveraging established electronics makers' distribution and manufacturing scale allows rapid market penetration without capital expenditure, while high-end graphics justify a premium price to early adopters.

However, the skeptic identifies a fatal structural flaw: the incentive mismatch between the start-up, licensees, and the market. Established competitors sell hardware at a loss to subsidize game sales, creating a price ceiling far below the proposed $700. Licensees, required to make a profit on hardware, cannot lower prices to compete, nor can they absorb losses to gain market share. This forces the console into a niche luxury segment, preventing the mass adoption required to become a "standard." Without a large install base, game developers will not prioritize the platform, starving the ecosystem of content.

The better-way analysis shows the status quo (selling hardware at a loss) or vertical integration (controlling both hardware and software margins) are the only viable paths to mass market dominance. Licensing dilutes control over pricing and quality while adding a profit layer that makes the product uncompetitive. The "do nothing" option avoids sinking costs into a fragmented, overpriced launch.

The pre-mortem reveals the most likely failure mode: licensees fail to sell sufficient units at $700, exit the partnership to avoid losses, and the start-up is left with no manufacturing capacity, no market presence, and a reputation for an overpriced failure. The kill criterion is clear: if the hardware cannot be priced within 20% of competitors within six months, the model collapses. Since the proposal mandates licensee profitability on hardware, this price reduction is impossible by design.

The strongest attack survived is merely theoretical distribution speed, but it fails against the economic reality of 1993 console wars where price and game library are the primary drivers. The proposal ignores the fundamental economics of the industry.

DECISION: NO-GO
