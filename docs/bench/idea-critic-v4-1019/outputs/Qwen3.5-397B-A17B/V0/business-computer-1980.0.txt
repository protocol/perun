The claim is that adding lowercase and 80-column support to a successful product line will capture the business market within a year. The steelman is that these specific omissions are the sole barrier to enterprise adoption, and a rapid successor will secure market dominance before competitors react.

However, the proposal fails the "better-way" and "fit" tests critically. First, it mandates phasing out the only successful product before the new one proves reliable or gains software support. This violates the principle of preserving the revenue engine that funds the transition. If the new hardware has unproven reliability (as admitted), killing the cash cow creates an existential revenue cliff.

Second, the "do nothing" alternative or a "dual-track" approach is ignored. A cheaper, lower-risk path exists: release the business machine as a premium addition without sunsetting the original. This allows the market to validate the new features while maintaining cash flow from education and hobbyist sectors. The proposal's insistence on a hard cutover introduces unnecessary catastrophic risk.

Third, the timeline is incompatible with the complexity. Building complex new hardware, ensuring reliability, and porting a software ecosystem in under a year is historically improbable. The pre-mortem is clear: the device ships late or buggy, the old machine is gone, sales collapse, and third-party developers abandon the platform due to instability and a shrinking install base.

The strongest attack is the forced obsolescence of the current revenue generator. There is no kill criterion defined for what happens if the new machine's reliability fails initial testing, yet the plan proceeds to kill the old one regardless. Without a "go-if" condition tied to successful beta reliability metrics and a committed software pipeline, the decision to phase out the current model is reckless. The proposal demands a binary choice (replace or nothing) when a hybrid approach mitigates the primary financial risk.

Because the plan explicitly includes phasing out the only successful product before de-risking the successor, it threatens the company's immediate survival on an unproven bet.

DECISION: NO-GO
