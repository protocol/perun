Steelman: Leveraging social graphs to amplify ad relevance via purchase sharing increases revenue while offering users transparency and control.

However, this claim fails the pre-mortem and fit checks. The proposal assumes a notification and opt-out mechanism sufficiently mitigates privacy invasion. History and behavioral economics suggest otherwise: default settings drive behavior, and "notice and choice" rarely protects users when the default is maximum exposure. Sharing sensitive purchases (medical, financial, personal) with friends creates immediate, high-severity reputational damage for both the platform and the user. Once a purchase is broadcast, an opt-out cannot undo the social fallout.

The risk of partner embarrassment is critical. If a user's friends mock or negatively react to a purchase in the feed, the partner brand suffers public humiliation, likely causing them to terminate ad relationships—the opposite of the revenue goal. The "hard to withdraw" warning is a definitive one-way door signal; launching this creates a liability bomb that cannot be defused by a simple feature flag flip after data has propagated socially.

The "better way" analysis shows this approach is unnecessarily risky. Targeted advertising based on private signals (shown only to the user) achieves revenue goals without exposing sensitive user data to their social circle. The proposed method trades long-term trust and partner stability for short-term engagement spikes, violating the core constraint of maintaining a safe ecosystem.

The strongest attack survived testing: even with an opt-out, the initial default broadcast causes irreversible harm. A single wave of negative publicity regarding sensitive purchases could trigger regulatory scrutiny and mass user exodus, outweighing any incremental ad revenue. The kill criterion is clear: if the feature exposes any sensitive category by default, it must not launch. Since the proposal relies on broadcasting by default with an escape hatch, it fails the safety requirement.

This is a classic case where the technical ability to do something does not equate to strategic wisdom. The cost of delay is negligible compared to the cost of a privacy scandal.

DECISION: NO-GO
