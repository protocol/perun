The claim is that launching twelve plastic fashion models with a one-million-unit target will succeed without destroying brand equity. The steelman: leveraging low-cost manufacturing to capture a new, volume-driven market segment through fashion differentiation, treating the plastic not as "cheap" but as "accessible style," thereby creating a revenue stream distinct from traditional quality perceptions.

However, the attack reveals critical fractures. First, the reputational risk is understated; an inexpensive plastic product from a movement maker known for quality creates immediate cognitive dissonance. If the fashion angle fails, the brand is left with "cheap plastic" rather than "failed fashion," causing long-term equity damage that outweighs short-term volume. Second, the sales target of one million units assumes immediate market acceptance of a radical category shift. With no pilot data, this is a binary bet: hit the number or absorb massive inventory costs for a perishable fashion good. Third, the "fashion" defense relies on stable tastes, yet the proposal admits tastes change quickly. Twelve models lock capital into specific designs that may become obsolete before the first million units sell, especially if established brands cut prices to squeeze the newcomer.

The "do nothing" alternative preserves reputation and capital while allowing competitors to test the plastic water. A "better way" would be a limited regional pilot with two models to validate the fashion premise and price elasticity before committing to twelve models and a million-unit supply chain. The current proposal lacks a kill criterion for reputational damage or a phased rollout to mitigate inventory risk. It demands full-scale commitment based on unverified assumptions about consumer willingness to accept a luxury-adjacent brand in a disposable format. The aggressive target combined with the high risk of brand dilution and fashion obsolescence makes the current plan a reckless gamble rather than a calculated expansion. The evidence provided (risks of reputation, aggressive targets, volatile tastes) directly contradicts the feasibility of the proposed scale and scope.

DECISION: NO-GO
