The claim is that a small studio should accept a deal to produce the first all-CG feature film despite having no long-form experience, limited rendering capacity, and a powerful distribution partner.

Steelman: This is a unique window to define a new medium before competitors do, leveraging proprietary talent that larger studios are actively trying to poach. Waiting risks obsolescence or losing the lead animator anyway.

However, the failure hypothesis is severe: the company lacks the technical pipeline for feature-length rendering and the managerial structure for a multi-year production. Historically, technology transitions kill studios that overextend. The partner's control over distribution creates an asymmetry where the studio bears the production risk while the publisher captures the upside. If the film fails technically or financially, the company ceases to exist.

Alternatives include negotiating a co-production to share risk, licensing the technology instead of content, or producing a shorter special to prove the pipeline first. "Do nothing" preserves the company but likely loses the lead animator.

The pre-mortem reveals the most likely cause of failure is not creative, but operational: render times explode costs, causing cash flow collapse before completion. The "one-way door" nature of this commitment means there is no easy exit once resources are diverted from profitable shorts.

Evidence is entirely hypothetical as no data exists for a feature-length CG film in 1991. The assumption that the current team can scale is unverified. The assumption that the studio partner will act fairly is risky given the power imbalance.

Kill criteria must be immediate: if a technical proof-of-concept for a 5-minute sequence cannot be rendered within budget and schedule constraints in 90 days, the project must stop. Without this, the company is gambling its entire existence on an unproven workflow.

The proposal as stated lacks these safeguards, a phased approach, or leverage against the distributor. Approving it "as proposed" ignores the existential operational risks.

DECISION: NO-GO
