The proposal to launch a low-cost, randomized robotic vacuum despite lacking consumer experience and facing competitor disinterest is a classic "good enough" disruption play. The steelman is that mainstream floor-care companies rejected the prototype because it threatened their high-margin, feature-heavy business model, creating an opening for a cheaper, simpler alternative that prioritizes convenience over perfection.

However, the risks are severe. The company has zero consumer-product experience, meaning supply chain, retail distribution, warranty support, and brand trust are unknown variables that could destroy capital before product-market fit is found. Randomized coverage invites criticism on efficacy; if users perceive the device as "dumb" compared to mapping rivals, the low price may not compensate for the perceived lack of value. Furthermore, two potential partners explicitly rejected the concept, suggesting the market signal is negative, not just misaligned with incumbent incentives.

The "better way" would be to secure a licensing deal or joint venture with an established consumer brand to mitigate the distribution and trust gaps, rather than going it alone. Alternatively, a limited direct-to-consumer pilot could validate the "random vs. map" value proposition without the cost of a full launch. The proposal ignores the cost of building a consumer brand from scratch.

The kill criteria are missing: there is no defined metric for adoption success, no cap on initial spend, and no pivot point if early sales fail to materialize. Launching a physical hardware product without a safety net or verified demand channel is a one-way door with high irreversible costs. The assumption that "low price" overrides "no trust" and "random movement" is unproven. Without a partner to absorb go-to-market risk or a validated pilot showing consumers accept randomized cleaning, the probability of failure due to operational inexperience outweighs the disruption opportunity.

DECISION: NO-GO
