The claim is that a young carmaker should immediately commit to an all-electric future and purchase a large factory in 2008. The steelman is that first-mover advantage in EVs justifies massive capital risk despite scarcity of infrastructure and production experience.

However, the proposal fails the evidence and fit checks for the 2008 context. First, the capital mismatch is fatal: the proposal admits the company lacks funds for the factory and batteries, yet recommends proceeding. In 2008, credit markets are freezing; securing "far more capital than the company has" is not a risk to manage but a hard stop. Second, the ecosystem is nonexistent: scarce charging stations mean the product has limited utility, suppressing demand precisely when cash flow is critical. Third, the "buy a large factory" strategy contradicts the "young carmaker" constraint; established makers are unlikely to sell productive assets cheaply during a transition they control, and retrofitting old ICE plants for EVs is often more costly than greenfield builds.

The "do nothing" or "wait" alternative is superior. Waiting allows battery costs to fall (following Moore's Law trends visible in 2008) and charging infrastructure to develop via government or utility investment. A niche, low-volume roadster (a la the actual path taken) validates the tech without the crushing overhead of a full-size sedan and a massive factory. Committing to a full-size sedan as the *only* product ignores the need for a learning curve in mass production; one mistake could bankrupt the firm before volume is reached.

The strongest attack survived is the fear of losing first-mover status. However, history shows that being too early with insufficient infrastructure often kills the pioneer, not the fast follower. Without a verified source of billions in capital and a concrete plan to solve the charging desert, this is a suicide pact, not a strategy. The risks are not merely "raised"; they are unmitigated showstoppers.

DECISION: NO-GO
