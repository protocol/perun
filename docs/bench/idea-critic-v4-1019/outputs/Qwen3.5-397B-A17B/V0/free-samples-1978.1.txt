The proposal fails the steelman test because it assumes the product's failure was due to naming and lack of awareness, rather than a fundamental lack of utility. The initial four-city test already demonstrated low demand; renaming and distributing free samples in a single mid-sized city does not address the core risk that office workers simply do not need re-positionable notes.

The "better way" analysis reveals a critical flaw: the success metric relies on recipients *saying* they would buy the product after receiving it for free. This is a vacuous metric. Users often express intent to purchase when an item costs them nothing, but this rarely converts to actual sales, especially for a novel category with unproven value. The proposal ignores the "do nothing" option, which preserves capital while the company investigates whether the adhesive has more viable applications.

Furthermore, the plan lacks a true kill criterion. A national rollout contingent on "most recipients saying they would buy" sets a low bar for a high-cost expansion. It does not require proof of repeat usage or willingness to pay before committing to a national budget. The risk of sinking costs into a national launch based on polite feedback from free samples is severe. The pre-mortem is clear: the company spends its marketing budget on a national rollout, only to find that the "interest" generated by free samples does not translate to retail sales, resulting in total loss.

The evidence from the first test (disappointing sales) contradicts the assumption that a name change and free distribution will fix the problem. Without a pilot that measures actual conversion to paid units rather than stated intent, the proposal is a gamble on hope, not data. The safest path is to halt the national plan and redesign the test to measure actual purchases, not surveys.

DECISION: NO-GO
