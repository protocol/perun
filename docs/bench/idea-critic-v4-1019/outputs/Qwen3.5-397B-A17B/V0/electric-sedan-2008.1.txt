Steelman: Going all-in on electric vehicles (EVs) with a purchased factory allows the young carmaker to bypass traditional development hurdles and dominate a nascent market before incumbents react.

However, the proposal fails the evidence and fit checks. The capital required for both a large factory and battery packs exceeds the company's current holdings, creating an immediate insolvency risk before production begins. Relying on a purchased factory from established makers introduces severe integration risks; legacy lines are rarely optimized for new EV architectures without costly retooling, contradicting the "speed" advantage. Furthermore, the scarcity of charging stations in 2008 severely limits the addressable market for a full-size sedan, making mass adoption unlikely in the near term.

The "do nothing" or "limited pilot" alternatives are superior. Building a single large factory locks the company into high fixed costs with no proven demand. A better way is to contract manufacture initially or build a smaller pilot line to validate the powertrain and supply chain before committing to massive real estate acquisition. The proposal ignores the "one-way door" nature of buying a huge factory; if the battery technology shifts or demand lags, the asset becomes a stranded cost that cannot be easily pivoted.

The strongest attack is the capital mismatch: the plan requires funds the company does not have, with no clear path to secure them given the unproven nature of the market and the company's lack of mass production experience. Established makers can respond by leveraging their existing supply chains and capital reserves to undercut prices or flood the market once the niche is proven. Without a phased approach, the company risks total collapse on the first attempt.

There is no kill criterion or leading metric defined to measure success before full commitment. The proposal assumes market readiness and capital availability without evidence.

DECISION: NO-GO
