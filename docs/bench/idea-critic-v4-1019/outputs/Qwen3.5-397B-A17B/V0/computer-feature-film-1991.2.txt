The claim is that a short-film CG studio should immediately accept a 1991 deal to produce the first-ever feature-length computer-animated film with a major studio partner.

Steelman: This partnership provides the only viable path to leverage the studio's unique animation talent into a profitable feature market, mitigating financial ruin by sharing the massive rendering costs and distribution risks with an established publisher.

However, critical failures loom. First, the "no one has done it" risk is not just technical but economic; rendering times in 1991 could make the budget explode before a single reel is finished, bankrupting the smaller partner if the deal lacks strict cost-overrun protections. Second, the power imbalance is dangerous: a large studio controlling distribution of a novel medium could easily shelve the film if early tests falter, leaving the small studio with no IP control and exhausted resources. Third, scaling from shorts to a 90-minute narrative requires a production pipeline that simply does not exist yet; assuming it can be built concurrently with the film is a fatal scheduling gamble.

Better ways exist. The studio could negotiate a "short-first" clause, producing a 30-minute special to prove the pipeline and audience appetite before committing to a full feature. Alternatively, they could seek a co-production deal that retains partial distribution rights or limits the larger studio's ability to shelve the project arbitrarily. Doing nothing and continuing lucrative commercials while slowly prototyping feature technology is also a valid, lower-risk alternative.

The pre-mortem is clear: the project fails because rendering costs triple, the studio partner loses confidence and delays release, and the small company burns out trying to invent a feature pipeline without a safety net. This is a one-way door; once resources are committed to a specific partner and format, retreat is impossible without insolvency.

Without verified evidence of a contract that caps financial liability, guarantees a release date, or allows for a phased production approach, the risk of total company failure outweighs the potential reward. The proposal as stated accepts too much unchecked risk.

DECISION: NO-GO
