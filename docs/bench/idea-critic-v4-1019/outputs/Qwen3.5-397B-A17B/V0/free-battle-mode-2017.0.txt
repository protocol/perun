The claim assumes asset reuse guarantees a two-month delivery and that cosmetic monetization will succeed without prior free-to-play infrastructure. This ignores the operational complexity of anti-cheat, server scaling, and live-ops required for a "last-player-standing" genre, which rivals have already optimized over years.

**Assumptions:** That "reusing assets" equates to 80% of the work, ignoring that network code, balancing, and economy design are net-new efforts. That players will pay cosmetics in a saturated market where the rival offers the same for free with a larger player base. That cannibalization is merely a risk, not a near-certainty that devalues the core paid product without a proven replacement revenue stream.

**Better Ways:** Instead of a separate title, integrate a limited-time event into the existing paid game to test retention and monetization mechanics with lower overhead. Alternatively, license the rival's technology or partner rather than building a fragile clone from scratch. The "do nothing" option preserves the studio's reputation for quality paid games, avoiding a likely failed entry into a hyper-competitive free market.

**Kill Criteria:** If the prototype cannot demonstrate a stable 60-player match with <100ms latency within four weeks, the technical debt is too high. If pre-launch sign-ups do not exceed 10% of the existing paid user base, the market interest is insufficient to justify the pivot.

**Pre-mortem:** The mode launches late due to net-code rewriting, the economy inflates immediately due to lack of monitoring, and the paid game's sales collapse as users wait for the free version. The studio burns cash on servers with no ROI, forcing a shutdown within six months.

The proposal fails to address the lack of free-to-play expertise and underestimates the engineering lift for a competitive multiplayer environment. The risk of brand damage and financial loss outweighs the speculative gain of chasing a trend dominated by an entrenched rival.

DECISION: NO-GO
