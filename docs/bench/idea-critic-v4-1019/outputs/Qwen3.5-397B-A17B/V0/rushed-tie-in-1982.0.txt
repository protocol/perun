The claim is that a single programmer can deliver a high-quality, market-ready game based on a hit film in five weeks, justifying a massive production run for Christmas. The steelman: speed-to-market captures the holiday wave, and the sunk license fee demands immediate monetization.

However, the attack reveals fatal flaws. First, the timeline is physically implausible for quality assurance; a five-week cycle from scratch typically yields a broken or unfinished product, damaging the brand associated with the hit film. Second, the "large production run" assumes perfect sales forecasting without any market testing. If the game is poor or the film's hype fades slightly, the company faces catastrophic inventory costs on top of the already high license fee. This is a one-way door with irreversible financial consequences.

The "do nothing" or "delay" alternative allows for a proper development cycle (3-6 months) and a smaller initial batch to test reception. The cost of delay (missing some Christmas sales) is far lower than the cost of failure (total inventory write-off plus reputational damage). The proposal ignores the fundamental constraint: software quality cannot be compressed linearly with headcount or hours, especially with a single developer. There is no kill criterion here; if the game is buggy at week four, the pressure to ship for Christmas will override quality gates, guaranteeing a bad release.

The strongest attack is the inventory risk coupled with the high probability of poor quality due to time compression. The proposal offers no evidence that a single developer can meet this specific scope in this timeframe, nor does it propose a pilot run to mitigate inventory exposure. It bets the entire margin on a best-case scenario with no safety net.

DECISION: NO-GO
