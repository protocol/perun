The claim is that breaking compatibility to rely on compiler-scheduled parallelism will succeed despite a massive installed base and emulation overhead. The steelman: this architectural shift offers such superior theoretical performance that it justifies the friction of emulation and the difficulty of compiler development, outpacing rivals extending the dominant design.

However, the attack reveals fatal flaws in the current premise. First, the "emulation tax" is a concrete failure scenario: if emulated legacy code runs slower than on existing 64-bit extensions, early adopters lose immediate value, stalling market entry before native software exists. Second, the dependency on "hard to write" compilers creates a chicken-and-egg deadlock; without mature compilers, hardware cannot deliver its promised parallelism, rendering the silicon inferior to simpler, compatible rivals. Third, the "huge installed base" is not just a barrier but an active moat; rivals extending the dominant architecture preserve native speed for legacy apps, neutralizing the only advantage of the new design while avoiding the emulation penalty.

The proposal assumes performance gains from parallelism will outweigh the systemic costs of fragmentation and translation. Yet, no evidence is provided that compiler technology in 1994 is ready to automate instruction scheduling effectively enough to beat decades of optimization in the dominant ecosystem. The risk of rivals extending the current architecture is not merely competitive; it is a superior strategy that captures the 64-bit benefit without the compatibility penalty.

Without a guaranteed path to native application availability on day one, or proof that emulation can match native speeds of extended rivals, the project faces a high probability of becoming a niche failure. The cost of delay is low compared to the cost of building a incompatible ecosystem that software vendors refuse to support. The kill criterion is clear: if native software coverage cannot reach a critical mass immediately, the architecture fails. Since the proposal relies on future compiler breakthroughs and accepts slow emulation, it fails the viability test against the safer rival strategy of extension.

DECISION: NO-GO
