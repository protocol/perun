The claim is that launching a heated tobacco product in 1988 as a "cleaner" alternative will succeed despite known usability and regulatory hurdles. The steelman posits that the massive sunk cost and potential for reduced smoke justify immediate wide promotion following test markets.

However, the skeptic hat identifies fatal flaws in the current plan. First, the core value proposition relies on health implications ("cleaner"), yet regulators are flagged as likely to object to such claims. Promoting widely before resolving this regulatory stance invites immediate legal injunctions and reputational damage. Second, the proposal admits smokers may dislike the taste and lighting mechanism; if the user experience is inferior to ordinary cigarettes, wide promotion will accelerate negative word-of-mouth, wasting the marketing budget. Third, "complicated to use" is a death sentence for a habit-forming consumer good where convenience is paramount.

The better-way analysis suggests that "do nothing" or a limited, non-health-focused pilot is superior to a wide launch. A wide launch assumes the product works for the mass market, but the evidence provided suggests it fails on taste and ease of use. The cheaper path is to fix the usability and secure regulatory clearance before spending more on promotion.

The pre-mortem reveals that six months post-launch, sales are flat, regulatory bodies have issued cease-and-desist orders regarding "cleaner" claims, and the brand is associated with a confusing, bad-tasting product. The sunk cost of development is not a reason to compound the loss with a failed launch; it is a reason to be more rigorous, not less.

The kill criteria are clear: if the taste and lighting mechanism cannot match the simplicity of ordinary cigarettes, and if regulatory approval for health-adjacent marketing is not secured, the launch must not proceed. The current proposal ignores these prerequisites.
