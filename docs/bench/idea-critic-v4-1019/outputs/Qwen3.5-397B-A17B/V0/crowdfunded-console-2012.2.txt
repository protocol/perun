The claim is that a small team can successfully fund, build, and ship a new open-source game console via crowdfunding within one year, despite having no marketing budget, no existing developer ecosystem, and facing entrenched competition from established console and mobile markets.

The steelman: An open platform could attract niche developers ignored by major studios, and crowdfunding might validate demand without upfront capital, allowing a lean team to deliver a specialized device to a dedicated community faster than incumbents can react.

However, the failure hypothesis is strong: hardware manufacturing is notoriously prone to supply chain delays, component shortages, and quality control issues that almost universally cause first-time hardware startups to miss "within a year" shipping promises. Missing this window destroys backer trust and cash flow. Furthermore, a console's value is entirely dependent on its software library; without a marketing budget or established relationships, attracting developers to build for an unproven platform with an unknown user base is a chicken-and-egg problem that crowdfunding alone rarely solves. The "no marketing budget" constraint is fatal when competing for attention against phone games and major consoles.

Alternatives include building the software platform first for existing hardware (zero manufacturing risk) or securing publisher partnerships before announcing hardware. The proposal attempts to solve hardware distribution, software ecosystem, and marketing simultaneously with zero leverage in any area.

The pre-mortem reveals the most likely outcome: the campaign raises some funds, but component costs rise, production delays push shipping to 18+ months, backers demand refunds, and the lack of launch titles kills momentum. The cost of delay is total project collapse. There is no evidence provided that the team has prior hardware shipping success or committed developer partners. The kill criteria for success (shipping in a year with a viable game library) are unlikely to be met given the constraints.

This proposal ignores the massive execution risk of hardware manufacturing and the ecosystem barrier to entry. It should not be approved as proposed; it requires a software-first validation or secured supply chain commitments before seeking public funds.

DECISION: NO-GO
