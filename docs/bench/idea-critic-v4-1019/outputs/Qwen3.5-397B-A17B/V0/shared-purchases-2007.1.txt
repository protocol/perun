Steelman: Leveraging social proof via purchase sharing drives ad revenue while maintaining user agency through opt-out mechanisms.

However, the claim that a "notification step" and "option to decline" sufficiently mitigate privacy invasion is unsupported by the stated risks. The proposal admits purchases can be sensitive; for many users, the mere existence of a notification implies data tracking they did not explicitly consent to share socially. The "option to decline" places the burden of privacy protection on the user, creating a default state of exposure. This contradicts the goal of sustainable revenue if it triggers a mass exodus or regulatory scrutiny, which the "hard to withdraw" risk amplifies.

Better ways exist that do not rely on exposing sensitive transactional data to peers. Aggregated, anonymized trend data ("Your friends bought X") offers social proof without individual surveillance. Contextual advertising based on site content rather than user history avoids the privacy trap entirely. These alternatives carry lower reputational risk and are easier to reverse.

Pre-mortem: Assume the feature launched and caused a public backlash. The cause was likely a high-profile incident where a sensitive purchase (medical, adult, financial) was broadcast to a user's network before they could opt out, or users felt coerced by the default-on design. Partners, embarrassed by negative public comments on their products, terminate contracts. The "hard to withdraw" architecture means the damage persists while engineering scrambles to dismantle the integration, causing prolonged negative press.

The cost of delay is minimal compared to the potential cost of a brand-destroying privacy scandal. The "notification step" is a weak control against the fundamental inversion of incentives: the platform profits from user embarrassment. No kill criteria or leading metrics are proposed to detect early signs of user churn or partner dissatisfaction specifically tied to this feature.

Without a default-off architecture, rigorous pre-launch legal clearance on sensitivity categories, and a guaranteed one-click rollback mechanism tested prior to release, the risk profile is unacceptable. The current proposal relies on users protecting themselves against a system designed to expose them.

DECISION: NO-GO
