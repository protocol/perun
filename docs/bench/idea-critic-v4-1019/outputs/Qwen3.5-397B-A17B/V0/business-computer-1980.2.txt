The claim is that adding lowercase and 80-column support will capture the business market, justifying the immediate phase-out of the current successful model. The steelman: these features are non-negotiable entry tickets for enterprise adoption, and delaying them cedes the segment to competitors.

However, the attack reveals critical flaws. First, the "phase out" strategy assumes immediate migration of the existing user base and software ecosystem, which is historically fragile; alienating current owners to chase unproven business users risks collapsing the only revenue stream before the new one stabilizes. Second, the timeline ("within the year") conflicts directly with the admitted "complex design" and "unproven reliability." Rushing a complex hardware revision without a reliability buffer invites a disastrous launch, potentially killing the brand's reputation for stability. Third, the proposal treats feature addition as sufficient for business success, ignoring that enterprise adoption often requires broader ecosystem support (software, service) not mentioned here.

The "do nothing" alternative (incremental updates) or a "parallel path" (keeping the old machine while testing the new one) offers lower risk. The cost of delay is losing some business leads, but the cost of failure is total revenue collapse if the new machine fails and the old one is dead. The proposal lacks a kill criterion for the phase-out; it should be contingent on the new machine proving reliability and sales traction, not a pre-committed sunset date.

The strongest attack survived is the necessity of the features themselves; without them, business entry is impossible. Yet, the execution plan (hard phase-out + rushed timeline) turns a necessary product evolution into an existential gamble. The risk of hardware unreliability combined with destroying the current cash cow is too high to approve as proposed. The plan must be revised to run both products in parallel until the new unit proves stable and adopted.

DECISION: NO-GO
