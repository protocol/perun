The proposal fails the "do no harm" test for an existing utility. Webmail is a private communication channel, not a discovery engine. Automatic enrollment with visible connections violates the implicit contract of privacy users have with their email provider. This is not a minor setting; it is a fundamental shift in the product's value proposition from "secure inbox" to "public square."

The risk of regulatory backlash is severe and immediate. In 2010, privacy regulators were already scrutinizing data usage. Enrolling millions without explicit, opt-in consent invites investigations and fines that outweigh any potential engagement metrics. The "hard to reverse" nature of exposed connections creates a permanent liability; once trust is broken by leaking a social graph, it cannot be fully restored by a settings toggle.

The "better way" is clear: social features must be opt-in, separate from the core inbox, and default to maximum privacy. A social layer inside the inbox distracts from the primary task (reading/sending mail) and risks low adoption because the context is wrong. Users do not want to socialize while paying bills or handling sensitive work.

The pre-mortem is straightforward: Launch occurs, privacy advocates expose the default visibility, regulators open inquiries, users mass-opt-out or abandon the service for competitors, and the company is forced to roll back the feature amid reputational damage. The cost of delay is zero compared to the cost of a botched launch. There is no kill criterion that saves this specific approach because the flaw is in the mandatory, default-on mechanism itself.

The strongest attack survived? None. The proposal's core mechanism (auto-enroll, visible defaults) is the failure point. No amount of tweaking saves a strategy that treats private correspondence as a social graph source without consent. The only viable path is a complete redesign to opt-in, which this proposal explicitly rejects.

DECISION: NO-GO
