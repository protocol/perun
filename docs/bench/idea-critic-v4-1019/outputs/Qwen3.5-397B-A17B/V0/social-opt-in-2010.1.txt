The claim is that auto-enrolling webmail users into a social layer with visible connections will succeed. The steelman: leveraging the existing user base creates immediate network effects that organic growth cannot match, making the product viable before competitors react.

However, the failure hypothesis is severe: treating private communication graphs as public social data violates user trust and regulatory norms. The "social network inside an inbox" conflicts with the core utility of email (private, task-oriented) versus social media (public, engagement-oriented). Automatic enrollment removes consent, turning a feature launch into a privacy incident. Once connections are exposed, the "hard to reverse" nature means the damage to brand reputation is permanent, even if the feature is shut down.

Alternatives:
1. **Do nothing:** Preserve trust and avoid regulatory scrutiny; risk missing a social trend but remain the trusted email provider.
2. **Opt-in launch:** Require explicit user consent. Slower growth, but respects privacy and reduces regulatory risk.
3. **Separate product:** Launch a standalone social network, linking to email only if users choose. Higher acquisition cost, but clear context for users.

The proposal fails the "fit" test: it sacrifices the north star (trustworthy communication) for a speculative engagement metric. The pre-mortem reveals a high probability of user backlash and regulatory fines, with low reversibility. The cost of delay (waiting for an opt-in model) is negligible compared to the cost of a privacy scandal.

There is no evidence provided that users want social visibility in their inbox; the assumption that "existing user base" equals "willing social participants" is unsupported. The risk of regulator attention is not hypothetical given the 2010 context of increasing data privacy concerns.

Kill criteria: If user trust drops or regulatory inquiries arise, the project must stop immediately. However, the current plan guarantees these triggers by design. The leading metric should be user retention and trust, not connection count.

DECISION: NO-GO
