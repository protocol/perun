The claim is that a cartoon studio should pivot from profitable shorts to a single feature film at 10x the typical budget, despite internal and external warnings.

Steelman: A feature film could unlock a new revenue ceiling and establish the studio as a major player, whereas staying with shorts caps growth and leaves the business vulnerable to market saturation in short-form content.

However, the risks are existential. The studio's current earnings are "thin," meaning the cash flow required to fund a 10x budget project leaves zero margin for error. If the feature fails, the studio likely collapses, taking the profitable short business with it due to resource diversion. The advice against this from the founder's family and industry insiders represents a massive "outside view" signal; these are people with skin in the game and industry context who see a fatal flaw the proposer is ignoring.

The assumption that audiences will sit through a feature-length cartoon is unproven. In 1934, this is not a minor behavioral shift but a fundamental change in consumption habits. There is no evidence provided of market testing or intermediate steps (e.g., a medium-length special) to validate demand.

Better ways exist:
1.  **Do nothing:** Continue steady, thin profits and wait for market conditions to improve or for a competitor to prove the feature model first.
2.  **Incremental scaling:** Produce a series of longer specials (20-40 minutes) to test audience retention and production logistics before committing to a full 80+ minute feature.
3.  **External financing:** Seek outside capital to share the risk rather than betting the entire studio's liquidity on one project.

The proposal lacks kill criteria. What specific metric proves the feature is working during production? What is the stop-loss point if costs overrun by 20%? Without these, the project is a blind bet.

The strongest attack is the liquidity risk: funding a 10x cost project from "thin" profits creates a single point of failure that threatens the entire enterprise. The "go" decision requires ignoring verified warnings and unproven market assumptions.

DECISION: NO-GO
