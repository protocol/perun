Steelman: Licensing converts fixed R&D and manufacturing risk into variable royalty revenue, enabling market entry without capital expenditure while leveraging a partner's distribution.

However, the attack reveals fatal flaws in this specific 1995 context. First, the "small royalty" model fails the unit economics test against the "dominant established makers." In a console war, victory requires subsidizing hardware to build an installed base, monetized later via high-margin software. A passive licensor earning pennies per unit cannot fund the ecosystem development (games, developer tools) required to compete with vertically integrated rivals who control the entire value chain. The licensee, motivated by their own margins, will not absorb the massive marketing spend needed to displace incumbents if the hardware margin is already eroded by the royalty.

Second, the brand risk is existential. If the low-cost console fails due to poor marketing or a weak game library (likely, as the licensor isn't driving it), consumers will associate the computer maker's brand with a "cheap toy" failure, damaging their core business reputation. The proposal assumes the partner will market "hard," but their incentive is misaligned: they bear the cost, while the licensor captures a slice of a likely small pie.

Third, the "do nothing" or "build internally" alternatives may be superior. Building internally retains control over the user experience and software ecosystem, which is the true moat in gaming. Licensing cedes control of the critical success factor: the software library. Without control over the platform's direction, the computer maker becomes a passive observer in a winner-take-all market.

The pre-mortem shows the product launching to silence, the licensee cutting losses after one quarter, and the computer maker left with a tarnished brand and no installed base. The cost of delay is low compared to the cost of a failed brand extension. The proposal lacks a mechanism to ensure the licensee prioritizes this product over their existing lines or to guarantee software support.

DECISION: NO-GO
