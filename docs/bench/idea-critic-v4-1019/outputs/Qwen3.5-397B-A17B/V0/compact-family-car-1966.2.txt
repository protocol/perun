The proposal assumes a dedicated dealer network is necessary to sell a compact family car, yet this contradicts the goal of minimizing upfront risk while ramping up a new plant. Building a new network is capital-intensive and slow, directly threatening the plant's capacity utilization targets before sales volume can justify the infrastructure cost.

The "better way" is to leverage existing distribution channels or partner networks to immediate launch, reserving dedicated outlets only for proven high-volume markets. The claim that a dedicated network is required for this specific segment remains unsupported; rivals succeed with low prices through established efficiencies, not necessarily exclusive storefronts.

The pre-mortem reveals a likely failure mode: the company burns cash on real estate and staffing for the new network while the new plant sits underutilized because market penetration is slower than projected. If buyers prefer cheaper alternatives or larger vehicles as warned, the fixed costs of the dedicated network become a sunk loss that accelerates bankruptcy rather than mitigating risk.

The kill criterion is clear: if the projected sales volume does not cover the operational cost of the new network within 18 months, the venture fails. However, the proposal offers no evidence that the 1100 cc segment is large enough to sustain both the plant and a new sales infrastructure simultaneously. The risk of cannibalization or misalignment with buyer preference (cheaper vs. larger) is heightened by the rigid cost structure of a dedicated network.

Without a phased approach that validates demand before committing to exclusive dealerships, the plan bets the company's liquidity on an unproven distribution model. The strongest attack—that the network cost destroys the margin needed to compete on price against established rivals—has not been refuted. The proposal must be revised to decouple plant utilization from immediate network construction.

DECISION: NO-GO
