The claim is that a young carmaker should commit exclusively to battery-electric vehicles (BEVs) in 2008, purchasing a large factory from established rivals to produce a full-size sedan.

Steelman: A bold, exclusive commitment forces focus and signals market disruption, while buying an existing factory theoretically accelerates production compared to building new.

However, the proposal fails the viability test under 2008 conditions.
1. **Capital Mismatch:** The proposal admits the company lacks capital for factories and batteries, yet the solution is to buy a *large* factory. Acquiring and retooling a mass-production plant requires significantly more upfront cash than greenfielding a smaller, specialized line. This directly contradicts the financial constraint.
2. **Infrastructure & Timing:** In 2008, charging infrastructure was negligible. Launching a full-size sedan (high battery cost, range anxiety sensitivity) before the market is educated or supported is a premature scale-up.
3. **Competitive Response:** Buying a factory from an established maker alerts competitors immediately, allowing them to leverage their superior supply chains and capital to crush a nascent rival before it produces a single unit.
4. **Experience Gap:** Mass production is notoriously difficult. Jumping straight to a "large factory" without pilot lines or iterative learning invites catastrophic quality and ramp-up failures.

Better ways exist: Start with a high-end, low-volume roadster to prove the technology and brand without the burden of a massive factory. Use contract manufacturing initially to preserve capital. Do not commit to "only" BEVs yet; keep optionality open until the technology matures.

The strongest attack is the financial contradiction: you cannot solve a capital shortage by undertaking the most capital-intensive option (buying a large plant) available. The risk of insolvency before production starts is near-certain.

Kill criteria: If the company cannot secure funding equal to 3x the estimated retooling cost within 6 months, or if battery costs do not drop by 15% year-over-year, the project must stop.

DECISION: NO-GO
