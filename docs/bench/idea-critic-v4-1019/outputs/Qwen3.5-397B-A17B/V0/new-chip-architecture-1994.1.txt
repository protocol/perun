The claim is that a jointly developed, compiler-dependent 64-bit architecture breaking compatibility with the dominant instruction set is viable despite emulation risks and a massive installed base. The steelman posits that compiler-scheduled parallelism offers such superior performance that it justifies the friction of emulation and the cost of new compiler development, capturing the high-end market before rivals extend the existing standard.

However, the failure hypothesis is severe: if emulation performance falls below a usable threshold for the huge existing software base, adoption will stall regardless of peak theoretical speed. The "better way" analysis favors rivals extending the dominant architecture to 64 bits, preserving compatibility while gaining speed, or waiting for hardware-based out-of-order execution to mature rather than offloading complexity to compilers. The installed base acts as a gravitational pull; breaking it requires a performance leap so vast that emulation cannot bridge the gap during the transition. The risk of compilers being too hard to write introduces a critical delay, allowing competitors to solidify their extended standards.

The pre-mortem reveals a scenario where the new chips launch with impressive benchmarks for native code but fail to run legacy enterprise software at acceptable speeds, causing OEMs to reject the platform. The cost of delay is low because the dominant architecture's extension is the path of least resistance for the market. There is no evidence provided that the compiler technology is ready or that the performance gain outweighs the compatibility tax. The proposal relies on the market accepting a downgrade in legacy performance for a future promise of speed, a bet history shows rarely succeeds against backward-compatible evolution.

Without a guaranteed mechanism to make emulation transparent and fast, or a killer application that strictly requires this specific parallelism immediately, the risk of creating an orphaned architecture is too high. The "do nothing" or "extend current" options dominate on risk and reversibility.

DECISION: NO-GO
