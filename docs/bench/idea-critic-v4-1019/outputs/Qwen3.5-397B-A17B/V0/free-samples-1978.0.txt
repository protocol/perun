The proposal fails the steelman test because it assumes the product's failure was due to naming and lack of awareness, rather than a fundamental lack of utility. The initial four-city test already demonstrated low demand; renaming and gifting units to a single mid-sized city does not address the core risk that office workers simply do not need re-positionable notes. This approach treats a potential product-market fit issue as a marketing execution problem.

The "better way" analysis reveals a critical flaw in the validation metric: relying on recipients saying they "would buy" is a vacuous metric. Politeness bias ensures high affirmative rates for free goods, but this rarely correlates with actual purchase behavior, as evidenced by the initial disappointing sales despite the product being available. A superior alternative would be to observe actual usage rates of the free samples over two weeks or offer a discounted paid trial to test real willingness to pay, rather than asking for intent.

The pre-mortem indicates a high probability of wasted capital. If the free samples are distributed and recipients express interest but never convert to paid users, the company will falsely interpret this as validation for a national rollout, leading to a much larger loss on marketing and inventory. The cost of delay is minimal compared to the risk of scaling a product with no proven demand. The proposal lacks a concrete kill criterion based on actual conversion rates, relying instead on subjective intent.

The strongest attack survived is the hypothesis that the product is useful but poorly named; however, the evidence provided (disappointing sales in four cities) strongly suggests the issue is utility, not identity. Without verifying actual usage or paid conversion in the pilot, the proposal cannot justify a national rollout. The plan commits to a binary "roll out nationally" based on weak leading indicators.

DECISION: NO-GO
