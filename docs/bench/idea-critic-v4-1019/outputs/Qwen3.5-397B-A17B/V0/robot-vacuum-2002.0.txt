Steelman: A low-cost, randomized robot vacuum can succeed by prioritizing affordability and simplicity over perfect coverage, targeting consumers who value convenience at a lower price point than premium mapping rivals.

**Skeptic Analysis**: The lack of interest from floor-care companies suggests B2B channels are dead, validating the pivot to direct consumer sales. However, the company's zero consumer experience is a critical vulnerability; without established trust, selling a device that enters homes is risky. The "randomized" approach invites criticism on efficacy compared to "smarter" rivals. Yet, if the price delta is significant, consumers may trade cleaning perfection for cost savings. The risk of buyers distrusting robots is real but mitigatable through branding and guarantees, not technical overhauling.

**Better-Way Check**: Alternatives include licensing the tech (rejected by partners) or waiting for mapping tech to cheapen (high delay cost). Doing nothing yields zero revenue. The proposed path—launching a stripped-down, affordable unit—is the only viable route to market entry given the constraints. It avoids the R&D sinkhole of mapping while undercutting competitors.

**Fit & Pre-mortem**: This fits a "disruptive innovation" stage, sacrificing performance for accessibility. If we assume the market cares more about "set and forget" convenience than 100% spot coverage, the random method works. Failure mode: The product is perceived as a toy rather than a tool. To prevent this, the launch must emphasize time saved, not square footage covered.

**Kill Criteria**: Proceed only if the unit cost allows a retail price at least 40% below mapping rivals. If the price gap narrows, the value proposition collapses. Success metric: Sell 10,000 units in Q1 with a return rate under 15%. If returns exceed 20% due to "poor cleaning" complaints, pivot or halt.

The objection regarding "no consumer experience" is severe but not fatal if the product is simple enough to require minimal support. The randomized approach is a feature (cheap, robust) not a bug, provided the price reflects the trade-off. The floor-care rejection confirms this must be a consumer play, not an industrial one.
