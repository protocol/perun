The claim is that a durable synthetic leather should launch as a premium shoe upper in 1963 via heavy marketing to manufacturers. The steelman posits that superior durability and modern aesthetics will overcome higher costs and comfort deficits, creating a new high-margin category.

However, the attack reveals fatal flaws in the current plan. First, the cost disadvantage against natural leather is structural; without a clear performance advantage (e.g., waterproofing or weight reduction) explicitly marketed to the *end consumer*, manufacturers have no incentive to retool factories for a more expensive input. Marketing to manufacturers alone fails to pull demand through the chain. Second, the comfort risk is existential in footwear; if wearers reject the material due to breathability or stiffness, brand damage will be immediate and irreversible, regardless of factory adoption. Third, the "fashion risk" is unmitigated; positioning as "premium" locks the product into trend cycles without establishing functional necessity.

The proposal lacks a "better way." It ignores a potential beachhead in non-footwear applications (e.g., upholstery or industrial goods) where comfort is less critical and durability commands a higher price premium, allowing production scale-up before tackling the resistant shoe market. It also fails to propose a consumer-facing campaign to create pull-demand, relying instead on pushing an expensive material to reluctant factories.

The pre-mortem shows failure: factories trial the material, consumers return shoes due to discomfort, retailers cancel orders, and the company is stuck with high-cost inventory. The cost of delay is low; entering without solving the comfort-to-cost ratio ensures failure. There are no kill criteria or metrics defined to test consumer acceptance before full-scale manufacturing commitment.

Without a strategy to address end-user comfort, a plan to lower costs or prove functional superiority over leather, and a shift from B2B push to B2C pull, this launch will likely result in significant financial loss and reputational harm. The current plan attempts to force a solution onto a market that has not agreed on the problem.

DECISION: NO-GO
