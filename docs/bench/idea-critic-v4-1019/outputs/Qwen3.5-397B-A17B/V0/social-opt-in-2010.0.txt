The proposal attempts to force a social layer onto a utility service (webmail) via automatic enrollment with permissive defaults. This strategy relies on the assumption that users value connectivity within their inbox more than privacy, a premise contradicted by the core function of email as a private communication channel.

The "better way" analysis fails because the proposal explicitly rejects the safest alternative: opt-in enrollment with strict default privacy. Automatic enrollment removes user agency, turning a feature launch into a potential regulatory liability and a breach of trust. The risk of regulator attention is not hypothetical; it is a near-certainty given the sensitivity of contact graphs and the non-consensual nature of the rollout.

The pre-mortem reveals a clear failure mode: users, feeling their privacy was violated by default settings, abandon the social features entirely while losing trust in the core mail service. The "social network inside an inbox" likely suffers from low engagement precisely because the context is wrong; people do not want to socialize where they pay bills and receive sensitive documents. Furthermore, the difficulty in reversing exposed connections creates a one-way door; once trust is broken and data leaked, it cannot be un-seen, causing permanent brand damage.

The kill criteria are absent. There is no metric defined for acceptable privacy complaints, no cap on the percentage of users enrolled before review, and no specific date to halt expansion if engagement metrics lag. Without an opt-in mechanism, the proposal cannot distinguish between genuine interest and forced adoption.

The strongest attack is that automatic enrollment with visible connections treats user data as a lever for growth rather than a trust asset. This approach prioritizes short-term network effects over long-term viability. Since the proposal offers no mechanism to mitigate the high risk of regulatory backlash and user churn, and because a safer, reversible opt-in alternative exists but was ignored, the claim that this should be done "as proposed" does not survive scrutiny.

DECISION: NO-GO
