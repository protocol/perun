**Steelman:** A $35 single-board computer removes the primary cost barrier for schools, creating a dedicated, hackable platform that generic tablets cannot match, thereby reigniting student interest in programming through direct hardware interaction.

**Attack & Alternatives:**
The claim faces four critical failures. First, the "low cost" is illusory if schools lack peripherals; a $35 board requiring a $100 monitor, keyboard, and power supply exceeds the total cost of a used office PC or budget tablet. Second, a charity lacks the supply chain leverage to manage manufacturing defects, logistics, and global support at scale; a single bad batch could destroy credibility. Third, selling through only two distributors creates artificial scarcity and single points of failure, guaranteeing stockouts or regional exclusion. Fourth, the market has shifted; by 2012, cheap Android tablets offered immediate usability without assembly, rendering a "kit" approach less attractive to time-poor educators.

**Pre-mortem:**
Assume failure: The device sells out instantly to hobbyists, leaving schools empty-handed. Parents buy the units but cannot configure them without technical skills, leading to returns. The charity bleeds cash on support tickets it cannot answer. The "educational" angle fails because teachers refuse to adopt hardware that requires IT setup they cannot perform.

**Kill Criteria & Evidence:**
The proposal fails the "total cost of ownership" test. Without a verified bundle including display, input, and power under $100 total, the value proposition collapses against existing tablets. The "two distributor" model fails the "availability" test for a global education initiative. The lack of manufacturing experience is a fatal operational risk without a named industry partner handling production and logistics.

**Verdict:**
The proposal relies on the unit price rather than the solution price. It ignores the operational reality of school IT departments and the competitive landscape of 2012 consumer electronics. The distribution strategy is too fragile, and the support model is non-existent. While the mission is sound, this specific execution plan is high-risk and likely to fail its primary beneficiary (schools) in favor of hobbyists.

DECISION: NO-GO
