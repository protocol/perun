The proposal attempts to enter a dominant console market via a low-cost, licensed peripheral in 1995. The steelman is that licensing minimizes capital risk and leverages a toy partner's distribution to bypass high barriers to entry. However, the attack reveals fatal structural flaws.

First, the "small royalty" model contradicts the "low-cost" strategy. With thin margins, the licensee lacks the financial incentive to market aggressively against established giants who control shelf space and mindshare. History shows that half-hearted marketing in the console wars leads to immediate obscurity.

Second, brand dilution is a near-certainty. If the product fails due to poor marketing or low quality (common in toy-company electronics), the consumer associates that failure with the computer maker, damaging their core brand equity without offering any upside control. The computer maker cedes all strategic levers: pricing, quality, and promotion.

Third, the "do nothing" alternative is superior. Entering a winner-take-all market with a weak, indirect position drains engineering support and management focus for negligible return. The "better way" is either a direct, fully-funded entry with a unique value proposition or staying out entirely. A passive royalty stream from a likely failed product is not a strategy; it is a slow leak of reputation.

The pre-mortem is clear: The console launches, sells poorly because the toy company treats it as a seasonal novelty rather than a platform, and the computer maker is remembered for a cheap gadget rather than innovation. The cost of delay is zero; the cost of this specific execution is brand damage. There are no kill criteria that save this specific structure because the incentives are misaligned at the contract level.

DECISION: NO-GO
