The claim that heavy marketing will secure adoption rests on the unverified assumption that manufacturers prioritize novelty over retooling costs and that consumers will accept a premium price for a material potentially inferior in comfort. The steelman is that this synthetic offers unique durability or aesthetic consistency unavailable in natural leather, justifying the switch despite friction. However, the proposal fails to address the core economic mismatch: if the synthetic costs more than leather and requires factory changes, the value proposition collapses unless the performance gap is massive, which is not demonstrated.

Alternatives include targeting niche industrial applications where comfort is irrelevant, licensing the technology to existing leather tanners rather than competing with them, or delaying launch until production costs drop. Doing nothing preserves capital while market signals clarify. The pre-mortem reveals a likely failure mode where early adopters face consumer backlash over comfort, leading to brand damage that marketing spend cannot repair. This is a one-way door; once positioned as "premium leather alternative," reversing course destroys credibility.

The evidence provided highlights four critical risks—manufacturing inertia, cost disadvantage, comfort deficits, and fashion volatility—yet the plan relies solely on marketing spend to overcome them. No data supports the assertion that marketing can override economic disincentives for manufacturers or physiological preferences for wearers. The cost of delay is low; waiting allows for pilot testing with a single partner to validate comfort and retooling estimates before a full-scale launch. Without a verified kill criterion based on actual wearer trials or manufacturer commitments, proceeding is speculative gambling disguised as strategy. The proposal lacks a concrete metric to define success beyond "spending heavily," making it impossible to pivot before total loss.
