The proposal to double monthly production while inventory sits at 4,500 units (7,500 produced minus 3,000 sold) during a recession with rising interest rates is fundamentally flawed. The core assumption—that increasing supply will solve a demand-side crisis—contradicts basic economic principles.

**Steelman:** The only defensible version of this claim is that massive overproduction is necessary to achieve unit-cost economies low enough to undercut competitors, thereby stimulating demand despite the recession.

**Attack:** This fails on three fronts. First, with 4,500 unsold units, the company already has roughly 18 months of inventory at the current sales rate; doubling production extends this to nine months of *new* production alone, ignoring existing stock. Second, high interest rates make financing this expanded inventory prohibitive for both the manufacturer and its dealers, likely causing dealer insolvency rather than increased sales. Third, the company has "limited cash"; doubling output requires immediate capital for parts and labor before any revenue is realized, creating a high probability of total liquidity collapse before the theoretical cost benefits materialize.

**Better Way:** The alternative is to halt or drastically reduce production to preserve cash, clear existing inventory through targeted incentives, and wait for macroeconomic conditions to improve. Doing nothing (maintaining current production) is also superior to doubling, as it slows the bleed.

**Pre-mortem:** If we proceed, the company runs out of cash within six months. Dealers refuse further deliveries due to financing costs, forcing the manufacturer to take cars back onto its own balance sheet, accelerating the bankruptcy. The "cost savings" from volume never arrive because the cars cannot be sold.

**Kill Criteria:** Any plan that increases cash burn while inventory turnover slows must be rejected. There is no evidence provided that demand is elastic enough to absorb double the output at lower prices, nor that the company can survive the cash gap.

The proposal ignores the binding constraint: lack of demand and capital, not production volume. Executing this would almost certainly guarantee immediate bankruptcy.

DECISION: NO-GO
