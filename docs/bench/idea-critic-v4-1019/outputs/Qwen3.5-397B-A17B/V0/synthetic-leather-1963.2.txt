The proposal assumes shoe manufacturers will accept higher costs and retool factories for a material with unproven comfort and uncertain fashion longevity. This ignores the fundamental economic reality of 1963 manufacturing: margins are thin, and leather is a known, trusted commodity.

**Steelman:** The synthetic offers superior durability and consistency, potentially creating a new "premium" category if marketed aggressively as futuristic and modern.

**Attack:**
1.  **Cost vs. Value:** If the synthetic costs more than leather, manufacturers have zero incentive to switch unless the consumer demand is undeniable. Marketing to manufacturers (B2B) cannot create consumer pull (B2C) fast enough to offset retooling costs.
2.  **Comfort Risk:** Leather breathes; early synthetics often do not. If early adopters experience discomfort, the brand becomes toxic instantly, killing the "premium" positioning.
3.  **Fashion Volatility:** Betting heavily on a single material in 1963 is dangerous. If fashion shifts back to natural textures or if the "plastic" look becomes associated with cheapness rather than premium innovation, the inventory becomes worthless.
4.  **Friction:** Asking factories to change processes for a more expensive, potentially inferior product is a non-starter without a massive subsidy or guarantee, which is not proposed.

**Better Way:** Do not launch as a direct leather replacement at a premium price point. Instead, target a niche where durability outweighs breathability (e.g., work boots or specific weather gear) at a competitive price to prove the technology, then expand. Alternatively, license the tech to one major innovator rather than spraying marketing spend across skeptical manufacturers.

**Kill Criteria:** The proposal lacks a pilot program. It bets the farm on immediate, heavy spending without validating comfort or factory willingness. The risk of alienating both manufacturers (by wasting their time) and consumers (by selling uncomfortable shoes) is catastrophic. The assumption that marketing spend alone can overcome higher costs and technical inferiority is flawed.

The proposal fails to address the primary barrier: why would a rational actor pay more for a potentially worse product? Without a clear answer, this is a speculation, not a strategy.

DECISION: NO-GO
