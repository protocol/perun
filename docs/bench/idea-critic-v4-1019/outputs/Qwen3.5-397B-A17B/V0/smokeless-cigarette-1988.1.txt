The claim assumes that "cleaner" (less smoke) automatically translates to consumer adoption and regulatory permission, ignoring that smoking is a sensory habit, not just a nicotine delivery mechanism. The steelman is that this product offers a harm-reduction bridge for current smokers without forcing a complete behavioral shift like patches or gum.

However, the "better-way" analysis reveals fatal flaws in the 1988 context. First, the "do nothing" option preserves high margins on a simple, beloved product; this proposal introduces complex manufacturing and a steep learning curve for users who value convenience above all. Second, the "health claim" risk is existential: regulators in the late 80s were increasingly hostile to tobacco marketing. Promoting a "cleaner" cigarette invites immediate scrutiny on whether "cleaner" implies "safer," potentially triggering a regulatory ban or mandatory warnings that destroy the value proposition. Third, the taste and lighting mechanism are direct attacks on the core user experience; if the ritual feels wrong, the nicotine yield is irrelevant.

The pre-mortem shows the product launching to confusion: smokers try it, find the taste flat and the lighting fiddly, and return to standard cigarettes. The company is left with massive sunk costs and a public relations nightmare where they appear to be manipulating health perceptions. The "large sum" already spent is a sunk cost fallacy; throwing good money after bad to launch a product that conflicts with user habits and invites regulatory war is irrational.

The strongest attack survived is the potential for a niche market of health-conscious smokers, but without verified data showing they exist in sufficient numbers to justify the launch cost, and with the high probability of regulatory retaliation against "health" implications, the risk outweighs the reward. The proposal lacks a concrete kill criterion for regulatory pushback or a pivot strategy if taste rejection exceeds 20% in test markets.

DECISION: NO-GO
