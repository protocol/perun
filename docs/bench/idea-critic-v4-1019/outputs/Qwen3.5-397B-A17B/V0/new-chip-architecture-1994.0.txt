The claim proposes a joint venture to replace the dominant processor architecture with a new 64-bit design relying on compiler-scheduled parallelism, breaking backward compatibility and relying on emulation for legacy software.

**Steelman:** A unified front by two major manufacturers could force a market shift toward a theoretically superior architecture, leveraging compiler optimization to outperform incremental extensions of the current standard.

**Attack:**
1.  **Emulation Penalty:** The proposal admits emulated programs may run slowly. Given the "huge" installed base, users cannot tolerate performance degradation for existing software. If emulation is slow, adoption is dead on arrival.
2.  **Compiler Complexity:** Writing compilers for this new design is acknowledged as "hard." If the toolchain lags, developers cannot optimize new software, negating the hardware's theoretical advantages.
3.  **Competitor Response:** Rivals can extend the dominant architecture to 64 bits without breaking compatibility. This offers users a smoother upgrade path with zero software friction, making the proposed break unnecessary.
4.  **Coordination Risk:** Joint development between two competitors often leads to design-by-committee delays, allowing the rival "extend" strategy to capture the market first.

**Better Way:** The "do nothing" or "extend current" alternative minimizes risk. Waiting for rivals to fail at extending their architecture, or joining that extension effort, preserves the installed base while achieving 64-bit goals. The cost of forcing a ecosystem reset outweighs the architectural purity gains.

**Fit & Pre-mortem:** Assuming the goal is market dominance, this fails the "north star" if users reject slow emulation. In a pre-mortem, the project fails because the compiler toolchain matures too late, and the rival's compatible 64-bit extension captures all enterprise customers who refuse to rewrite or slow down their software stack.

**Evidence & Kill Criteria:** The proposal relies on unproven assumptions: that emulation speed will be acceptable and compiler difficulty can be overcome quickly. There is no evidence provided that the performance gain outweighs the compatibility tax. The kill criterion is clear: if a compatible 64-bit extension exists or emerges within one year, this project must stop.

**Verdict:** The risks are existential and the alternatives superior. The proposal asks to bet the company on breaking compatibility when a lower-risk path exists.

DECISION: NO-GO
