The claim is that a $1,000 hybrid disc player will succeed in 1991 by occupying a niche between game consoles and PCs. The steelman: this device offers unique interactive multimedia capabilities neither cheaper consoles nor general-purpose PCs can yet match, justifying a premium for early adopters.

However, the attack reveals fatal flaws. First, the price point is prohibitive; at $1,000, it costs significantly more than dedicated game consoles and approaches low-end PC prices, which offer broader utility. Second, the "middle ground" positioning is a strategic liability: gamers will reject the high cost for limited titles, while PC buyers will prefer the flexibility of a computer gaining multimedia upgrades. Third, the small library of interactive titles creates a vicious cycle: low adoption discourages developers, and scarce software discourages buyers. Finally, consumer confusion is likely; without a clear identity (is it a toy? a tool?), marketing will struggle against established categories.

The "do nothing" alternative avoids sunk costs in a potentially fleeting format war. A "better way" would be to license the technology to existing PC manufacturers or wait for component costs to drop before entering the hardware market. The pre-mortem shows failure due to rapid PC multimedia integration making the standalone unit obsolete within months.

The evidence provided highlights that PCs are already gaining these abilities, directly undermining the product's unique value proposition. The risk of being stuck with expensive hardware and no software library is extreme. The cost of delay is minimal compared to the cost of launching a failed product category. There is no clear path to profitability given the price sensitivity and identity crisis.

DECISION: NO-GO
