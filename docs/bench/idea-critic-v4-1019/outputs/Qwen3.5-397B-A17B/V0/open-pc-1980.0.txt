Steelman: A dedicated, autonomous team can bypass institutional inertia to rapidly deploy a commodity-based product via new channels, capturing an emergent market before internal processes cause fatal delay.

However, the proposal fails the "fit" and "better-way" tests against the company's verified core competencies. The entity's established advantage is vertical integration (building, selling, servicing everything). The proposal explicitly discards this by relying on outside software, off-the-shelf parts, and independent retailers. This creates a "worse-off" scenario: the company enters a low-margin, high-competition arena while voluntarily surrendering its only proven defensive moats (proprietary control and direct service relationships).

The risks identified are not merely potential; they are structural inevitabilities of the chosen architecture. Rivals *will* copy standard parts; dependence on outside software *will* erode differentiation; lack of retail experience *will* increase channel conflict and cost. The proposal offers no mechanism to mitigate these specific vulnerabilities other than speed, which is an assumption, not a strategy.

Furthermore, the "do nothing" or "acquire" alternatives are ignored. Given the speed of the small-computer market growth, a one-year internal development cycle—even with a separate team—is likely too slow to capture leadership against entrenched agile competitors. Building from scratch contradicts the urgency implied by the threat. A superior path would be to acquire an existing successful small-computer maker to instantly gain retail channels, software ecosystems, and design maturity, rather than attempting to replicate them poorly from within a culture hostile to such operations.

The pre-mortem is clear: The project launches late, with higher costs than projected due to internal friction, achieves lower margins than core business, cannibalizes brand prestige without gaining significant share, and is eventually shut down as a distraction. The kill criteria are missing; there is no defined metric to stop the bleed if the "separate team" inevitably demands mainframe resources.

Without a concrete plan to secure the software ecosystem or a distribution strategy that leverages existing strengths rather than ignoring them, this is a speculative gamble that threatens core profitability for uncertain gains.

DECISION: NO-GO
