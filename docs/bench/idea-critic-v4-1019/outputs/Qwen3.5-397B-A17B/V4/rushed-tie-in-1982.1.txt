First call: NO-GO. The timeline is physically impossible for a quality product, and the financial exposure from a large production run on an unproven, rushed title is catastrophic.

**Steelman:** A single elite programmer can leverage the film's brand momentum to deliver a functional, profitable cartridge within five weeks if scope is ruthlessly limited to core mechanics, justifying a large print run to meet Christmas demand.

**Attack:**
1.  **Feasibility:** Five weeks for a full game in 1982 is unrealistic. Even simple games require debugging and playtesting. A single point of failure (the one programmer) means any illness or blocker kills the project.
2.  **Inventory Risk:** Ordering a "very large" run before verifying quality or market reception ignores the primary risk: the game might be unplayable. Unsold inventory of a licensed flop destroys margins faster than missing a season.
3.  **Quality vs. Brand:** Releasing a broken game under a hit film's name damages both the game studio's reputation and the licensor's brand, jeopardizing future deals. The high license fee increases the pressure to cut corners, worsening the outcome.

**Alternatives:**
*   **Do Nothing:** Save the license for a proper development cycle next year.
*   **Staged Run:** Build a minimal viable product in five weeks, produce a small test batch, and reorder only if sales validate quality.
*   **Scope Reduction:** Define a strictly limited feature set achievable in five weeks, but still avoid a massive initial print run.

**Pre-mortem:** The game ships in December with game-breaking bugs. Retailers return thousands of cartridges. The company cannot recover the license fee or manufacturing costs, leading to massive losses and industry ridicule.

**Blockers:**
*   **Timeline:** Five weeks is insufficient for development, testing, and manufacturing lead times for a "large run."
*   **Financials:** Committing to large-scale production without a playable prototype or market test is financially negligent given the already high sunk cost of the license.

**Risks:** Poor quality, brand damage, unsold inventory, single-developer bottleneck.

**Kill Criteria:** If a playable, bug-free prototype is not ready for internal review within 10 days, cancel the large production order immediately.

DECISION: NO-GO
