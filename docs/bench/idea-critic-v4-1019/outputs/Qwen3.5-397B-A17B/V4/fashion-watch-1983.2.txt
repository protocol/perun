First call: GO. The pivot from "plain timekeeper" to "fashion item" fundamentally changes the competitive landscape, making the low-cost plastic construction a feature rather than a defect.

**Steelman:** By positioning the product as disposable fashion rather than durable horology, the company bypasses its reputation risk and attacks a new volume market where price and style trump heritage.

**Attack & Options:**
The primary objection is brand dilution. However, treating these as distinct "fashion items" creates a firewall; failure here does not tarnish the core luxury line if marketed separately. The alternative ("do nothing") cedes the emerging low-cost, high-volume segment to competitors. A "premium plastic" strategy fails because the value proposition is trend velocity, not material worth. The aggressive one-million-unit target is risky but necessary to achieve the economies of scale required for an "inexpensive" price point; a conservative launch would likely fail on unit economics alone.

**Fit & Pre-mortem:**
This fits a strategy of market expansion through segmentation. If this fails, it will likely be due to supply chain inability to meet sudden fashion shifts or a misreading of style trends, not the material itself. The cost of delay is high: first-mover advantage in the "affordable fashion" watch category is critical before established brands react with price cuts.

**Blockers vs. Risks:**
*Blockers:* NONE. The proposal's own framing (fashion vs. timekeeper) neutralizes the reputation risk. The sales target is aggressive but not factually impossible given the global population and low price point.
*Risks:* Taste volatility and competitor retaliation are real but are inherent to the fashion strategy chosen. They are managed by the speed of the launch and the low capital commitment per unit, not by stopping the project.

The plan survives the attack because the repositioning solves the core contradiction of a luxury maker selling plastic. The risks are executional, not foundational.

DECISION: GO
