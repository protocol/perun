First call: NO-GO. The proposal commits to a capital-intensive strategy (buying a large factory, sole BEV focus) while explicitly admitting the company lacks the necessary capital for both the factory and battery packs. This is a fundamental resource mismatch that prevents execution as proposed.

Steelman: A dedicated, large-scale factory is the only way to achieve the volume and quality control required to disrupt the auto industry with a premium electric sedan, justifying the massive capital raise needed to secure it immediately in 2008.

Options:
1. Do nothing: Preserve cash, but miss the EV window and remain a design studio.
2. Contract manufacturing (proposed alternative): Use existing capacity (e.g., NUMMI, GM, Toyota) to build initial volumes. Lower capex, higher unit cost, less control, but viable without buying a plant.
3. Buy factory (Proposal): Maximum control and long-term unit economics, but requires immediate billions the company does not have. High risk of insolvency before production starts.

Fit: Aligns with a vision of total disruption and vertical integration. However, the 2008 context (financial crisis, credit freeze) makes raising the required capital for a "large factory" nearly impossible for an unproven entrant.

Pre-mortem: The company exhausts its cash reserve attempting to finance and retrofit the factory. Production is delayed by years due to construction and equipment installation complexities. Competitors launch compliant EVs using flexible existing lines. The company goes bankrupt before delivering a single vehicle. Door: One-way (sunk cost in real estate). Delay cost: Low; competitors are also hesitant in 2008.

Evidence: The proposal itself states the company "has little experience with mass production" and needs "far more capital than the company has." Buying a large factory exacerbates both weaknesses simultaneously.

Blockers:
1. Capital insufficiency: The plan requires funds the company explicitly does not possess, with no identified source to cover the gap for a *large factory* purchase in the 2008 credit environment.
2. Experience mismatch: Jumping straight to owning and operating a large factory without prior mass production experience creates an unmanageable execution risk.

Risks: Charging infrastructure scarcity, competitor response, battery supply chain constraints.

The proposal fails because it mandates the most expensive, hardest path (buying a large factory) as a prerequisite for starting, despite admitting the resources and experience to do so are missing. A staged approach using contract manufacturing would address the capital and experience gaps.

DECISION: NO-GO
