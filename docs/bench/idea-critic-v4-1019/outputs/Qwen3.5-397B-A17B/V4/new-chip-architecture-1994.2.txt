First call: NO-GO. Breaking compatibility with a massive installed base while relying on unproven compiler technology and slow emulation creates an insurmountable adoption barrier.

Steelman: A clean-slate 64-bit design exploiting explicit parallelism could theoretically outperform patched legacy architectures if compilers mature and emulation overhead becomes negligible.

Options:
1. Do nothing: Continue extending the dominant architecture; safe, preserves compatibility, lower R&D risk.
2. Rival extension: Competitors extend the dominant ISA to 64-bit; captures performance gains without breaking software.
3. This proposal: High cost, high risk, requires rewriting the entire software ecosystem toolchain.

Fit: Assumes the market values theoretical peak performance over immediate software availability. In 1994, the "Wintel" monopoly and enterprise reliance on legacy binaries make compatibility the primary north star, not raw instruction-level parallelism.

Pre-mortem: The project fails because emulation is too slow for practical use, compiler writers cannot automate instruction scheduling effectively, and developers refuse to port software to a niche platform. It is a one-way door with massive sunk costs. The cost of delay is low; extending existing architectures is faster and safer.

Evidence: The proposal admits emulated programs "may run slowly" and compilers "will be hard to write." These are not risks; they are known deficits in the current plan. The "huge" installed base is a factual blocker to breaking compatibility. No evidence suggests users will tolerate performance penalties for a new ISA.

Blockers:
1. The plan relies on emulation for the existing huge software base, but admits it may be slow. Without native performance parity, adoption is impossible.
2. Compiler technology required to schedule parallel instructions is admitted as "hard to write" with no proof of feasibility.
3. Rivals can achieve 64-bit performance by extending the dominant architecture, offering a compatible, lower-risk alternative that beats this proposal on the primary adoption criterion.

Risks: Market rejection, developer apathy, endless compiler debugging, superior compatible alternatives capturing the market.

The proposal fails because it asks the market to solve its hardest technical problems (compiler writing, emulation speed) before seeing any benefit, while a compatible alternative exists.

DECISION: NO-GO
