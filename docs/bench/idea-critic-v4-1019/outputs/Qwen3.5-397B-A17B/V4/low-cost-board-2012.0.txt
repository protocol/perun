First call: GO. The price point ($35) is an order of magnitude lower than existing alternatives, creating a new market segment rather than competing in an existing one.

Steelman: A ultra-low-cost, bare-bones computer removes the financial barrier to entry for students, forcing engagement with the hardware itself rather than passive consumption via polished tablets.

Options analysis:
1. **Do nothing:** Students continue using expensive, closed devices; programming interest remains stagnant.
2. **Subsidize laptops:** Cost per unit remains high ($200+), limiting scale and encouraging breakage/theft concerns.
3. **This proposal:** $35 entry. Requires peripheral scavenging (TVs, old keyboards), which aligns with the "maker" ethos and reduces total cost of ownership. It beats "do nothing" by enabling access where none existed.

Fit: Directly serves the north star of rekindling interest by making the machine affordable enough for every student to own one, even in low-income regions.

Pre-mortem: Failure occurs if the supply chain cannot scale past the first batch or if the "bring your own peripheral" requirement proves too friction-heavy for schools. However, sell-outs indicate demand, not failure. The lack of manufacturing experience is mitigated by using licensed distributors.

Evidence: The $35 price is the core fact. Ordinary laptops are not "cheap" relative to this; they are 5-10x more expensive. The risk of small batches is a distribution problem, not a product flaw.

Blockers: NONE. The stated risks (peripherals, batch size, inexperience, competition) are execution challenges, not fundamental flaws. Competition from tablets fails because tablets do not teach hardware-level programming effectively and cost significantly more.

Risks: Supply chain bottlenecks; user friction regarding peripherals. These are managed by capping the initial run and providing clear documentation on compatible low-cost peripherals.

Kill criteria: If the first batch does not sell out within 6 months, or if the cost to the end-user (including peripherals) exceeds $60, the model fails. Leading metric: Units sold to educational institutions vs. hobbyists.

DECISION: GO
