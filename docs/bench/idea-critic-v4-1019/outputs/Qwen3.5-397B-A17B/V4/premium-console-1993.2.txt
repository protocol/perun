First call: NO-GO. The proposed $700 price point is prohibitive against established competitors selling at a loss, and the licensing model creates a fatal misalignment where manufacturers cannot profit at that price while the start-up demands royalties.

**Steelman:** Licensing accelerates market penetration by leveraging existing manufacturing capacity, allowing the start-up to focus on software standards while partners capture hardware margins.

**Attack:**
1.  **Price vs. Model Conflict:** Incumbents sell hardware at a loss to subsidize game sales. This proposal demands manufacturers sell at a profit *plus* pay royalties. To cover costs and royalties, the retail price hits $700. In 1993, this is 3-4x the cost of competing 16-bit systems. Early adopters will not pay a 300% premium for unproven hardware when cheaper, established alternatives exist.
2.  **Licensee Incentive:** Electronics makers have no incentive to adopt this design. They cannot lower the price to compete without erasing their already thin hardware margins, and they cannot raise it further without killing demand. The "profit on hardware" requirement contradicts the industry standard of "loss leader" hardware, making the product uncompetitive immediately.
3.  **Market Reality:** With strong competitors already entrenched, a new entrant must offer superior value or lower cost. This proposal offers neither; it offers higher cost and fragmented manufacturing quality control.

**Alternatives:**
*   *Do nothing:* Avoids certain failure but misses the opportunity.
*   *Self-manufacture (Loss Leader):* Start-up absorbs hardware losses to drive unit volume, earning solely on game royalties. This aligns with industry norms but requires massive capital the start-up may lack.
*   *Exclusive Single Partner:* License to one major maker to share risk, but still requires subsidizing hardware to hit a competitive price point (<$300).

**Pre-mortem:** The launch fails because licensees refuse to produce units at a competitive price, or consumers reject the $700 tag. Sales flatline, licensees exit, and the standard dies before adoption.

**Blocker:** The economic model prevents a competitive retail price. Manufacturers cannot make a profit on hardware *and* pay royalties while selling at $700 in a market dominated by sub-$200 consoles. The stated facts confirm the price is "far above existing consoles" and competitors sell at a loss.

**Risks:** High execution risk, brand dilution across multiple manufacturers, lack of capital for marketing.

**Kill Criteria:** If a competitive price point (<$250) cannot be achieved without licensee losses, the model is invalid.

DECISION: NO-GO
