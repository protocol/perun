First call: GO. The $35 price point creates a unique economic category distinct from laptops, directly addressing the charity's goal of low-cost access for schools despite peripheral requirements.

Steelman: A dedicated $35 unit removes the barrier of expensive hardware, making programming tangible for students even if users must source peripherals, provided supply meets initial demand.

Alternatives:
1. Do nothing: Programming interest continues to decline due to high hardware costs.
2. Cheap laptops: Still significantly more expensive than $35, limiting scale for cash-strapped schools.
3. Wait for experience: The charity never gains manufacturing capability without starting; failure is a valid learning outcome.

Fit: Directly moves the north star of rekindling student interest through affordability.

Pre-mortem: Failure occurs if the "sell out" risk materializes as inability to scale production quickly, or if schools refuse to bundle peripherals. However, selling out indicates high demand, a solvable logistics problem, not a product flaw. The lack of experience is mitigated by using licensed distributors.

Evidence: The proposal states a $35 price vs. "ordinary laptops" which are implicitly higher cost. The risk of needing peripherals is acknowledged but does not negate the core value of the compute unit itself.

Blockers: NONE. No fact presented proves the plan fails as proposed. The lack of experience is a risk, not a blocker, as distributors handle sales logistics. The need for peripherals is a known constraint, not a hidden failure.

Risks: Supply chain inability to scale if demand spikes; schools finding peripheral sourcing too burdensome. These become kill criteria: if the second batch cannot be manufactured within 3 months of sell-out, or if <20% of buyers complete a setup, pivot strategy.

The cheaper alternative (doing nothing) yields zero upside. The $35 unit beats existing options on pure entry cost. The charity's inexperience is outweighed by the distributor model.

DECISION: GO
