First call: NO-GO. The proposal bets the company on unproven compiler technology and slow emulation to displace a massive installed base, while rivals can extend the existing architecture with native compatibility.

**Steelman:** A clean-slate 64-bit design with compiler-scheduled parallelism offers superior theoretical performance that justifies breaking compatibility if emulation gaps are closed quickly.

**Attack:**
1.  **Emulation Trap:** If emulated legacy code runs significantly slower than native execution on rival extended architectures, enterprise adoption will stall regardless of peak theoretical speed. The "huge installed base" is a moat, not just a number; users will not tolerate regression for speculative gains.
2.  **Compiler Bottleneck:** Shifting complexity from hardware to compilers creates a single point of failure. If compilers cannot consistently schedule parallel instructions efficiently across diverse workloads, the hardware advantage vanishes. Writing these compilers is historically harder than predicted.
3.  **Rival Response:** Incumbents extending the dominant architecture to 64 bits offer a two-way door (compatibility + growth) versus this proposal's one-way door (break compatibility). Unless the performance delta is order-of-magnitude, the path of least resistance wins.
4.  **Missing Evidence:** The proposal lacks data on emulation overhead benchmarks or compiler maturity timelines. Without proof that emulation reaches >80% native speed within year one, the risk of market rejection is critical.

**Alternatives:**
*   **Do Nothing/Extend:** Wait for rivals to fail or extend the current arch; lower risk, capped upside.
*   **Hybrid:** Co-develop an extension to the dominant arch rather than a breakaway; preserves ecosystem while gaining 64-bit.

**Pre-mortem:** Two years post-launch, the chip is technically brilliant but commercially dead because key enterprise software runs 40% slower via emulation, and compiler toolchains remain unstable for average developers. Rivals captured the 64-bit market with native backward compatibility.

**Blockers:**
*   No evidence provided that emulation performance is sufficient to retain the installed base.
*   No mitigation for the extreme difficulty of compiler development compared to hardware design.
*   Rival extension strategy offers strictly lower adoption friction.

**Risks:** Market fragmentation, developer hostility, delayed time-to-market due to toolchain immaturity.

The absence of a credible plan to overcome the emulation speed penalty and compiler complexity, against a rival path offering native compatibility, constitutes a fundamental strategic flaw.

DECISION: NO-GO
