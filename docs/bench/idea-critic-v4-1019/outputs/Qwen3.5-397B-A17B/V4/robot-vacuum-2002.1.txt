First call: GO. The proposal targets a massive, underserved market segment with a radically simpler and cheaper solution, accepting a known trade-off (random vs. mapped) to achieve affordability and speed to market.

Steelman: A low-cost, randomized robot vacuum can succeed by prioritizing "good enough" cleaning at a price point inaccessible to mapping competitors, effectively creating a new mass market rather than fighting for the high-end niche.

Options:
1. Do nothing: Cede the floor-care market to incumbents; zero revenue, zero risk.
2. High-end mapping robot: Requires immense R&D, higher price, slower launch; risks competing directly with established players on their turf.
3. Proposed low-cost random robot: Lower R&D, faster launch, accessible price; risks perceived as "dumber" but solves the core job (cleaning floors) for a fraction of the cost.

Fit: Aligns with a strategy of disruption through simplification and cost leadership. It assumes consumers value price and autonomy over perfect coverage efficiency.

Pre-mortem: Failure occurs if random movement leaves visible debris clusters that frustrate users, or if the brand's lack of consumer experience leads to quality control disasters. However, these are execution risks, not fundamental flaws. The "dumb" perception is a feature for cost-sensitive buyers. A one-way door exists only if significant capital is burned on tooling before validation; this must be staged.

Evidence: The lack of interest from two floor-care companies is not evidence of no market; it likely reflects their inability to see beyond their existing high-margin, manual-product business models (the innovator's dilemma). Their rejection validates the need to go direct-to-consumer.

Blockers: NONE. The stated risks (random coverage, no experience, trust) are manageable execution challenges, not facts that prove the plan fails. The lack of consumer experience is a hurdle, not a blocker, solvable by hiring or partnering.

Risks: Brand damage from poor reliability; slower adoption due to trust issues. These become kill criteria: if return rates exceed 15% in the first pilot or if NPS stays below 20 after 6 months, pivot or stop.

Decision: The potential to create a new category outweighs the execution risks. The rejection by incumbents is a signal to proceed, not stop.

DECISION: GO
