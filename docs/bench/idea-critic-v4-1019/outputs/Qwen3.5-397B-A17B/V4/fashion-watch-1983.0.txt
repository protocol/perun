First call: GO. The pivot from "plain timekeeper" to "fashion item" neutralizes the reputation risk by decoupling the plastic material from the brand's legacy of precision engineering.

**Steelman:** Selling twelve distinct plastic models as disposable fashion accessories creates a new, high-volume revenue stream that leverages existing manufacturing without cannibalizing the core luxury line, making the 1M unit target achievable through volume rather than margin.

**Attack & Alternatives:**
The primary objection is brand dilution. However, positioning the product strictly as "fashion" rather than "cheap watch" creates a psychological firewall; customers buy it for style, not horological status. Doing nothing cedes the emerging low-cost, high-style segment to competitors. Cutting prices on established brands destroys margin; launching a separate plastic line preserves it. The aggressive target is a feature, not a bug: it forces the distribution network to treat this as a volume game, distinct from the boutique sales of metal movements.

**Pre-mortem & Door:**
Failure mode: The company tries to sell these in luxury boutiques, confusing customers and depressing the brand. This is a two-way door; if the fashion angle fails, the line can be quietly discontinued or sold in discount channels without long-term damage. The cost of delay is high, as fashion trends move faster than traditional watch cycles; waiting allows competitors to define the "plastic watch" category first.

**Blockers & Risks:**
Blockers: NONE. The proposal explicitly addresses the reputation risk via the "fashion item" positioning.
Risks: Taste volatility and price wars are inherent to fashion but are mitigated by the low cost of goods and the diversity of the twelve-model collection.

**Decision:**
The plan transforms a potential weakness (plastic) into a strategic advantage (affordable fashion) while protecting the core brand equity. The sales target aligns with the volume nature of the product.

DECISION: GO
