First call: NO-GO. The studio lacks the operational DNA for free-to-play (F2P) monetization, and the timeline ignores the complexity of balancing a competitive genre dominated by an incumbent.

Steelman: Leverage existing assets to rapidly enter the battle royale market with a zero-cost entry point, converting a large user base into revenue via cosmetics while insulating the core paid product.

**Skeptic**: The claim that this can be built in "two months" is unsupported. A last-player-standing mode requires netcode for massive concurrency, anti-cheat, and server infrastructure that a paid cooperative game likely lacks. Furthermore, the studio has "not run a free-to-play business before"; without established pipelines for live ops, economy tuning, and cosmetic production, the monetization engine will fail regardless of player count. The risk of cannibalization is high if the free mode offers sufficient content to satisfy the core loop without purchase.

**Better-way**: The "do nothing" option preserves the brand's reputation for quality paid experiences. A safer alternative is a limited-time event within the existing paid game to test the mechanic, rather than spinning up a separate title with its own overhead. Reusing assets does not equal reusing architecture; the cost of building the F2P backend exceeds the two-month window.

**Kill-criteria**: If the studio cannot demonstrate a working prototype with 50+ concurrent players and a functioning cosmetic shop within four weeks, the project must stop. Without a pre-existing community eager for this specific mode, user acquisition costs will bleed the project dry before monetization matures.

**Blockers**: 
1. The "two months" timeline contradicts the technical reality of building a stable, large-scale competitive shooter from a cooperative codebase.
2. Lack of F2P operational experience creates a fundamental inability to execute the monetization strategy required for success.

**Risks**: Incumbent dominance, brand dilution, cannibalization of paid sales, and server instability.

The proposal relies on an impossible timeline and ignores the studio's lack of critical business infrastructure. It fails the feasibility test on its own stated constraints.

DECISION: NO-GO
