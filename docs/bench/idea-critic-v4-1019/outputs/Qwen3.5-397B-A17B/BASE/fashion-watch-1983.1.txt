The proposal to launch twelve plastic watch models as fashion items in 1983 carries significant strategic weight. The core tension lies between leveraging a new, inexpensive manufacturing capability and protecting an established reputation for quality. The risk that a plastic product could dilute brand equity is substantial; if the market perceives this as a decline in standards, long-term damage may outweigh short-term gains. Furthermore, positioning these items strictly as fashion accessories introduces volatility. Fashion trends are notoriously fleeting, making a one-million-unit sales target for the first year highly speculative rather than grounded in historical data.

The competitive landscape presents another critical hurdle. Established brands possess the liquidity to engage in price wars, potentially undercutting this new entry before it gains traction. Without a defensive moat or proven demand for plastic timepieces under this specific brand, the company is exposed to immediate margin compression. The aggressive volume goal assumes rapid market adoption without evidence of consumer readiness to accept a luxury or quality-focused brand in a disposable plastic format.

While innovation is necessary, the current plan lacks safeguards against reputation erosion and does not account for the speed at which fashion cycles can render inventory obsolete. Launching twelve distinct models simultaneously multiplies the risk of misreading consumer preference, potentially leading to excessive unsold stock. The combination of unproven material perception, volatile demand drivers, and hostile competitive responses creates a scenario where the probability of failure exceeds acceptable thresholds. Proceeding immediately ignores the need for market testing or a phased rollout to validate the "fashion item" positioning without endangering the core brand identity. The risks regarding reputation and the aggressiveness of the sales targets cannot be mitigated under the current proposal structure.

DECISION: NO-GO
