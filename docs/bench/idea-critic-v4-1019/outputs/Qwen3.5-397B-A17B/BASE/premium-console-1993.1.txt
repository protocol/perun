The proposal presents a fundamentally flawed business model for the 1993 console market. The core strategy relies on licensing hardware design to multiple manufacturers who must sell units at a profit, resulting in a $700 retail price. This pricing is prohibitive compared to existing competitors, effectively excluding the mass market and limiting the user base to a tiny fraction of early adopters.

Furthermore, the industry standard established by dominant players involves selling hardware at a loss to subsidize widespread adoption, recouping costs through software sales. By insisting on hardware profitability for both the start-up and its licensees, this proposal ignores the economic reality required to build a critical mass of users. Without a large installed base, third-party game developers will not prioritize the platform, starving the ecosystem of content.

The risk of fragmentation is also severe. Licensing to several electronics makers could lead to inconsistent quality, varied marketing efforts, and a diluted brand identity, confusing consumers in an already crowded market. Established competitors with deep pockets and integrated supply chains will easily undercut this high-cost, fragmented approach. The royalty model on games is contingent on high software volume, which is impossible to achieve with such a small, expensive hardware footprint.

The combination of an uncompetitive price point, a misunderstanding of the loss-leader hardware strategy, and the dangers of a multi-licensee fragmentation makes success highly improbable. The market conditions described strongly favor integrated, low-cost hardware strategies over this licensing model. Proceeding as proposed would likely result in immediate market rejection and financial failure.

DECISION: NO-GO
