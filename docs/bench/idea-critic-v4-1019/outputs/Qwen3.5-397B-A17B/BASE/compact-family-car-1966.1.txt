The proposal faces immediate structural contradictions. Building a dedicated dealer network introduces high fixed costs precisely when rivals are competing on low prices. This dual pressure—high overhead for distribution and aggressive pricing from competitors—threatens margins before sales begin. Furthermore, the new plant requires near-capacity utilization to justify its investment, yet market signals suggest buyer preference may lean toward either cheaper alternatives or larger vehicles, neither of which this specific 1100 cc compact model addresses.

Launching a niche product with expensive, exclusive distribution in a price-sensitive market creates a fragile business case. If buyers reject the car in favor of cheaper rivals, the plant will sit underutilized, failing to cover its costs while the expensive dealer network remains idle. Conversely, if the market demands larger cars, the product specifications are misaligned with consumer needs regardless of distribution quality. The risk of the dealer network becoming a financial drain is acute given the lack of existing infrastructure to share costs.

The combination of high entry costs for sales channels, intense price competition, uncertain product-market fit, and the rigid requirement for high volume production creates an unacceptable risk profile. The proposal attempts to force a specific solution onto a market that may not want it, while simultaneously burdening the operation with unnecessary fixed costs. Without evidence that the 1100 cc segment is underserved or that buyers will pay a premium for a dedicated network, proceeding now invites significant financial loss. The risks outweigh the potential rewards under the current constraints.

DECISION: NO-GO
