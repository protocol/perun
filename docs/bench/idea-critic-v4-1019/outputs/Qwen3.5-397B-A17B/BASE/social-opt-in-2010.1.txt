The proposal to automatically enroll existing webmail users into a new social layer with default visible connections presents unacceptable risks that outweigh potential benefits. The core strategy relies on bypassing explicit user consent, which directly triggers the identified risk of users feeling their privacy is disregarded. In the digital landscape, trust is the primary currency; violating it by making private connections public without permission can cause immediate and severe reputational damage.

Furthermore, the risk of regulatory attention is not merely theoretical but a critical threat. Automatic enrollment with exposed data invites scrutiny from privacy authorities, potentially resulting in fines, mandated shutdowns, and lasting legal complications. The difficulty in reversing the launch once connections are exposed compounds this danger. Unlike a feature that can be quietly toggled off, exposed social graphs create a permanent record of the privacy breach, making remediation impossible after the fact.

The concern regarding low ongoing use further undermines the business case. If the social network fails to gain traction within the inbox environment, the company will have incurred all the reputational and legal risks for zero strategic gain. Building a social layer requires voluntary engagement and network effects driven by user choice, not forced integration. A product that users did not ask for and cannot easily opt out of is destined for resistance rather than adoption.

Proceeding as proposed ignores the fundamental principle that privacy settings must be user-controlled, especially when transitioning from a private communication tool like email to a social platform. The automatic nature of this rollout treats the user base as a resource to be exploited rather than customers to be served. Given the high probability of backlash, regulatory intervention, and the irreversible nature of the data exposure, the project cannot move forward in its current form. The risks are immediate, severe, and potentially existential for the brand's trust equity.

DECISION: NO-GO
