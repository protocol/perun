The proposal presents a high-risk strategy to displace a dominant market leader by introducing a radically different 64-bit architecture in 1994. The core reliance on compiler-scheduled parallel instructions shifts significant complexity from hardware to software, creating immediate vulnerabilities. The identified risks are substantial and currently unmitigated. First, breaking compatibility with the existing instruction set alienates the massive installed base of software, which is a critical barrier to adoption. Second, the dependence on emulation for legacy programs introduces a severe performance penalty that could render the new systems unattractive to users accustomed to native speed. Third, the difficulty in writing compilers for this novel design suggests potential delays in toolchain maturity, further hindering developer adoption. Finally, the threat of rivals extending the current dominant architecture to 64 bits offers a smoother migration path for the market, likely making this disruptive approach unnecessary.

Proceeding without a clear strategy to overcome the emulation speed deficit or to guarantee compiler availability invites commercial failure. The market inertia favoring the dominant architecture is too strong to overcome with a solution that potentially degrades performance for existing applications. The risks of slow emulated execution and the sheer effort required to build a new software ecosystem outweigh the theoretical performance benefits of the new design at this stage. Without evidence that the compiler challenges can be solved quickly or that the performance gap in emulation can be closed, the venture is premature. The possibility of competitors achieving 64-bit capabilities within the existing framework renders this breakaway strategy excessively dangerous. Approval cannot be granted while these fundamental flaws remain unaddressed.

DECISION: NO-GO
