The proposal to launch a premium synthetic leather in 1963 faces immediate, fundamental contradictions that preclude approval in its current form. The core strategy relies on convincing shoe manufacturers to adopt a material that costs more than traditional leather while simultaneously requiring them to retool or alter their existing factory processes. Given the explicit risk that manufacturers are unwilling to change their facilities, the value proposition is inverted; suppliers will not pay a premium for a material that increases their operational friction and production costs.

Furthermore, the end-user experience presents significant hurdles. If wearers find the material less comfortable than genuine leather, repeat purchases and brand reputation will suffer regardless of marketing spend. In the footwear industry, comfort is a primary driver of adoption, often outweighing durability or aesthetic novelty. Launching a product known to be potentially inferior in comfort contradicts the "premium" positioning intended to justify the higher price point.

The market timing also introduces unacceptable volatility. Fashion trends are notoriously fickle, and the risk that styles could move away from this specific look renders a heavy, upfront marketing investment dangerous. Without guarantees of long-term stylistic relevance, the proposed heavy spending lacks a safety net. The combination of higher costs, manufacturing resistance, potential consumer discomfort, and trend volatility creates a scenario where the likelihood of financial loss is high.

The proposal assumes that marketing spend alone can overcome structural economic disadvantages and physical product deficiencies. This is a flawed assumption. Before any launch can be considered, the cost structure must be addressed to ensure the synthetic is cheaper or equal to leather, comfort must be validated through testing, and manufacturer buy-in must be secured without requiring costly factory changes. As it stands, the plan ignores critical barriers to entry and adoption. Proceeding now would expose the company to significant reputational and financial damage with little chance of success. The risks overwhelmingly outweigh the potential benefits under the current parameters.

DECISION: NO-GO
