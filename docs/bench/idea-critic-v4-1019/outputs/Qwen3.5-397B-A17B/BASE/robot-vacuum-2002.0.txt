The proposal presents a classic disruptor strategy: entering a market with a simplified, low-cost alternative to complex, expensive incumbents. While the lack of interest from two floor-care companies is notable, their business models likely rely on high-margin, professional-grade equipment or established distribution channels that a low-cost consumer robot would cannibalize or fail to support. Their rejection does not validate consumer disinterest; historically, legacy players often miss mass-market shifts because they are anchored to existing customer bases.

The identified risks are real but manageable through positioning rather than technical overhaul. The "randomized movement" limitation is a trade-off for affordability. If the price point is sufficiently low, consumers may accept "good enough" cleaning done frequently over "perfect" cleaning done rarely. The perception of being "less smart" can be reframed as "simple and reliable," avoiding the complexity and high failure rates often associated with early mapping technology.

The lack of consumer-product experience is the most significant hurdle. However, this can be mitigated by partnering with existing retail distributors who already have consumer trust and logistics networks, rather than building a direct-to-consumer infrastructure from scratch. Regarding trust, early adopters are often willing to tolerate imperfections in exchange for novelty and convenience, provided safety mechanisms are robust. The market in 2002 is ripe for automation, and waiting for perfect technology or external validation from skeptical incumbents could mean missing the first-mover advantage. The low-cost approach lowers the barrier to entry for customers, creating a new category rather than fighting for share in the existing high-end segment. The potential reward of defining a new mass market outweighs the operational learning curve.

DECISION: GO
