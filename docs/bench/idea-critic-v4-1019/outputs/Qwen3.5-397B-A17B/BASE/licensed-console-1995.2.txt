The proposal presents a high-risk, low-reward scenario for the computer maker. Entering a market dominated by established console manufacturers in 1995 requires significant differentiation and aggressive marketing, neither of which this plan secures. Relying on a toy and game company to drive adoption of a computer-based internet console is strategically misaligned; toy companies typically lack the technical distribution channels and brand credibility required for computing hardware.

The financial structure is fundamentally weak. Earning only a small royalty per unit caps upside potential while exposing the licensor to brand damage if the product fails. If the licensee does not market the product aggressively, sales will likely stagnate, yielding negligible revenue. Furthermore, decoupling marketing from the licensor creates a disconnect between the product and the computer maker's brand. Consumers may not associate the console with the licensor, missing an opportunity to expand market share or strengthen brand loyalty. Instead, a failed product could negatively impact the perception of the computer maker's core platform.

The risks outweigh the potential benefits. The market is already crowded with powerful competitors who control shelf space and consumer mindshare. Without direct control over marketing, pricing, or product quality, the computer maker becomes a passive observer in its own expansion attempt. The likelihood of the licensee under-investing in promotion is high given the low margins inherent in licensing deals where the licensor takes minimal risk. Additionally, the "small share" of the current market suggests the brand lacks the pull to drive sales indirectly.

This approach sacrifices long-term strategic positioning for immediate, albeit unlikely, minor cash flow. A more viable path would involve direct investment, a joint venture with shared marketing responsibilities, or developing a proprietary device to ensure brand alignment and quality control. Approving this proposal as written invites failure and wastes a potential growth opportunity. The terms do not protect the brand, ensure market penetration, or provide adequate financial return.

DECISION: NO-GO
