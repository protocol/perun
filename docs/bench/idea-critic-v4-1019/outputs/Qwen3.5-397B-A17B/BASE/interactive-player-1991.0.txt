The proposal faces critical structural flaws that make immediate approval inadvisable. The $1,000 price point creates an insurmountable barrier for the mass market, especially when positioned against significantly cheaper game consoles. In 1991, this cost exceeds what most families are willing to pay for an entertainment device that lacks the clear utility of a personal computer or the established library of a dedicated gaming system.

Furthermore, the value proposition is dangerously vague. If buyers do not understand what the product is for, adoption will stall regardless of technological capability. The acknowledged small library of interactive titles exacerbates this confusion; without compelling software, the hardware serves little purpose beyond playing music and photos, functions already served by existing, cheaper devices.

The competitive landscape is shifting rapidly against this specific hardware configuration. Personal computers are actively gaining multimedia abilities, offering greater versatility at a potentially comparable long-term value. Launching a specialized $1,000 box that sits awkwardly between two evolving categories invites obsolescence before market penetration occurs. The risk of creating a "white elephant" product—expensive, misunderstood, and underutilized—is extremely high.

While the vision of interactive multimedia in the living room has merit, this specific execution strategy ignores fundamental market realities regarding price sensitivity, consumer education, and content availability. Proceeding now would likely result in significant financial loss and brand damage. The gaps in positioning and the hostile pricing environment require a complete strategic rethink before any resources are committed.

DECISION: NO-GO
