The proposal presents a fundamentally flawed business model for the 1993 console market. The core strategy relies on licensing hardware design to third-party manufacturers who must turn a profit on each unit sold. This directly contradicts the established industry standard where dominant players sell hardware at a loss to subsidize adoption, recouping investments through high-margin software sales. By forcing licensees to maintain hardware profitability, the final retail price is driven to $700. This price point is prohibitively high compared to existing competitors, effectively limiting the addressable market to a tiny fraction of early adopters insufficient to sustain a new ecosystem.

Furthermore, the dual-royalty structure, demanding fees on both consoles and games, creates excessive friction. Licensees face squeezed margins due to the high manufacturing costs required for "high-end graphics," while the start-up extracts value from both ends of the transaction. In a market with several strong, entrenched competitors, entering with a premium-priced, closed-loop licensing model ignores the critical importance of install base volume. Without a massive user base, third-party game developers will not prioritize the platform, leading to a weak software library. A weak library further reduces consumer demand, creating a death spiral for the hardware.

The risks identified in the proposal are not merely hurdles; they are fatal structural defects. The pricing strategy alienates the mass market, the licensing model discourages manufacturer participation compared to integrated competitors, and the timing fails to account for the aggressive subsidy wars already underway. Attempting to set a "standard" requires market dominance or a disruptive cost advantage, neither of which is present here. The approach guarantees a niche product that will fail to achieve the critical mass necessary for a viable gaming platform. Proceeding as proposed ensures immediate financial failure and reputational damage in a highly competitive sector.

DECISION: NO-GO
