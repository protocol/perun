The proposal seeks to increase advertising revenue by broadcasting user purchases and activities from partner sites directly to friends' news feeds. While the plan includes a notification step and an opt-out mechanism, the fundamental design conflicts with user expectations of privacy. In 2007, social norms regarding digital data sharing were far more restrictive than today; users generally viewed their browsing and purchasing habits as private matters, not content for public dissemination.

The identified risks are severe and likely underestimated. Labeling this an "invasion of privacy" is not merely a potential reaction but a probable outcome. Sensitive purchases, such as medical supplies, gifts, or financial services, could cause significant personal embarrassment if revealed to a user's entire network. Furthermore, partner sites face reputational damage if their brands become associated with unwanted public scrutiny or negative social reactions stemming from these automated posts.

The argument that the feature will be "hard to withdraw once launched" is a critical warning. Introducing a functionality that triggers immediate backlash creates a trust deficit that is difficult to repair. Once users feel betrayed, retention drops, and the core value of the network erodes. An opt-out model places the burden of privacy protection on the user rather than the platform, which is often perceived as aggressive and disrespectful. Given the high probability of user revolt, partner dissatisfaction, and long-term brand damage, the potential short-term advertising gains do not justify the existential threat to the platform's reputation. The current safeguards are insufficient to mitigate the inherent intrusiveness of the mechanism.

DECISION: NO-GO
