The proposal to launch a heated tobacco product in 1988 faces immediate and substantial hurdles that outweigh the potential benefits of proceeding as currently structured. The core value proposition relies on the product being a "cleaner" way to smoke, yet regulators have already signaled objections to such health claims. Without regulatory approval for these specific marketing angles, the strategy to "promote it widely" becomes legally untenable and potentially liable for severe penalties.

Furthermore, consumer adoption risks are critically high. The target demographic consists of existing smokers who are accustomed to the specific taste and mechanical simplicity of traditional cigarettes. This new device introduces complexity in usage and alters the sensory experience through different heating mechanics and a carbon tip ignition system. If early users dislike the taste or find the lighting mechanism cumbersome, widespread adoption will fail regardless of marketing spend. The proposal acknowledges these specific consumer friction points yet offers no mitigation strategy other than proceeding to test markets without prior resolution of these fundamental design or perception issues.

Financially, while a large sum has already been invested, committing further resources to a full launch without resolving the regulatory and product-market fit uncertainties constitutes poor capital allocation. Sunk costs should not drive future investment decisions when the path to revenue is blocked by external regulatory barriers and internal product acceptance issues. The current plan ignores the necessity of securing regulatory clarity before defining the promotional strategy. Additionally, the complexity of the device requires a consumer education campaign that contradicts the ease-of-use expectation of the category.

Approving this launch now exposes the company to regulatory enforcement actions and significant brand damage if the product fails to gain traction due to its complicated nature. The risks are not merely theoretical; they are identified facts within the proposal itself. A strategic pause is required to address regulatory feedback, simplify the user experience, and validate taste preferences before any public rollout.

DECISION: NO-GO
