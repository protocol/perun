The proposal presents a fundamentally flawed business model for the 1993 console market. The core strategy relies on licensing hardware design to third-party manufacturers who must turn a profit on each unit sold. This directly contradicts the established industry standard where dominant players sell hardware at a loss to subsidize software sales and drive market penetration. By insisting on a profitable hardware margin for licensees, the final retail price is projected at $700. This price point is prohibitively high compared to existing competitors, effectively limiting the addressable market to a tiny fraction of early adopters.

Furthermore, the fragmented manufacturing approach introduces significant risks. Unlike a single integrated manufacturer controlling quality and supply chains, coordinating multiple electronics makers to maintain consistent standards while ensuring their individual profitability creates operational friction. If licensees cannot compete on price due to the mandatory margin, they will lack the incentive to prioritize this platform over their own proprietary products or other licensed ventures.

The competitive landscape is already crowded with strong incumbents who have deeply entrenched relationships with developers and consumers. These rivals can easily undercut the $700 price point, leveraging their willingness to absorb hardware losses to secure long-term software royalties. A new entrant cannot demand a premium price without a proven, exclusive library of hit games, which this proposal fails to secure upfront. The reliance on high-end graphics alone is insufficient justification for a price nearly double that of current market leaders.

The financial structure is unsustainable. Expecting multiple partners to build expensive hardware while the start-up extracts royalties from both hardware and software creates a double-margin problem. This inflates costs for the end-user and reduces the total potential volume of units sold. Without massive unit sales, the ecosystem will fail to attract third-party game developers, leading to a software drought that will kill the platform regardless of graphical fidelity. The risks overwhelmingly outweigh the potential rewards in this specific configuration.

DECISION: NO-GO
