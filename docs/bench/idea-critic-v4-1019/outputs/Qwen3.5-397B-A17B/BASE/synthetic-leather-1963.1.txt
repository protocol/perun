The proposal to launch a premium synthetic leather in 1963 faces insurmountable structural and market barriers based strictly on the facts provided. The core strategy relies on convincing shoe manufacturers to adopt a new material, yet a primary risk is their explicit unwillingness to modify existing factory infrastructure. Without manufacturer buy-in, the product cannot reach production lines, rendering heavy marketing expenditure ineffective. Marketing to manufacturers cannot overcome the physical constraint of retooling factories if they are resistant to change.

Furthermore, the economic value proposition is flawed. The synthetic material costs more than genuine leather while offering potentially inferior comfort to the end wearer. In a premium segment, customers expect superior quality and comfort, not a higher price for a perceived downgrade. Charging a premium for a product that is less comfortable and more expensive than the natural alternative creates a negative value equation that marketing alone cannot fix.

Finally, the threat of shifting fashion trends adds significant volatility. Investing heavily in a single application (shoe uppers) for a material that might fall out of favor exposes the company to rapid obsolescence. The combination of high production costs, manufacturer resistance to operational changes, potential consumer dissatisfaction regarding comfort, and fashion instability creates a scenario where the likelihood of commercial failure is high. The current plan does not address how to lower costs, prove comfort superiority, or mitigate the manufacturers' reluctance to alter their facilities. Proceeding now would likely result in significant financial loss with no clear path to adoption. The risks outweigh the potential benefits under the current conditions.

DECISION: NO-GO
