The proposal presents a high-risk, low-reward scenario for the computer maker. Entering a market dominated by established console manufacturers requires significant differentiation and aggressive marketing, neither of which is secured here. Relying on a toy and game company to drive adoption of a computer-based internet console creates a strategic mismatch; the licensee lacks the technical credibility to sell a computing platform, while the licensor surrenders control over brand positioning.

The financial structure is fundamentally flawed. Small royalties per unit mean the computer maker requires massive sales volume to generate meaningful revenue. Given the dominance of competitors and the lack of a dedicated marketing commitment from the licensee, achieving such volume is highly improbable. Furthermore, passively leaving marketing to a partner who may not prioritize this specific product exposes the project to failure without the ability to intervene.

Brand association risks are severe. If the low-cost console fails or is perceived as a mere toy, it could dilute the computer maker's brand equity rather than enhance it. Consumers may fail to connect the product with the licensor, rendering the branding exercise useless, or worse, associate the brand with a failed, cheap gadget. Without direct control over quality, messaging, or distribution, the computer maker assumes all the reputational risk for a fraction of the potential profit.

The 1995 market context suggests that internet connectivity was an emerging, complex feature requiring consumer education. A toy company is ill-equipped to explain the value of an internet console compared to dedicated gaming rivals. The combination of a weak revenue model, inadequate marketing guarantees, and high brand risk makes this specific licensing arrangement untenable. The company cannot afford to be a passive observer in a contested market with such limited upside. Approval in the current form would likely result in financial loss and brand damage.

DECISION: NO-GO
