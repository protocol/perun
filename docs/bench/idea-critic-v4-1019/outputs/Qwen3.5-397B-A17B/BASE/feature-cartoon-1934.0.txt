The proposal to produce a full-length animated feature in 1934 represents a monumental shift from the studio's current model of short films. While the existing business generates steady, albeit thin, profits, the suggested project requires a budget approximately ten times that of an average short. This massive capital expenditure must be funded entirely by current earnings, placing the entire financial stability of the studio at immediate risk.

Significant external and internal warnings highlight the precarious nature of this venture. The founder's own family and seasoned film-industry insiders have explicitly advised against proceeding. Their skepticism likely stems from the unproven market demand for feature-length animation; there is no guarantee that audiences possess the attention span or willingness to pay for a cartoon of this duration. If the public rejects the format, the studio faces catastrophic losses that could erase years of accumulated profit.

Furthermore, the risk of budget overruns is inherent in such an ambitious, unprecedented project. Without a prior template for success, cost controls may fail, draining resources faster than anticipated. Since the funding relies solely on the thin margins of current shorts, any disruption to regular production or unexpected cost spikes could lead to insolvency before the feature is even completed.

While innovation is necessary for growth, the current facts suggest the timing and financial structure are too dangerous. The consensus of experienced insiders and family members, combined with the lack of market validation for feature-length cartoons and the extreme leverage of the studio's entire cash flow, creates an unacceptable level of risk. Approving this now could destroy the company. The prudent path is to halt this specific proposal in its current form until risks are mitigated or external financing is secured.

DECISION: NO-GO
