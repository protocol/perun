The proposal outlines a high-stakes strategic pivot in 1994 to replace the dominant processor architecture with a new 64-bit design relying on compiler-scheduled parallelism. While technically ambitious, the identified risks present existential threats to immediate viability. The reliance on emulation for the massive existing software installed base introduces a critical performance bottleneck; if emulated programs run slowly, early adopters will reject the platform regardless of theoretical peak performance. Furthermore, the difficulty in writing compilers for this novel architecture suggests significant delays in toolchain maturity, potentially leaving hardware without optimized software support at launch.

The competitive landscape poses an equally severe challenge. Rivals extending the current dominant architecture to 64 bits offer a seamless migration path for users, preserving compatibility while delivering performance gains. This incremental approach neutralizes the primary advantage of the proposed radical break. History suggests that breaking backward compatibility requires overwhelming performance superiority to justify the transition costs for developers and end-users. Given the admitted hurdles in compiler development and the uncertainty of emulation speed, the proposal lacks a guaranteed mechanism to overcome the inertia of the established ecosystem.

Proceeding now exposes the partners to catastrophic market failure. The combination of a hostile software transition, unproven tooling, and a viable competitive alternative creates a scenario where the new architecture could become obsolete before gaining traction. The risks are not merely technical but fundamental to market adoption. Without a clear path to mitigate the emulation performance penalty or guarantee compiler availability, the project cannot succeed in its current form. The prudent course is to halt this specific joint development plan to avoid sinking resources into a potentially incompatible standard that the market will not embrace under these conditions.

DECISION: NO-GO
