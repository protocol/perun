The proposal to shift laptop production to in-house designed processors leverages existing expertise from the company's phone and tablet divisions. This strategic move aims to unify the hardware ecosystem, potentially improving performance integration and long-term cost efficiency. However, the risks identified are substantial and immediate. The requirement for existing software to remain fully functional on new, unproven architecture presents a critical technical hurdle. Failure here could render the entire laptop line unusable for current customers, causing severe reputational damage.

Furthermore, the company's laptop-specific chip designs lack a track record compared to the established supplier's proven solutions. Transitioning without a validated prototype phase introduces high uncertainty regarding performance stability and power efficiency. The allocation of significant engineering time to this migration diverts resources from other innovation streams, while the potential for buyer confusion during the switch could depress sales volumes. Finally, severing ties with the current supplier eliminates a reliable backup, creating a single point of failure in the supply chain.

Given that the proposal offers no mitigation strategy for software compatibility, no timeline for validating the new chips against market standards, and no plan to manage customer communication during the transition, the risks outweigh the potential benefits at this stage. Proceeding immediately without addressing these foundational gaps invites operational failure. The absence of a phased rollout plan or a parallel testing period makes the current proposal too volatile for execution. Approval requires a revised plan that demonstrates successful software emulation, benchmarked performance data, and a clear customer migration path. Until these elements are presented, moving forward is imprudent.

DECISION: NO-GO
