The proposal to launch a premium synthetic leather in 1963 faces insurmountable structural and market barriers based strictly on the facts provided. The core strategy relies on heavy marketing spend to persuade shoe manufacturers to adopt a material that costs more than traditional leather. This creates an immediate economic disincentive for producers who operate on thin margins and rely on established supply chains.

Furthermore, the operational friction is significant. Shoe makers are explicitly noted as potentially unwilling to retool or change their factories to accommodate this new material. Without guaranteed adoption from manufacturers, marketing spend directed at them yields no return on investment. The value proposition collapses if the supply chain refuses to integrate the product.

Consumer acceptance is equally uncertain. The material is described as potentially less comfortable than leather. In the footwear industry, comfort is a primary driver of repeat purchases and brand loyalty. A premium price point combined with inferior comfort creates a high risk of product rejection by the end user. Even if manufacturers were convinced to produce the shoes, poor consumer reception would lead to inventory glut and brand damage.

Finally, the threat of shifting fashion trends adds a layer of volatility. Investing heavily in a specific aesthetic or material type is dangerous when consumer preferences are fluid. If fashion moves away from the look of this synthetic, the entire inventory becomes obsolete.

The combination of higher production costs, manufacturer resistance to changing facilities, potential consumer discomfort, and fashion volatility makes this a high-risk venture with no clear path to profitability. The proposal lacks mechanisms to mitigate these specific, identified risks. Approving this plan now would commit significant capital to a product that is more expensive, less comfortable, and harder to manufacture than the existing standard, with no guarantee of market acceptance. The risks outweigh the potential benefits of durability alone.

DECISION: NO-GO
