The proposal to automatically enroll existing webmail users into a new social layer with default visible connections presents unacceptable risks. The core strategy relies on bypassing explicit user consent, which directly triggers the identified danger of users feeling their privacy is disregarded. In the digital landscape, trust is the primary currency; violating it by making private connections public without permission invites immediate backlash and potential regulator scrutiny.

The argument for automatic enrollment to jumpstart network effects fails when weighed against the severity of the consequences. Once private data is exposed and connections are mapped publicly, the damage is irreversible. The proposal admits the launch will be hard to reverse, meaning any error in judgment becomes a permanent liability. Furthermore, the risk that the feature may see little ongoing use suggests the potential reward does not justify the massive reputational cost. Building a social network inside an inbox requires voluntary engagement, not forced integration.

Users expect their email environment to remain a private communication channel. Transforming this space into a public social forum by default breaches the implicit contract between the service provider and the user base. The potential for regulatory intervention is high given the automatic nature of the data sharing. Proceeding as proposed would likely result in a loss of user trust, legal challenges, and a damaged brand reputation that outweighs any theoretical gain in social connectivity. The risks are immediate, severe, and largely uncontrollable once executed. The strategy must be rejected in its current form to prevent irreversible harm to the company's standing and user relationships.

DECISION: NO-GO
