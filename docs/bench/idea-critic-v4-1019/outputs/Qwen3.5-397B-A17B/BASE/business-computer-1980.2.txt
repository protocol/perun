The proposal addresses a critical market gap: the current successful product lacks essential business features like lowercase letters and an 80-column display. Without these, expansion into the lucrative business sector is impossible. The strategy to build a successor specifically targeting these needs is logically sound for growth.

However, the execution plan contains fatal flaws. Phasing out the only successful product before the new machine proves reliable is reckless. The risks identified are severe: complex design, tight schedules, unproven hardware reliability, and potential alienation of the existing user base and software developers. If the new hardware fails or the schedule slips, the company loses its current revenue stream with no replacement ready.

Launching within a year while managing such complexity suggests an unrealistic timeline. Moving existing owners and software makers to a new architecture requires more than just better features; it demands compatibility or a compelling migration path, neither of which is detailed. Ending the current product immediately upon launch assumes instant adoption, ignoring the inertia of the installed base.

The danger of cannibalizing a working revenue source with an unproven, complex successor outweighs the potential benefits at this stage. The company must first validate the new hardware's reliability and secure software commitment before threatening the current cash cow. A phased approach, keeping the old machine while introducing the new one as an optional upgrade, would mitigate the risk of total revenue collapse. As proposed, the plan gambles the entire company on a single, rushed transition. The schedule is too tight for the complexity described, and the strategy of immediate phase-out is premature.

DECISION: NO-GO
