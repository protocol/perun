The initial test launch across four cities yielded disappointing sales, indicating a fundamental lack of market demand or poor product-market fit. The current proposal attempts to address this by renaming the product and distributing free samples in a single mid-sized city, with a conditional national rollout based on recipient feedback. This approach is flawed for several critical reasons.

First, renaming a product does not alter its utility or solve the underlying reason why customers previously rejected it. If office workers did not see a need for the item during the first test, a new name is unlikely to create that need artificially. Second, relying on stated intent ("say they would buy it") after receiving a free gift is a unreliable metric. Recipients often express positivity to be polite, which does not translate into actual financial transactions. This creates a high risk of false positives leading to a costly failure.

Furthermore, the proposal ignores the significant financial exposure involved. Distributing free samples incurs immediate manufacturing and logistics costs without any guarantee of revenue. Given that the first test already demonstrated little demand, investing further capital into sampling before validating a genuine use case is financially imprudent. The risk of a national rollout requiring a large marketing budget is compounded by the lack of proven traction. Before committing to even a second city test, the company must identify why the initial launch failed. Is the price too high? Is the adhesive insufficient? Is the concept unnecessary? Without answering these questions, repeating the cycle with a new name and free units is merely guessing with company funds. The evidence suggests the product, in its current form, does not solve a pressing problem for the target audience. Proceeding as proposed ignores the clear warning signs from the initial data.

DECISION: NO-GO
