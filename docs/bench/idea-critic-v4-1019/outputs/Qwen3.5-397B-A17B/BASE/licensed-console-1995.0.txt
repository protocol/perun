The proposal presents a high-risk, low-reward scenario for the computer maker. Entering a market dominated by established console manufacturers in 1995 requires significant differentiation and aggressive marketing, neither of which this plan guarantees. Relying on a toy and game company to drive adoption of a computer-based internet console assumes capabilities they may not possess in the technology sector.

The financial structure is fundamentally weak. Earning only a small royalty per unit limits upside potential while exposing the licensor to brand damage if the product fails. If the licensee does not market the product aggressively, sales will likely stagnate, rendering the small royalty meaningless. Furthermore, the disconnect between the computer maker's brand and the final consumer product creates a strategic void. Consumers may not associate the console with the licensor, missing an opportunity to build brand equity, yet the licensor still bears the reputational risk if the device performs poorly.

The risks identified are severe and unmitigated. Dominant competitors have deep pockets and entrenched distribution channels. A passive licensing model surrenders control over the most critical success factor: market penetration. Without direct oversight of marketing or a substantial revenue share to justify the brand association, the computer maker effectively rents out its technology for negligible return while risking its reputation. The reliance on a third party to execute a complex launch in a hostile market is a strategic error. The potential for the licensee to underperform is high, and the consequences of failure outweigh the limited financial gains from small royalties. This approach lacks the necessary control and incentive alignment to succeed against entrenched rivals.

DECISION: NO-GO
