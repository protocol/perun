The proposal presents a classic disruptor scenario with significant hurdles. The inventor possesses a functional technology proven through thousands of prototypes, eliminating technical feasibility as a primary concern. However, the commercial risks are substantial. Pricing the unit above established competitors creates an immediate barrier to entry, especially when the value proposition relies on consumers accepting a higher upfront cost to save on recurring bag purchases. In 1991, consumer habits regarding disposable bags are deeply entrenched, and convincing the market to change behavior requires immense marketing capital, which a new entity lacks.

Furthermore, the absence of a manufacturing record introduces severe operational risk. Scaling from prototype to mass production often reveals unforeseen engineering and supply chain challenges that could drain resources before the first unit sells. The threat from incumbents is equally daunting; large companies possess the distribution networks and financial reserves to either undercut prices or rapidly replicate the cyclone technology once the concept is validated in the market. Without exclusive patents explicitly confirmed as unbreakable or a pre-existing retail partnership, the new company is vulnerable to being squeezed out immediately after proving the concept works.

The combination of no manufacturing history, a premium price point in a price-sensitive category, and the looming threat of copied innovation by giants with superior distribution makes this venture excessively risky in its current form. While the technology is sound, the go-to-market strategy does not adequately address how to overcome the incumbents' advantages or the consumer's reluctance to pay more initially. Proceeding now would likely result in rapid capital depletion without securing sufficient market share. The risks outweigh the potential rewards at this specific stage without further strategic planning, secured funding, or protected distribution channels.

DECISION: NO-GO
