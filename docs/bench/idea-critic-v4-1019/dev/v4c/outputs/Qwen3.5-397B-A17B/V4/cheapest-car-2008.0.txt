First call: GO. The arithmetic of moving families from two-wheelers to four-wheelers at a price point 40% below existing options creates a market where none existed, outweighing execution risks.

Steelman: By engineering a vehicle specifically for the "scooter-upgrade" demographic rather than competing with established sedans, the project unlocks a massive volume game that justifies a dedicated factory, provided safety meets minimum legal standards.

Alternatives: Doing nothing cedes the emerging mass-market to competitors. Selling a used car or scooter fails to offer the weather protection and status of a new four-seater. A higher-priced entry model misses the specific volume needed to amortize the new factory.

Fit: This aligns with the vision of motorizing a developing nation's middle class. The "cheapest" label is a feature, not a bug, for price-sensitive families.

Pre-mortem: Failure occurs if land protests halt construction indefinitely or if a high-profile safety incident validates quality fears, destroying brand trust before launch. This is a two-way door regarding marketing tweaks, but a one-way door on factory construction costs.

Evidence: The $2,500 price point is the core evidence; if the unit economics hold at this price with a margin, the volume potential is unprecedented. The risks regarding land and safety are real but manageable through site diversification and rigorous (publicized) crash testing.

Blockers: NONE. The farmer protests are a delay risk, not a fundamental flaw in the product-market fit. Safety concerns are addressed by adhering to regulations, not by abandoning the low-cost mandate.

Risks: Brand perception of "cheap" meaning "unsafe"; supply chain strain at high volumes; competitor price wars.

Kill Criteria: If the first pilot batch reveals safety defects exceeding regulatory limits, halt shipment. If land acquisition delays exceed 12 months, reassess the single-site strategy. Monitor monthly sales volume against the break-even point for the new factory.

DECISION: GO
