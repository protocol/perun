First call: NO-GO. The proposal relies on a tiny installed base, unproven demand for high-priced hardware in a category with poor sales, and an impossible supply chain timeline without existing vendor relationships.

Steelman: A premium, high-capacity device could redefine the category if it offers a seamless end-to-end experience that cheaper, fragmented competitors cannot match, forcing users to buy into the ecosystem.

However, the blocker is the addressable market and component sourcing. With a small computer market share and no consumer electronics experience, the volume required to justify a custom 5GB drive at consumer prices likely does not exist. Cheaper players have failed because the friction of acquiring music outweighs the benefit; this proposal adds a compatibility wall without solving the content problem (no legal downloads). The "seamless software" argument is weak when the software only runs on a minority of machines.

Alternatives:
1. Do nothing: Avoid capital burn on a likely inventory disaster.
2. License the tech: Partner with an existing manufacturer to reduce timeline risk (still fails on market size).
3. Software first: Solve the legal download/licensing issue before building hardware.

Pre-mortem: The device launches late due to sourcing delays, priced too high for early adopters, and compatible with too few machines to reach critical mass. Inventory sits unsold as competitors lower prices. The brand suffers a high-profile failure in a new category.

The risks here are not merely executional; they are foundational. The addressable market is too small to support the economics of a custom hardware supply chain, and the lack of a legal music ecosystem means the "premium experience" has no content to deliver. Without a path to expand compatibility or secure content, the unit economics do not work.

Blockers: Incompatible market size for custom hardware economics; no content ecosystem; unproven supply chain capability.
Risks: High price sensitivity; brand damage from failure.

DECISION: NO-GO
