First call: NO-GO. The proposal attacks an entrenched monopoly with a late, incompatible product while alienating the very partners (labels, device makers) required for content and distribution.

**Steelman:** A superior hardware experience and seamless wireless sharing can overcome late entry and ecosystem lock-in if the marketing budget converts non-users or steals high-value switchers.

**Attack:**
1.  **Fit & Incentives:** Dropping the industry copy-protection standard is not a differentiator; it is a deal-breaker. Labels will refuse to license content without the protection they demanded and the company previously championed. No content means no store; no store means the device is a glorified hard drive.
2.  **Alternatives:** "Do nothing" preserves partner trust. A "cheaper alternative" would be licensing the existing standard to enter the market faster, but the proposal explicitly rejects this.
3.  **Pre-mortem:** The device launches with a limited catalog because major labels block access due to the missing DRM. The "wireless sharing" feature becomes a liability for piracy rather than a selling point. The large marketing budget burns cash promoting a hollow ecosystem.
4.  **Evidence:** The proposal admits the leader has an "enormous share and loyal ecosystem" and that the company is "late." It offers no mechanism to force label cooperation other than hope, while actively removing their primary requirement (DRM).

**Blockers:**
*   **Content Availability:** The plan removes the copy-protection standard partners require. Without a confirmed exception from labels (not stated), the store cannot launch with sufficient inventory.
*   **Economic Logic:** Entering a winner-take-all market late, without a price advantage, without partner support, and with a conflicting technical standard creates a negative feedback loop immediately.

**Risks:** High marketing spend yielding low conversion; regulatory scrutiny on sharing features; hardware commoditization.

**Decision Method:** This fails the "must beat do nothing" test. The removal of the copy-protection standard contradicts the stated reality of label restrictions. A cheaper, staged approach would be to negotiate a DRM exception *before* committing to hardware production, or to license the existing standard. As proposed, the strategy guarantees a content-starved launch.

DECISION: NO-GO
