First call: NO-GO. The market is declared dead, retailers refuse stock, and the financial exposure from unlimited take-backs combined with no Western track record creates a catastrophic failure mode.

Steelman: Repositioning the hardware as a toy "Entertainment System" with non-game bundles (robot, gun) bypasses the "video game" stigma, while the lockout chip ensures quality control to prevent another crash, and the buyback guarantee forces reluctant retailers to take the risk.

Alternatives:
1. Do nothing: Zero cost, zero risk, but forfeits the market if the "fad" diagnosis is wrong.
2. Direct console launch: Certain rejection by retailers; immediate failure.
3. License to existing US publisher: Lower risk, but cedes control and brand identity; unlikely to succeed given the category toxicity.

Fit: Assumes the goal is market entry despite the crash. The "toy" angle is the only plausible bridge across the retailer blockade.

Pre-mortem: The venture fails because the take-back clause is exercised en masse due to lackluster consumer interest, bankrupting the local division, while the lockout chip triggers a developer boycott that starves the platform of software. The "toy" branding confuses consumers rather than enticing them.

Evidence Check: The proposal admits retailers are refusing stock and the company has no Western track record. The take-back promise is an uncapped liability against a product with unproven demand. The lockout chip creates an artificial scarcity of software in a market that already doubts the medium's value.

Blockers:
1. Financial Suicide: The unlimited take-back promise on a product retailers already deem unsellable creates a liability with no corresponding revenue guarantee.
2. Content Starvation: Aggressively limiting third-party publishers via lockout chips when the library size is critical to overcoming consumer skepticism contradicts the need for maximum software availability.

Risks: Brand confusion, high unit cost of robot peripheral, potential legal challenges on restraint of trade.

The financial exposure of the buyback guarantee without a proven sales mechanism is a fatal flaw. The restriction on developers further weakens the value proposition.

DECISION: NO-GO
