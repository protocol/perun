First call: NO-GO. The proposal commits irreversible capital before validating the core business model in a single market.

Steelman: Rapidly building a national footprint of automated warehouses creates an unassailable moat through scale and speed, deterring competitors before they can enter.

However, the claim fails the blocker test. The proposal explicitly admits the company has "no operating track record" and has not yet "learning the unit economics of the first" market. Groceries are defined as "low-margin," meaning small errors in density or logistics destroy profitability. Committing $1 billion to duplicate a potentially flawed model across a dozen cities before fixing the variables in the first is not strategy; it is gambling with capped upside (margins) and uncapped downside (total capital loss).

Alternatives:
1. **Do Nothing/Slow Down:** Perfect unit economics in the first city, then expand. Cost: Low. Risk: Competitors enter. Reversibility: High.
2. **Pilot Expansion:** Expand to 2-3 similar cities using existing infrastructure or smaller pilots. Cost: Medium. Risk: Moderate scaling issues. Reversibility: Medium.
3. **Proposal (Big Bang):** Build everywhere now. Cost: $1B+. Risk: Catastrophic failure if model is wrong. Reversibility: None (sunk cost in specialized automation).

The "Big Bang" approach does not beat "Do Nothing" because the stated lack of unit economic validation means the foundation is unproven. If the first city's model fails (e.g., delivery density is insufficient to cover costs), replicating that failure twelve times magnifies the loss rather than securing victory. The risk of "customer-density needs" cannot be solved by capital alone; it requires operational learning which this plan bypasses.

Blockers:
- Committing $1B without validated unit economics in the initial market.
- Zero operating track record combined with irreversible infrastructure spend.
- Low-margin industry structure punishes scaling unproven models.

Risks:
- Competitor entry (mitigated by speed, but irrelevant if the model loses money on every order).
- Capital inefficiency.

This is a one-way door decision with no evidence the door leads to profit. The plan must be revised to validate economics before scaling.

DECISION: NO-GO
