First call: NO-GO. The market is declared dead, retailers refuse stock, and the financial exposure from unlimited take-back promises combined with no local track record creates an unacceptable downside.

Steelman: Repositioning the console as an "Entertainment System" with toys and a strict quality lockout can bypass the "video game" stigma and restore retailer confidence through risk reversal.

However, the proposal fails the blocker test on financial structure. The "promise to take back unsold stock" is uncapped. In a market where retailers are actively clearing inventory and viewing the category as a fad, demand is near zero. An uncapped buyback guarantee in a collapsing market is not a safety net; it is a liability that could exceed the company's total capital if the launch fails. Without a specific cap on returns or a phased rollout to limit exposure, the math does not work. The risk of angering developers is secondary; the risk of immediate insolvency via returns is primary.

Alternatives:
1. Do nothing: Zero cost, zero risk, preserves capital for other markets.
2. Direct licensing only: Test the hardware with existing partners before full retail push.
3. Proposed plan: High upfront cost, unlimited downside liability, relies on changing consumer psychology instantly.

The pre-mortem is clear: The system launches, retailers order cautiously but the public does not bite. Retailers exercise the buyback clause en masse. The company is left with warehouses of obsolete "toys" and a massive cash deficit. The lockout chip prevents a software rescue by limiting third-party support when it is needed most to drive hardware adoption.

This is a one-way door with a potentially fatal cost of delay if it fails. The lack of a cap on the buyback promise is a factual blocker that renders the plan financially unsound as written. A capped pilot or a revised contract with limited liability would be required to proceed. As proposed, the downside is infinite while the upside is speculative.

Blockers: Uncapped financial liability on stock returns in a dead market.
Risks: Developer backlash, continued consumer apathy, high operational complexity.

DECISION: NO-GO
