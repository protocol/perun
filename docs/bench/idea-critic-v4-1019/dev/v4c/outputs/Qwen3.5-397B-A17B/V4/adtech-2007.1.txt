First call: NO-GO. The $6 billion cash price for a fast-changing technology asset, paid at a large premium to compete with a rival's similar move, signals an emotional reaction rather than a disciplined strategy.

Steelman: Acquiring the platform is the only viable path to rapidly scale display capabilities and prevent the search leader from monopolizing the ad-serving infrastructure, justifying the premium as a barrier-to-entry cost.

However, the proposal fails the blocker test. The primary blocker is the **strategic conflict with existing agency customers**. The buyer's core business relies on serving these agencies; owning a competing ad-serving platform creates an immediate, unresolvable channel conflict that threatens the existing revenue base. The proposal acknowledges this risk but offers no mechanism to mitigate it. In a B2B model, alienating the current customer base to chase a speculative B2C/display market is a fundamental strategic error.

Furthermore, the **valuation logic is flawed**. Paying a large premium for "fast-changing" technology is historically perilous; the asset risks obsolescence shortly after purchase, turning the $6 billion into a sunk cost with no defensive moat. The justification relies entirely on matching a competitor's move ("the search leader just agreed..."), which is a reactive stance, not a proactive value creation strategy. The "do nothing" or "build/buy smaller" alternatives are superior because they preserve cash and customer trust while allowing the market dynamics to clarify before committing capital.

The absence of a concrete plan to resolve the channel conflict or a capped, time-boxed pilot to validate the technology's longevity constitutes a fatal flaw. This is not a risk to be managed; it is a structural failure of the thesis as presented.

Blockers: Unmitigated conflict with existing agency customers; reactive premium pricing for depreciating tech.
Risks: Market shifts, integration failure, cultural clash.

DECISION: NO-GO
