First call: GO. The proposal addresses a critical vulnerability: commodity pricing driven by buyer ignorance. If buyers cannot distinguish the core component, the manufacturer loses pricing power to clone makers.

Steelman: By shifting demand pull to the end-user, the chip maker forces computer manufacturers to adopt their component to satisfy customer requests, effectively breaking the clone market's ability to compete on obscurity.

Alternatives considered:
1. Do nothing: Accept commoditization and margin erosion as clones proliferate.
2. Price cuts: Triggers a race to the bottom, destroying value for the leader.
3. Legal action against clones: Slow, expensive, and difficult given similar part numbers.
The proposed branding campaign is the only path to differentiate the product in the buyer's mind without engaging in destructive price wars.

Fit: Moves the north star from "unit sales to manufacturers" to "end-user preference," creating a moat against clones.

Pre-mortem: Failure occurs if computer makers unite to boycott the co-op advertising, viewing it as interference. However, the financial incentive (paid advertising) makes refusal irrational for any single maker fearing loss of market share to competitors who do accept. The cost is high, but the cost of inaction (total commoditization) is higher.

Evidence: The proposal correctly identifies that buyers currently do not know what chip they own. This information asymmetry is the root cause of the threat. The mechanism (co-op advertising) aligns incentives: manufacturers get cheaper ads; the chip maker gets brand placement.

Blockers: NONE. The risks cited (unprecedented nature, resentment, budget share) are execution challenges, not factual contradictions. Unprecedented moves often yield the highest returns in shifting markets. Resentment is mitigated by the subsidy. Budget share is justified by the defense of the core business model.

Risks: High spend with delayed ROI; potential channel friction. These are managed by setting clear kill criteria (e.g., lift in named requests within 6 months) rather than blocking the initiative.

This is a classic two-way door experiment with one-way upside. If it fails, spending stops. If it succeeds, it defines the category. The logic holds: make the invisible visible to create demand pull.

DECISION: GO
