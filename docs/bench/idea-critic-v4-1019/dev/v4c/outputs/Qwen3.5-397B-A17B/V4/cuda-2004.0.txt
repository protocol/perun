First call: NO-GO. The proposal asks to raise unit costs and divert engineering focus from a fierce core rivalry to chase a non-existent mass market, violating basic survival logic for a single-product company.

Steelman: Embedding general-purpose compute capability in every consumer chip creates a ubiquitous hardware base that could unlock a massive parallel processing market once software matures, securing a decade-long moat against rivals focused solely on graphics.

However, the blocker is fatal under the stated facts. The company's revenue comes *almost entirely* from gaming and workstations, where it is locked in a "core race" with a main rival. In such a duel, diverting engineering resources to a feature with "no large paying market" and raising unit costs on every SKU is suicidal. The competitor will undercut on price or outperform on graphics fidelity, starving the proposer of the very cash flow needed to fund the multi-year program. The proposal admits there are "no committed customers" and demand is "niche." Betting the entire company's margin structure on a hypothetical future while losing the present war is not strategy; it is gambling with existence.

Alternatives:
1. **Do nothing:** Maintain focus on the graphics race, preserve margins, and let academics continue hacking APIs until a market proves itself. Cost: Low. Risk: Missing the wave. Reversibility: N/A.
2. **Targeted High-End Only:** Add features only to expensive workstation cards where unit cost impact is negligible and scientific users actually buy. Cost: Medium. Risk: Slower adoption. Reversibility: High.
3. **Proposed Plan:** Subsidize R&D via every consumer sale. Cost: High (margin erosion). Risk: Losing core market share. Reversibility: Low (silicon is permanent).

The plan fails the "beat do nothing" test because the cheaper alternative (High-End Only) captures the initial scientific upside without jeopardizing the core mass-market war. The stated facts confirm the market does not exist yet; therefore, the cost must not be borne by the mass market.

Blockers: Raising unit costs and diverting engineering focus during a head-to-head rivalry with no committed revenue source threatens immediate market share loss to the rival.
Risks: Niche demand remains niche; software ecosystem fails to materialize.

DECISION: NO-GO
