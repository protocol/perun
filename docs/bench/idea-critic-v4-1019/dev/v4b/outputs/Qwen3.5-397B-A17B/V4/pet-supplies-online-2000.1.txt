First call: NO-GO. The plan relies on selling products below cost to acquire customers while simultaneously funding expensive TV ads and acquisitions; with negative gross margins and high shipping costs, every additional sale deepens the loss, making the business unviable regardless of capital availability.

Steelman: Aggressively capture dominant market share through a "blitzscale" strategy, using IPO proceeds to subsidize prices and outspend rivals on marketing and logistics, betting that future monopoly power will allow margin recovery once competitors are eliminated.

Options:
1. Do nothing: Conserve cash, fix unit economics, and grow organically; lower risk, slower growth.
2. Private funding round: Raise capital without public market scrutiny to test pricing strategies; preserves flexibility.
3. Proposed plan: Maximum aggression; highest upside if markets stay open and unit economics flip, but immediate insolvency risk if they don't.

Fit: Assumes the "growth at all costs" north star outweighs solvency. However, selling below cost with negative gross margins contradicts the fundamental requirement of eventual profitability.

Pre-mortem: The IPO window slams shut due to market volatility or poor earnings disclosures regarding negative margins. The company burns through cash on TV ads and below-cost sales, unable to raise follow-on funding. Competitors with deeper pockets or better unit economics survive the price war. The company collapses under debt and inventory costs before achieving dominance.

Evidence: The proposal admits to "negative gross margins," "costly to ship," and "high customer-acquisition cost." Selling below cost exacerbates these known deficits. No evidence suggests unit economics will improve simply due to scale; historically, shipping heavy, low-margin goods rarely achieves positive contribution margins through volume alone.

Blockers:
1. Negative unit economics: Selling below cost while incurring high shipping and acquisition costs guarantees that increased revenue leads to increased losses. No path to profitability is demonstrated.
2. Capital dependency: The plan requires continuous access to public capital markets. If markets close (a stated risk), the company cannot fund its operating losses or the proposed expansion, leading to immediate bankruptcy.

Risks: Competitor retaliation, inability to integrate acquisitions, operational complexity of new warehouses.

The plan fails the basic test of solvency. Expanding a loss-making model with borrowed money is not a strategy; it is a liquidation event waiting for a trigger.

DECISION: NO-GO
