First call: NO-GO. The proposal commits massive, certain capital to build a market that does not exist, while the company fights a life-or-death race in its core business with zero committed revenue to offset the risk.

**Steelman:** Betting that general-purpose GPU computing will become a massive industry justifies diverting engineering from the core gaming war and adding cost to every unit now to secure a decade-long lead.

**Attack:**
1.  **Alternatives:** "Do nothing" preserves cash and engineering focus for the existential gaming rivalry. A "wait-and-see" approach allows the company to adopt programmability only after academic hacking proves a viable commercial path, avoiding the cost of building a compiler and toolchain for a niche audience.
2.  **Fit:** The company's survival depends on winning the gaming market. Diverting silicon area and top engineering talent to a feature with no paying customers directly undermines the primary north star: beating the main rival in graphics performance and price.
3.  **Pre-mortem:** The program burns cash for years; the gaming rival releases faster, cheaper chips; the company loses market share; the new computing division fails to find customers because the hardware was optimized for hypothetical rather than actual workloads. The company goes bankrupt or is acquired.
4.  **Evidence:** The proposal admits "no large paying market exists" and "no committed customers." The cost is immediate and universal (every unit); the benefit is speculative and distant.

**Blockers:**
*   **Resource Conflict:** The engineering effort required for a full compiler and new chip architecture directly conflicts with the "core race" against the main rival. Losing the core market means no company remains to enjoy the future upside.
*   **Economic Failure:** Raising unit costs on all products to serve a non-existent market violates basic unit economics without a guaranteed off-take agreement.

**Risks:** Niche demand remains niche; academic experiments do not translate to enterprise sales; the "hacked APIs" suffice for early adopters without custom silicon.

The proposal asks to gamble the company's present survival on a future that is not yet proven. The correct move is to monitor academic progress and integrate specific features only when demand is quantifiable, not to fund the entire ecosystem upfront.

DECISION: NO-GO
