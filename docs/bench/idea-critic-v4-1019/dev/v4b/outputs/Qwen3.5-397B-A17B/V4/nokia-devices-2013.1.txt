First call: NO-GO. Buying a declining hardware unit to solve a software ecosystem deficit rarely works; the cost of integration usually outweighs the benefit of controlling a shrinking installed base.

Steelman: Acquiring the handset maker secures the primary reference device, prevents an immediate defection to the rival platform, and signals commitment to developers.
Attack: The core failure mode is not hardware supply but app availability. Developers follow users; users follow apps. This acquisition swaps a cash burn for a hardware business with falling sales, diverting €5.4B and management focus from the actual lever: making the OS attractive enough that developers port apps regardless of who builds the phone.

Options:
1. Do nothing: Risk the manufacturer defects, accelerating decline.
2. Acquire (Proposed): High cost, high integration risk, unproven fix for ecosystem.
3. Subsidize/Partner: Pay the manufacturer exclusively to stay on the OS without owning the liability. Cheaper, reversible, same strategic outcome (preventing defection).

Fit: If the north star is ecosystem scale, owning a low-share, falling hardware line is a distraction. It creates a conflict of interest with other handset partners, potentially pushing them toward the rival platform—a catastrophic second-order effect.

Pre-mortem: Two years post-acquisition, the division is still losing money, other manufacturers have abandoned the OS fearing competition from the owner, and the app gap remains unchanged because total market share didn't grow. The company is stuck with a hardware turnaround it didn't want.

Evidence: The proposal admits smartphone sales are falling and the rival has the apps. No evidence suggests ownership reverses developer sentiment faster than partnership.

Blockers:
1. Strategic contradiction: Owning a competitor alienates other hardware partners, likely shrinking the total OS footprint further.
2. Misdiagnosis: Hardware ownership does not solve the "chicken-and-egg" app problem; it merely verticalizes a losing position.

Risks: Integration failure, continued cash burn, partner exodus.

The cheaper, safer alternative (exclusive partnership/subsidy) achieves the "no defection" goal without the baggage. As proposed, this bet conflates control with success.

DECISION: NO-GO
