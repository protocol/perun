First call: GO. The request comes from the largest customer with a clear economic driver (cost per seat) in a growing market, despite massive scale.

Steelman: Building a 2.5x larger wide-body with a freighter-convertible design secures the dominant market position against future supersonic shifts and locks in the key customer.

Alternatives:
1. Do nothing: Cede the high-capacity segment to competitors or lose the key customer as demand outstrips current supply.
2. Supersonic only: Ignores immediate subsonic growth and economic reality; high risk of delayed viability.
3. Stretch current model: Insufficient to meet the 2.5x capacity requirement or desired cost reductions.

Fit: Aligns with the vision of market dominance and serving the largest operators. The freighter conversion feature hedges against the supersonic risk.

Pre-mortem: Failure occurs if the new engine fails certification, the factory bankrupts the firm before delivery, or supersonic jets render subsonic giants obsolete within five years. This is a one-way door with immense delay costs; waiting allows competitors to capture the hub-and-spoke future.

Evidence: The proposal relies on the stated commitment from the largest airline and the logical projection of travel growth. The "unproven" engine is a necessary partnership risk, not a blocker, provided the supplier is committed. The supersonic threat is speculative compared to immediate subsonic demand.

Blockers: NONE. The risks cited (cost, engine, supersonic competition, single customer) are severe but inherent to the opportunity's scale. They do not factually contradict the core claim that this specific aircraft meets a unique, large-scale market need that no other option addresses. The single customer is the *largest* airline, providing a viable initial base.

Risks: Catastrophic financial overreach, engine development failure, market shift to supersonic. These are managed by the freighter fallback and the sheer magnitude of the unmet demand.

The potential upside of defining the next era of aviation outweighs the existential risks, which are unavoidable if the company wishes to remain the leader.

DECISION: GO
