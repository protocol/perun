First call: NO-GO. The plan targets a tiny installed base with a premium price in a category where cheaper options already struggle, while ignoring the lack of legal content.

Steelman: A high-capacity, integrated device could define a new standard for ease of use, leveraging tight hardware-software control to solve the "it just works" problem that fragmented PC players fail to address.

However, the blockers are fatal under the stated facts. First, the addressable market is self-limiting to owners of a niche computer brand; without Windows compatibility or standalone file transfer, the volume required to justify custom component sourcing at a $400 price point cannot materialize. Second, the value proposition collapses without a legal music ecosystem; a 5GB drive is a solution in search of a problem if users cannot legally fill it easily. Cheaper competitors have already proven that hardware alone does not drive adoption in this vacuum.

Alternatives: Doing nothing preserves capital. Licensing the software to Windows would expand the base but contradicts the "own computers only" constraint. Waiting for the music industry to license downloads reduces the content risk but misses the "under a year" window and cedes hardware ground.

Pre-mortem: The device launches to critical acclaim for design but sells poorly because the target audience is too small to sustain the supply chain, and the lack of a legal store makes the high price unjustifiable compared to CD players or cheaper, albeit clunkier, MP3 players. The company is stuck with unsold inventory and a reputation for overpriced niche gadgets.

Blockers: (1) The "own computers only" restriction creates a total addressable market too small to support the economics of a custom hardware launch at $400. (2) No legal download ecosystem exists to populate the device, rendering the storage capacity moot for the average consumer.

Risks: Component sourcing delays, brand dilution if it fails, rapid obsolescence by flash memory.

The plan fails because it relies on a walled garden that is currently too small to sustain the business case, ignoring the primary friction point: content availability.

DECISION: NO-GO
