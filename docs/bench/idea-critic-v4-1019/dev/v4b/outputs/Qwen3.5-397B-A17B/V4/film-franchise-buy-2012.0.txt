First call: NO-GO. The proposal relies on a creative engine (the founder) that is explicitly leaving, while recent output quality is declining, making the $4B price tag for future trilogy value highly speculative.

Steelman: Acquiring this franchise secures a complete, beloved universe capable of sustaining theme parks and merchandise for decades, justifying the premium if the new trilogy succeeds.

Attack: The core value proposition collapses if the new films fail. With the founder retiring and recent entries receiving mixed reviews, the probability of creative success is unproven and likely low. The proposal admits fans may reject new entries, yet offers no mitigation other than "produce a trilogy." If the films flop, the $4B investment yields little synergy, as the "universe" appeal is tied to specific creative voices now departing. Furthermore, with much merchandise value already licensed out, immediate revenue upside is capped, delaying ROI.

Alternatives:
1. Do nothing: Avoids $4B risk; capital remains available for organic growth or safer acquisitions.
2. License only: Secure theme park and merchandising rights without the $4B acquisition cost or film production risk.
3. Staged buy: Option the franchise with a smaller upfront payment, tying the bulk of the $4B to the box office performance of the first new film.

Pre-mortem: The trilogy releases to poor reviews and fan backlash. Box office fails to recoup production and marketing costs. The stock price dips due to the write-down. The theme park expansion based on the IP underperforms because the cultural moment has passed. The company is left with a $4B hole and no creative leadership to fix it.

Blockers:
1. Creative Vacuum: The founder's retirement removes the primary driver of the franchise's past success, and recent mixed reviews indicate the IP cannot sustain itself without that specific vision.
2. Valuation Mismatch: The $4B price assumes successful future films despite explicit evidence of declining quality and fan reception risks.

Risks: Fan rejection, licensing saturation, high integration costs.

The absence of a plan to replace the creative lead or structure the deal against film performance makes this an unjustifiable gamble at the proposed price.

DECISION: NO-GO
