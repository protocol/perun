First call: GO. The market collapse was driven by retailer distrust and product saturation, not a lack of consumer demand. This proposal directly addresses the root causes: rebranding bypasses the "video game" stigma, the buy-back guarantee removes retailer financial risk, and the lockout chip prevents the quality collapse that destroyed the previous market.

Steelman: By positioning the hardware as a toy/entertainment system and absorbing inventory risk, the company can re-enter a hostile market where competitors cannot, while the lockout chip ensures software quality to rebuild consumer trust.

Alternatives like waiting for the market to heal or launching as a standard console fail because they do not solve the immediate retailer blockade or the perception of games as a fad. The "do nothing" option cedes the entire North American territory to competitors once the sentiment shifts.

The pre-mortem failure scenario is that developers revolt against the lockout chip, starving the platform of content. However, the take-back promise is the critical lever that forces retailer participation despite this risk. Without it, no shelf space exists. The risk of returns is real but manageable compared to the certainty of zero distribution without the guarantee. The bundling with non-game toys (robot, light gun) reinforces the "entertainment system" narrative, differentiating it from the failed consoles of 1983.

Blockers: NONE. The proposal's specific constraints (lockout, buy-back, rebranding) are the exact mechanisms required to overcome the stated market conditions. The risks cited (developer anger, return costs) are operational hurdles, not factual contradictions of the strategy.

Risks: Developer relations strain; high cost of unsold returns if the product fails to gain traction despite the incentives. These are mitigated by the high margin potential of a monopolized software library via the lockout chip.

Kill criteria: If retailer adoption is below 40% of targeted chains within 6 months despite the buy-back offer, or if third-party software releases drop below 5 titles in the first year due to lockout friction, pivot to a more open licensing model or withdraw. Leading metric: Number of retail units placed and sell-through rate per month.

DECISION: GO
