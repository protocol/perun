First call: NO-GO. The price is extreme relative to revenue, the core user base hates the proposed monetization method, and the buyer has a proven inability to integrate acquisitions without destroying value.

Steelman: Acquiring the platform at a premium is justified only if independence can be strictly maintained to preserve culture while slowly introducing non-intrusive ads, leveraging the portal's cash to scale faster than organic growth allows.

Alternatives: Doing nothing avoids immediate capital destruction but accepts slow irrelevance. Building a native youth product is cheaper and safer but slower. Buying a smaller, more compatible competitor offers better integration odds. The "do nothing" or "build" options likely offer better risk-adjusted returns than a $1.1B gamble on a hostile integration.

Fit: This assumes the portal can resist the urge to integrate, contradicting its own history of struggling with past acquisitions. The strategy relies on a behavioral change in the acquirer that has no evidence of occurring.

Pre-mortem: The portal immediately imposes branding and ad standards; the founder quits; the youth audience migrates to a new competitor within 18 months; the $1.1B is written down as goodwill impairment. This is a one-way door with a high cost of delay only if no other entry point to the youth market exists.

Evidence: The proposal itself admits the price is high against revenue, users dislike ads, content is hard to sell, and integration has historically failed. These are not risks; they are stated facts that directly contradict the success conditions.

Blockers:
1. **Economic Mismatch**: The monetization strategy (ads) is antithetical to the asset's value driver (user experience that dislikes ads).
2. **Execution History**: The acquirer's stated track record of failed integrations makes the "keep it independent" promise implausible.
3. **Valuation**: Paying a massive premium for an entity with little revenue and high churn risk violates basic capital allocation discipline.

Risks: Founder departure, brand toxicity, regulatory scrutiny on content.

The stated facts confirm the plan fails as proposed: the buyer cannot monetize the asset without destroying it, and history suggests they will try.

DECISION: NO-GO
