First call: NO-GO. The proposal commits to a fixed public schedule for unproven core technologies without a demand signal or regulatory mandate, creating massive downside with no immediate upside.

**Steelman:** A bold, fixed-schedule launch of a novel hybrid powertrain by 1997 will secure first-mover dominance and define the future standard before competitors react.

**Attack:**
1.  **Technology Risk:** Nickel-metal-hydride durability is unproven in volume production. A fixed 1997 deadline forces shipping immature batteries, guaranteeing catastrophic warranty exposure and brand damage if they fail early.
2.  **Market Misalignment:** With cheap fuel and no mandates, consumers have zero financial incentive to pay a premium for unproven hybrid tech. There is no near-term demand signal to justify mass production costs.
3.  **Strategic Inflexibility:** A "bold public schedule" removes the option to delay for technical fixes. If the transmission or battery lags, the company must either miss the public deadline (reputation hit) or ship a flawed product (liability hit).
4.  **Better Way:** A "do nothing" stance on mass production preserves capital. A superior alternative is a limited technical pilot or fleet-only trial to validate battery life without consumer-facing risk, decoupling R&D from a hard public launch date.

**Pre-mortem:** It is 1998. The cars are recalled due to battery degradation after 18 months. Fuel prices remain low, so no one bought the car voluntarily. The company faces billions in warranty claims and is labeled the manufacturer of unreliable "experimental" vehicles.

**Blockers:**
*   **Unproven Core Tech:** Battery durability is unknown; a fixed mass-production date ignores this uncertainty.
*   **No Demand:** Cheap fuel and no regulations mean the market cannot absorb the cost premium.
*   **Fixed Schedule:** Public commitment prevents necessary technical delays, forcing a choice between failure and delay.

**Risks:** High R&D sunk cost, competitor leapfrogging if tech matures slower than expected, supply chain strain.

**Kill Criteria:** Do not proceed to mass production until battery cycle life exceeds 100,000 miles in verified testing AND a regulatory mandate or fuel price shock creates a clear demand signal.

DECISION: NO-GO
