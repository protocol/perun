First call: NO-GO. The proposal conflates a communication device with a dedicated gaming console, creating a hybrid that fails at both primary functions while costing significantly more than the market leader.

Steelman: A converged device could leverage existing mobile distribution to bypass cartridge logistics, offering casual gamers a "good enough" library in a device they already carry.

However, the specific execution described fails the blocker test. The requirement to remove the battery to change games is a fundamental usability flaw that breaks the core loop of gaming (quick swapping) and telephony (constant connectivity). This is not a minor friction point; it renders the device operationally broken for its secondary function. Furthermore, entering a market dominated by a competitor with a massive library and lower price point ($300 vs. leader's lower cost) without publisher commitment is a strategic suicide pact. The "better way" is to license games to existing phones or wait for digital distribution maturity, rather than building expensive, awkward hardware that alienates both gamers (due to library size and swap mechanics) and callers (due to ergonomics).

The pre-mortem is clear: The device launches, critics mock the battery-swap mechanic, gamers reject the small library and high price, and the manufacturer retreats with unsold inventory. The cost of delay is negligible compared to the cost of launching a flawed product; waiting for digital distribution or better battery technology solves the primary mechanical blockers.

Blockers:
1. Mechanical failure: Removing the battery to swap games makes the device unusable for its primary purpose (calling) during gaming sessions and frustrates the gaming loop.
2. Economic mismatch: $300 price point exceeds the market leader's dedicated console without offering superior value or library depth.
3. Supply chain gap: Lack of publisher commitment ensures a weak launch library, dooming adoption.

Risks: Ergonomic awkwardness, brand dilution, inventory write-offs.

This plan attempts to force a convergence before the technology (battery life, digital storage) and market (publisher buy-in) are ready. The specific constraint of battery removal is a hard stop.

DECISION: NO-GO
