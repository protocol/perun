First call: NO-GO. The proposal targets a mass home market but requires hardware ("high-end PC") that the majority of that market does not own, creating an immediate addressable market failure.

Steelman: A cartoon house interface could reduce intimidation for novices if the hardware barrier didn't exclude the primary audience.

Alternatives:
1. Do nothing: Wait for the upcoming major OS release which likely addresses interface modernization natively without extra cost or hardware requirements.
2. Software-only overlay: Build a simpler shell running on existing standard PCs, sacrificing high-fidelity graphics for reach.
3. The Proposal: Fails because it demands new hardware purchases for a $100 software experiment.

Fit: The strategy aims to help "many new home users," yet the hardware requirement restricts it to a tiny fraction of users. This contradicts the north star of broad adoption.

Pre-mortem: The product launches, but 90% of target customers cannot run it. Those who can are either experienced users who find it patronizing or early adopters waiting for the imminent major OS update. Sales flatline; the brand looks out of touch.

Evidence & Math: The proposal admits "most home PCs" lack the memory. Selling a $100 product requiring a hardware upgrade (costing hundreds more) to a price-sensitive "home user" segment is economically unsound. The "major new operating-system release" coming in months renders a stop-gap, high-cost shell obsolete before it gains traction.

Blockers:
1. Hardware Mismatch: The core requirement (high-end PC) excludes the stated target audience (many new home users who presumably have standard or older machines).
2. Imminent Obsolescence: A major OS update with a new interface arrives in months, making a $100 third-party shell a hard sell against a free/bundled native update.
3. Market Fit: The "patronizing" risk for experienced users combined with the hardware barrier leaves no viable segment large enough to sustain development.

Risks: Low third-party add-on support reinforces the platform's weakness; high development cost for graphics vs. low potential revenue.

The plan fails on its own stated facts regarding hardware availability and competitive timing. No amount of interface charm overcomes the inability of the target market to run the software or the impending native competition.

DECISION: NO-GO
