First call: NO-GO. The core value proposition (reliable handwriting recognition) is explicitly stated as unproven in testing, while the price is high and the form factor compromises the primary use case (portability).

Steelman: Launching now establishes category ownership and brand leadership before competitors, betting that publicity can overcome early technical flaws and that software will follow hardware adoption.

Options:
1. **Do nothing:** Avoid financial loss but cede potential category leadership; safe but passive.
2. **Delay launch:** Wait for recognition accuracy to improve and size to shrink; reduces product risk but increases chance of competitor entry.
3. **Launch as proposed:** High cost, high risk of reputation damage if the "unreliable" input fails in public, potentially killing the category before it starts.

Fit: Aligns with a vision of leading a new category, but the current execution contradicts the functional requirement of a "personal" assistant that works reliably. A device that fails its primary input method cannot lead.

Pre-mortem: The device launches to heavy hype; users immediately encounter unreliable handwriting recognition. Reviews trash the $700 product as a gimmick. The category is branded as "not ready," causing a collapse in demand that prevents future iterations from succeeding. This is a one-way door for brand reputation regarding this specific category.

Evidence: The proposal itself admits recognition is "unreliable in testing." No evidence suggests this will magically resolve upon shipping. The price ($700 in 1993) is a significant barrier for an unproven utility.

Blockers:
1. **Core functionality failure:** The primary input method is admitted to be unreliable. Shipping a product where the main feature does not work is a fundamental defect that marketing cannot fix.
2. **Form factor mismatch:** Being "too big for a pocket" negates the "handheld/personal" value proposition for a mass market.

Risks: Lack of software, unproven category, high price. These are secondary to the fact that the device does not work as intended.

The plan fails the basic test of delivering a functional core promise. Reliability must be achieved before a mass-market launch at a premium price point.

DECISION: NO-GO
