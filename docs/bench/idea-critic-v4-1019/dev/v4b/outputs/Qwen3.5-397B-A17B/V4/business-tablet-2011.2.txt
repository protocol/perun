First call: NO-GO. Launching a business-centric device without native email, calendar, or contacts directly contradicts the company's core strength and value proposition, especially when targeting the same enterprise users who rely on these functions daily.

Steelman: The company bets that its new operating system and phone-pairing ecosystem will eventually lure developers and users away from the rival's mature app store, offering a unique, integrated experience that justifies the lack of standalone productivity apps initially.

However, the attack reveals fatal flaws. First, fit is broken: a "business email leader" releasing a tablet without built-in email is strategically incoherent. It cedes the only ground where the company currently wins to a rival with a superior app ecosystem. Second, the pre-mortem is clear: enterprise IT departments will reject a device requiring a specific phone pairing for basic PIM functions, leading to zero adoption in the target segment. Third, the economics fail: matching the price of a larger, feature-complete rival tablet with a smaller, crippled device offers no value advantage to offset the risk of an untested OS and sparse app store.

The "cheaper alternative" of waiting to integrate native apps or lowering the price significantly is not just better; it is mandatory for survival. Shipping now guarantees a product that fails its primary pitch (business) while offering no compensatory advantage in consumer features or price. The risk of developers not committing is compounded by launching a device with no immediate utility for its stated audience.

Blockers:
1. The product lacks the core functionality (native email/calendar) required to support its primary marketing pitch (business leadership), creating an immediate contradiction that cannot be overcome by pairing requirements.
2. Pricing matches a superior competitor (larger screen, full app store, native apps) without a differentiating value proposition, ensuring market rejection.

Risks: Untested OS stability, developer apathy, brand dilution in the enterprise sector.

The plan fails on its own stated terms: it attempts to sell a business tool that cannot perform basic business tasks independently.

DECISION: NO-GO
