First call: NO-GO. The proposal bets the entire company on a single customer's forecast while ignoring a looming technological shift (supersonic flight) and an unproven core component (engine).

Steelman: Building a massively larger, convertible freighter jet now secures market dominance for decades by locking in the primary carrier and creating a dual-use asset before competitors can react.

Options:
1. **Do nothing:** Maintain current production; risk losing the key customer to a rival willing to take the gamble.
2. **Supersonic focus:** Pivot R&D entirely to next-gen speed, abandoning the size race.
3. **Proposed plan:** Massive capital outlay for a subsonic giant with unproven engines.
The proposal fails to demonstrate why the "size" bet beats the "speed" bet given the industry expectation of supersonic replacement.

Fit: Assumes subsonic travel remains the volume driver. If supersonic jets become viable for mass transit quickly, this factory becomes a stranded asset.

Pre-mortem: The project fails because the new engine program delays delivery by years, draining cash reserves. Simultaneously, supersonic prototypes succeed, rendering the subsonic giant obsolete before it recoups costs. The single customer cancels due to market shifts, leaving no other buyers for such a specialized, expensive plane. This is a one-way door; once the factory is built, there is no exit.

Evidence: The proposal admits the engine is "unproven" and the outlay is "far larger than any... made." It relies on a single order. The risk of supersonic replacement is stated as an expectation, not a dismissed possibility.

Blockers:
1. **Unproven core technology:** Committing to production before the high-bypass engine is validated creates an unacceptable schedule and performance risk.
2. **Technological obsolescence:** The stated expectation that supersonic airliners will replace subsonic jets directly threatens the asset's long-term viability.
3. **Single-customer dependency:** The financial scale of the project cannot be supported by one airline; without broader market validation, the risk of default is catastrophic.

Risks: Cost overruns, supersonic acceleration, freighter conversion complexity.

The absence of a proven engine and the credible threat of supersonic displacement are fatal flaws in the current plan. The company cannot survive a failure of this magnitude without de-risking the engine and the market segment first.

DECISION: NO-GO
