First call: GO. The market gap for an affordable, lightweight roadster is clear, and the proposed specs directly address the void left by discontinued models.

Steelman: A mid-size manufacturer can profitably capture a neglected niche by leveraging existing front-engine, rear-drive components to create a low-cost, emotionally compelling two-seater, accepting limited volume for high margin and brand halo.

Options analysis shows "do nothing" cedes the segment entirely. A heavier, four-seat alternative destroys the "lightweight roadster" value proposition. A premium sports car conflicts with the "affordable" constraint. The proposed path is the only one fitting the specific market gap.

Fit: Assumes the company's north star includes brand revitalization and margin improvement over pure volume. The proposal moves this by targeting unserved demand.

Pre-mortem: Failure occurs if safety compliance inflates costs beyond "affordable," or if the lack of heritage prevents enthusiast adoption. However, these are execution risks, not fundamental flaws. The door is two-way; production can be capped or halted if early sales miss targets. Cost of delay allows competitors to fill the void or consumer interest to atrophy further.

Evidence: The proposal states the segment has shrunk and mass-market makers have exited. This confirms the lack of competition but validates the "dead niche" fear. However, the explicit mention of "disappeared" models suggests pent-up demand rather than zero demand. No internal data contradicts the feasibility of using existing drivetrains.

Blockers: NONE. The risks cited (shrinking segment, safety costs, no heritage) are known industry conditions, not new facts that invalidate the plan. They are mitigated by the "affordable" and "lightweight" constraints which lower the break-even point.

Risks: Sales fading after early adopters; regulatory cost creep. These become kill criteria: if initial sales do not sustain a second production year, or if compliance costs exceed 15% of the target price, the project stops.

The plan is sound because it exploits a competitor retreat with a low-overhead product. The risks are manageable via strict production caps.

DECISION: GO
