First call: NO-GO. Buying a shrinking hardware unit to solve a software ecosystem deficit is a classic trap; it consumes capital on a depreciating asset while the core problem (lack of apps) remains untouched.

Steelman: Vertical integration is the only lever left to prevent the manufacturer's defection to the rival platform, securing the OS's sole remaining significant hardware partner.

Options:
1. **Do nothing:** High risk of manufacturer switching to the rival, likely killing the OS immediately.
2. **Subsidize the partner:** Pay the manufacturer to stay loyal without owning them; cheaper, reversible, but less control.
3. **The Proposal:** Acquire the division. High cost (€5.4B), low reversibility, inherits falling sales and integration chaos.

Fit: This assumes hardware ownership fixes app scarcity. History suggests otherwise; developers follow users, not corporate org charts. If sales are already falling due to the app gap, owning the factory accelerates cash burn without solving the root cause.

Pre-mortem: The acquisition closes, but the app gap widens because the OS share remains low. The company is now stuck with a bleeding hardware division, alienated other handset partners who fear competition, and a €5.4B hole in the balance sheet, forcing a retreat from the mobile market entirely.

Evidence: The proposal admits smartphone sales are falling and the rival has "most of the apps." No evidence is presented that ownership reverses developer sentiment. The cost of delay is losing the manufacturer, but the cost of action is potentially losing the entire company's liquidity on a failing bet.

Blockers:
1. **Causal Mismatch:** The core failure mode is an app ecosystem deficit, not a lack of hardware control. Buying hardware does not generate apps; it merely changes who owns the declining distribution channel.
2. **Partner Alienation:** Other handset makers running this OS will likely flee to the rival platform to avoid competing with the OS owner, shrinking the total install base further.

Risks: Massive integration overhead, continued sales decline, capital misallocation.

This is a one-way door into a shrinking market with the wrong solution. The capital is better spent aggressively subsidizing developers or pivoting strategy than buying a legacy hardware burden.

DECISION: NO-GO
