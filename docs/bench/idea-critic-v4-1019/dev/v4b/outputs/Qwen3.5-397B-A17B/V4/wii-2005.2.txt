First call: GO. The market leader and runner-up are locked in a costly arms race for high-definition graphics and hardcore gamers, leaving the casual and family segment underserved and price-sensitive.

Steelman: By abandoning the spec war, the console maker can drastically reduce hardware costs, price the unit below competitors, and unlock a massive "blue ocean" of non-gamers through intuitive motion controls, making profitability possible via software volume rather than hardware margins.

Alternatives:
1. Do nothing: Remain a distant third, fighting a losing battle on graphics power with shrinking margins.
2. Compete on specs: Attempt to match rivals' HD power; likely results in a more expensive, inferior product that fails to differentiate.
3. The proposal: Differentiate on interaction and price, targeting a new demographic entirely.

Fit: This aligns with a strategy of market expansion rather than share stealing. It assumes the "non-gamer" is a viable, monetizable audience—a risk, but one supported by the low entry price.

Pre-mortem: Failure occurs if third-party publishers refuse to support the unusual architecture, leaving a content drought, or if the motion gimmick fades quickly, branding the console as a toy. However, the low hardware cost provides a wider runway than a failed HD launch. The door is two-way; if the casual bet fails, the company can pivot or exit with lower sunk costs than a spec-war failure.

Evidence: The proposal relies on the observed saturation of the hardcore market and the success of simple, pick-up-and-play arcade concepts. The thin margin is a feature, not a bug, enabling the low price point required to reach the mass market.

Blockers: NONE. No factual contradiction exists in the proposal; the risks are executional, not fundamental flaws in the thesis.
Risks: Third-party adoption, perception as a toy, unproven controller ergonomics. These are managed by the low capital requirement and first-party software focus.

The potential upside of expanding the total addressable market vastly outweighs the risk of continuing a losing war on graphics. The low cost structure creates a unique advantage that rivals cannot copy without cannibalizing their own high-margin businesses.

DECISION: GO
