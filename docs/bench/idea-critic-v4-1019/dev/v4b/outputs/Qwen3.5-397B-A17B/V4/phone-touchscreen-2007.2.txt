First call: GO. The proposal attacks the core vulnerability (music player erosion) by extending the company's strongest asset (user experience) into a category where incumbents are constrained by carrier control and fragmented software.

Steelman: Launch a premium, keyboard-less touchscreen device that integrates music, web, and email, secured by an exclusive carrier partnership that cedes design control and shares service revenue, thereby bypassing industry norms to deliver a superior user experience.

Options analysis:
1.  **Do nothing:** Accept gradual erosion of music player sales as competitors bundle mediocrity; lose relevance in the emerging mobile computing era.
2.  **License software:** Retain reach but surrender hardware/software integration, resulting in a fragmented experience indistinguishable from existing smartphones.
3.  **Proposed path:** Sacrifice initial volume for total control over the user experience and a new recurring revenue stream. This is the only option that leverages the company's specific competitive advantage.

Fit: Aligns with the north star of integrating hardware and software to solve user friction. The lack of a keyboard is a feature, not a bug, enabling a larger display for the stated web and music goals.

Pre-mortem: Failure occurs if the carrier rejects the revenue-share model or if the lack of a physical keyboard renders the device unusable for business users. However, the "no third-party apps" constraint in version one reduces complexity, allowing a focus on perfecting core functions (web, music, email) before opening the platform.

Evidence & Blockers: The proposal correctly identifies that wireless carriers normally dictate design; the exclusive deal is the specific mechanism to invert this power dynamic. The risk of "no phone experience" is mitigated by the fact that the device is fundamentally a computer and music player first. The premium price is justified by the unique integration of capabilities no other handset offers. There are no factual blockers; the risks are execution challenges inherent to innovation, not flaws in the logic.

Kill criteria: If the carrier refuses the revenue-share model or demands design changes, the deal dies. Success is measured by activation rates and retention, not just unit volume in year one.

DECISION: GO
