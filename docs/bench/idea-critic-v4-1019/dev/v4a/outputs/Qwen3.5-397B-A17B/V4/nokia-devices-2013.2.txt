First call: NO-GO. The proposal attempts to solve a software ecosystem deficit by acquiring a failing hardware asset, a mismatch of cause and remedy.

Steelman: Buying the handset division secures the primary distribution channel for the operating system, preventing its immediate abandonment by the largest partner and allowing tight integration to improve user experience where the platform currently lags.

However, the claim fails the blocker test. The core problem is identified as a lack of apps and developers due to the rival's dominance. Hardware ownership does not directly compel developers to build apps; it only controls one slice of the installed base. With smartphone sales already falling, the company is purchasing a depreciating asset (€5.4 billion) to protect a shrinking foothold. The integration risk of a large, struggling workforce further distracts from the actual work needed: courting developers.

Alternatives:
1. Do nothing: Allow the manufacturer to switch to the rival platform. This accelerates the OS decline but saves €5.4 billion and avoids integration chaos.
2. Direct developer incentives: Spend a fraction of €5.4 billion on bounties, tools, and subsidies to attract app creation directly, addressing the root cause rather than the symptom.
3. Acquire the division: High cost, high distraction, addresses distribution but not the app gap.

Pre-mortem: One year post-acquisition, the handset line continues to lose market share due to lack of apps. The company is now burdened with a failing hardware business and has burned capital that could have funded a software revival. The other handset makers, fearing competition, accelerate their exit to the rival platform, isolating the OS further.

Blockers:
- The stated cause of failure is the app ecosystem; hardware ownership is not a proven lever to fix this and may worsen it by alienating other manufacturers.
- Acquiring a business with falling sales to solve a software problem contradicts the principle of fixing the root cause.

Risks: Integration failure, competitor retaliation, capital misallocation.

The plan commits significant capital to a defensive move that does not address the primary constraint (developer adoption) and introduces massive operational overhead.

DECISION: NO-GO
