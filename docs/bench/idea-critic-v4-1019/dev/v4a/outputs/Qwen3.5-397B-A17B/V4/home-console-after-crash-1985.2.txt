First call: NO-GO. The market is declared dead, retailers are hostile, and the financial exposure from unlimited stock returns combined with no local track record presents an existential threat.

Steelman: Repositioning the console as an "Entertainment System" with non-game toys (robot, light gun) and strict quality control via a lockout chip can bypass retailer stigma and prevent a repeat of the market flood that caused the crash, but only if financial risk to retailers is capped.

The proposal fails the blocker test on financial structure. While the "entertainment system" framing and bundling are sound psychological pivots to distance the product from the "video game" label, the unconditional promise to take back *all* unsold stock is a fatal flaw. In a market where demand is unproven and perceived as zero, this removes all risk for the retailer and places 100% of the inventory risk on a company with no local foothold. If the rebranding fails to convince consumers, the company faces massive logistics costs and worthless inventory returns, potentially bankrupting the venture before it gains traction.

Furthermore, restricting third-party publishers via a lockout chip while simultaneously needing to prove the platform's value creates a content bottleneck. Without a critical mass of software, the hardware sells poorly; without sales, publishers won't bypass the lockout.

A viable path requires shifting the risk profile. The take-back promise must be converted into a consignment model or a capped return policy tied to specific performance milestones, rather than an open-ended liability. The "entertainment system" positioning is the correct strategic move, but the financial terms proposed guarantee failure if the initial consumer uptake is slow. The plan as stated bets the entire company on a perfect execution with no safety net against the very market hostility it seeks to overcome.

Blockers: Unconditional take-back promise creates uncapped downside liability for an unproven product in a hostile market.
Risks: Retailer refusal despite rebranding; developer backlash over lockout chip; competition from home computers.

DECISION: NO-GO
