First call: NO-GO. The proposal commits the company to an existential wager on a single customer's demand while ignoring a looming technological discontinuity (supersonic flight) and relying on unproven core technology (high-bypass engines).

Steelman: Building a massive, convertible wide-body jet now secures dominance in subsonic cargo and passenger markets before competitors can react, leveraging the only available engine supplier to lock out rivals.

However, the risks constitute blockers, not merely uncertainties. First, the "supersonic expected to replace subsonic" risk is a strategic mismatch; if the market shifts to supersonic as predicted, this massive investment becomes stranded assets before reaching payback. Second, the financial exposure ("far larger than any... made") combined with "only one airline has ordered" creates a single-point-of-failure dependency. If that carrier falters or the engine fails, the company collapses. Third, the "unproven engine" is a technical blocker; committing to airframe production before engine validation violates basic integration sequencing.

Alternatives:
1. Do nothing: Preserve capital to invest in supersonic research or wait for engine maturity.
2. Upgrade current fleet: Improve efficiency of existing models rather than betting on a 2.5x size jump.
3. Joint venture: Share the factory and development risk with the customer or government before committing.

The proposal fails the "beat do nothing" test because the downside (bankruptcy via stranded assets or engine failure) outweighs the upside (cost reduction for one airline) given the stated probability of supersonic displacement. The lack of multiple orders confirms the market has not validated the need for this specific capacity.

Blockers:
1. Strategic obsolescence: High probability of supersonic replacement renders subsonic mega-jets uneconomical.
2. Financial concentration: One customer cannot underwrite a company-sized bet.
3. Technical dependency: Airframe commitment precedes engine proof.

Risks:
1. Cost overruns on the new factory.
2. Delayed engine certification.

This is a one-way door with insufficient evidence. The plan requires a phased approach: secure engine validation and at least three more launch customers before breaking ground on the factory.

DECISION: NO-GO
