First call: NO-GO. The proposal compounds a recent 60% price hike with immediate, high-friction operational fragmentation, ignoring the primary risk of customer backlash.

Steelman: Separating the brands allows distinct strategies for a declining physical medium and a growing digital one, preventing legacy constraints from stifling streaming innovation.

However, the timing is fatal. Executing a complex split—creating new brands, accounts, queues, and billing systems—immediately after a massive price increase treats loyal customers as enemies. The proposal explicitly acknowledges "customer confusion" and "reaction to the price increase" as risks but offers no mitigation other than separation, which actually increases friction (two logins, two bills).

Alternatives:
1. **Do Nothing:** Maintain the combined plan. Risk: Streaming strategy may be dragged down by DVD logistics. Upside: Customer stability.
2. **Delayed Split:** Announce the strategic intent now but delay the operational split (logins/billing) by 12–18 months. This allows the price shock to absorb before adding friction.
3. **Soft Separation:** Keep unified billing and login but brand the services distinctly on the interface.

Fit: While the north star of streaming growth is valid, the method destroys the unified value proposition that justifies the recent price hike. A pre-mortem reveals a mass exodus: customers hit with a 60% cost increase will not tolerate the hassle of managing two separate subscriptions. The "loss of cross-service recommendations" further devalues the bundle without lowering the price.

Blockers:
1. **Compounded Friction:** Implementing separate billing and logins immediately after a 60% price hike creates a predictable churn event. The proposal admits this risk but proceeds anyway.
2. **Value Destruction:** Removing shared ratings and recommendations reduces the utility of both services without offering a price reduction to offset the inconvenience.

Risks: Brand damage, immediate subscriber loss, operational complexity of running two distinct stacks prematurely.

The plan fails because it prioritizes internal management convenience over customer retention during a period of extreme price sensitivity. The "declining" nature of DVD does not justify alienating the remaining paying base today.

DECISION: NO-GO
