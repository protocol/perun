First call: NO-GO. The proposal commits to a fixed public schedule for unproven core technologies without a demand signal or regulatory mandate, creating asymmetric downside.

**Steelman:** A bold first-mover commitment forces rapid resolution of battery and transmission risks, securing decade-long IP dominance before competitors react.

**Attack:**
1.  **Blocker (Evidence Contradiction):** The plan relies on Nickel-Metal-Hydride (NiMH) durability for volume production, yet the proposal explicitly states durability is "unproven." Committing to a 1997 launch date before validating cycle life creates a near-certain warranty catastrophe. You cannot schedule physics.
2.  **Better Way:** With fuel cheap and no mandates, the "do nothing" or "low-volume test fleet" option captures learning without exposing the entire brand to reliability failure. A silent pilot beats a public promise when the core mechanism is unverified.
3.  **Fit:** Without a regulatory stick or customer pull (demand signal), this is a technology push in search of a problem. The cost premium cannot be justified to consumers saving money on cheap petrol.
4.  **Pre-mortem:** It is 1998. The cars are recalled due to battery degradation within 18 months. The public schedule made the failure visible, destroying trust in the brand's engineering competence while competitors watched and learned from your mistakes.

**Blockers:**
*   **Unproven Core Tech vs. Fixed Date:** The proposal admits battery durability is unknown yet locks a mass-production date. This is a factual contradiction; the timeline assumes a solution that does not yet exist.
*   **No Economic Driver:** Cheap fuel and zero mandates mean the value proposition is negative for the customer at launch prices.

**Risks:**
*   Massive warranty exposure from premature battery failure.
*   Brand damage from public failure of a "bold" promise.
*   Capital misallocation away from proven revenue generators.

**Decision Logic:**
A plan that schedules the impossible (proven durability by a fixed date without current proof) and ignores market reality (no demand, cheap substitutes) fails the basic viability test. This requires a phased approach: validate the battery first, then set the schedule. As proposed, it is a gamble with the company's reputation.

DECISION: NO-GO
