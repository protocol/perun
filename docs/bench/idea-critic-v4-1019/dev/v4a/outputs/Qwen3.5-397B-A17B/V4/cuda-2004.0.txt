First call: NO-GO. The proposal commits massive, certain costs to every unit sold for a market that does not yet exist and has no committed customers, while distracting engineering from the core survival battle against a main rival.

**Steelman:** Embedding general-purpose compute capabilities now creates an inevitable platform monopoly, turning every gaming chip into a scientific supercomputer before competitors realize the shift, securing decades of future dominance.

**Options:**
1.  **Do Nothing:** Maintain focus on the core gaming/workstation race; let academics continue hacking existing APIs. Cost: Zero incremental unit cost. Risk: Missing a future shift. Reversibility: High (can add features later if demand proves real).
2.  **Specialized Skunkworks:** Build the toolchain and features only for high-margin workstation cards, not mass-market consumer chips. Cost: Limited R&D, no unit cost increase. Risk: Slower adoption. Reversibility: High.
3.  **Proposed Plan:** Subsidize unknown future demand by taxing every current customer. Cost: High per-unit margin erosion. Risk: Fatal distraction from core rival; bankruptcy if gaming market share slips. Reversibility: Low (silicon area is permanent).

**Fit:** This contradicts the immediate north star of winning the core graphics race. It assumes a market transition that is currently hypothetical.

**Pre-mortem:** The program drains cash and engineering focus; the rival launches a superior gaming chip; market share collapses; the scientific features remain unused by anyone willing to pay; the company is acquired or fails. This is a one-way door with a high cost of delay only if the rival does it first (which they are not currently doing).

**Evidence:** The proposal admits "no large paying market exists" and "no committed customers." The costs are certain; the revenue is speculative.

**Blockers:**
1.  **Unit Economics:** Raising unit cost on every chip without a paying customer violates basic cash-flow discipline for a company in a tight race.
2.  **Strategic Focus:** Engineering distraction from the "core race with its main rival" creates an immediate existential threat that outweighs a potential future opportunity.

**Risks:** Niche demand remains niche; academic experiments do not convert to enterprise spend; compiler effort becomes a money pit.

**Decision:** The plan fails the blocker test. It bets the company's current survival on a phantom market. A cheaper, safer alternative (workstation-only or wait-and-see) reaches the same learning upside without the existential risk.

DECISION: NO-GO
