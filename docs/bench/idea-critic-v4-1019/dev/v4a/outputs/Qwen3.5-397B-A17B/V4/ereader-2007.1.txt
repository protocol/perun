First call: NO-GO. The proposal asks a software-centric bookseller to enter capital-intensive consumer hardware manufacturing, a domain where it has zero experience, while simultaneously disrupting its core publisher relationships and cannibalizing existing print revenue.

Steelman: A dedicated, cellular-enabled device with a paper-like screen removes friction for book buying, creating a closed ecosystem that locks users into the store and solves piracy fears via controlled access.

However, the blockers are fatal under the stated constraints. First, the company has never designed consumer hardware; developing a reliable e-reader with cellular connectivity requires supply chain, manufacturing, and radio certification capabilities the company explicitly lacks. Second, the economic model is unproven and dangerous: offering "free" cellular connectivity shifts significant operational costs to the retailer, while "single low price" titles threaten to collapse margins and alienate publishers who already fear piracy. Third, historical data cited in the proposal indicates earlier dedicated e-readers sold poorly; entering a failed market category with a high-cost, high-risk hardware play contradicts prudent capital allocation.

Alternatives such as licensing software to existing hardware manufacturers or waiting for the market to mature offer the same upside (digital sales growth) with drastically lower risk and capital expenditure. The "do nothing" option preserves current high-margin print sales and publisher relationships.

The risks are not merely executional but existential: failure in hardware could bankrupt the initiative, while success might erode the core print business faster than digital revenue can replace it. The proposal assumes the company can simultaneously master hardware engineering, negotiate new publisher terms, and create a new consumer behavior pattern, all without prior evidence of capability in any of these areas.

Blockers: Lack of hardware design/manufacturing experience; unproven economic model threatening margins and publisher relations; historical failure of similar dedicated devices.
Risks: Cannibalization of print sales; high capital burn; slow consumer adoption.

DECISION: NO-GO
