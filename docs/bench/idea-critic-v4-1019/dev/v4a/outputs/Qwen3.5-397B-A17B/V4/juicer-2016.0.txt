First call: NO-GO. The unit economics and behavioral friction of a $699 connected juicer relying on proprietary, subscription-only inputs create an insurmountable barrier to mass adoption in a crowded, low-margin kitchen appliance market.

**Steelman:** A premium, closed-loop ecosystem guarantees fresh, consistent juice with zero prep, justifying a high upfront cost and recurring revenue through unmatched convenience and quality control.

**Attack:**
1.  **Price Dissonance:** At $699, the device competes with high-end blenders and traditional juicers that accept *any* produce. The "razor-and-blade" model fails when the "razor" (hardware) is prohibitively expensive for the average consumer, severely limiting the installed base required to make the "blades" (fruit bags) profitable.
2.  **Unit Economics:** Proprietary pre-packed bags inherently cost more per ounce than loose produce due to packaging, logistics, and margin stacking. Consumers sensitive to grocery bills will reject a permanent premium on a daily consumable.
3.  **Unnecessary Connectivity:** Wi-Fi and app-based freshness checks introduce failure points (connectivity loss, server shutdowns) for a mechanical task. This adds cost and complexity without solving a critical user pain point, risking the device becoming e-waste if the startup folds.
4.  **Market Size:** The intersection of consumers willing to pay $699 for hardware *and* commit to a costly subscription for a single-function appliance is vanishingly small.

**Alternatives:** A cheaper, non-connected device accepting generic bags, or a premium blender marketed for convenience, offers similar value with lower friction and risk.

**Pre-mortem:** The company burns cash on hardware inventory and customer acquisition, but churn spikes as users realize the per-juice cost is 3x higher than store-bought juice. The app becomes a liability, and the high price point prevents the scale needed to lower bag costs. The company pivots or shuts down within 18 months.

**Blocker:** The combination of a luxury hardware price point with a premium consumable model for a commoditized activity (juicing) creates a negative value proposition that no amount of marketing can sustain against cheaper, flexible alternatives. The math does not work for mass market viability.

DECISION: NO-GO
