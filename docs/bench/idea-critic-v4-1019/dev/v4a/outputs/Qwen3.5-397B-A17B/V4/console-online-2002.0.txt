First call: NO-GO. The proposal requires a mandatory paid subscription for online play in a market where rivals offer it free or publisher-managed, while broadband adoption is low and the company lacks operational experience.

Steelman: A unified, premium online service with shared identity and voice chat creates a superior, sticky ecosystem that justifies an annual fee through quality and convenience, differentiating the console from fragmented, free alternatives.

However, the blockers are fatal as stated:
1.  **Market Contradiction:** The proposal mandates a paid service where competitors are free. With "few homes having broadband," the addressable market is already tiny; adding a paywall on top of hardware and ISP costs shrinks the viable user base below the threshold needed to sustain the required "hosting and support operations."
2.  **Operational Void:** The company admits it has "never run" the necessary hosting and support infrastructure. Building this from scratch while simultaneously launching a console and convincing a skeptical, non-broadband market to pay is an execution impossibility. The risk isn't just high; the capability is absent.
3.  **Adoption Friction:** Requiring broadband in 2002 excludes the vast majority of the installed base. Making this service the *only* online option (implied by "console-wide") alienates the dial-up majority and removes the publisher's ability to experiment with free models that might drive hardware sales.

Alternatives:
*   **Do Nothing/Status Quo:** Let publishers handle online play. Cost: $0 new ops. Risk: Fragmented experience. Reversibility: High.
*   **Hybrid Model:** Offer the premium service as an optional value-add for broadband users while allowing free, publisher-managed play for others. This captures the high-end market without blocking the low-end.

The current plan fails because it forces a high-cost, high-friction solution on a market not ready for it, without the internal capability to deliver it. It bets the console's success on a service that excludes most potential buyers.

Blockers: Mandatory paid model in a free-market expectation; lack of operational capability; low broadband penetration making the TAM insufficient to support new ops.
Risks: User backlash, publisher defection, operational collapse.

DECISION: NO-GO
