First call: NO-GO. The proposal commits to a fixed public schedule for unproven core technologies without a demand signal or regulatory mandate, creating asymmetric downside.

Steelman: A bold, fixed-date commitment forces the organization to solve novel battery and transmission challenges rapidly, potentially securing a decade-long lead if durability and cost targets are miraculously met.

However, the claim fails the blocker test. The proposal explicitly admits battery durability is "unproven" and there is "no near-term demand signal," while fuel is cheap. Committing to mass production by 1997 based on these facts is not a calculated risk; it is a gamble with corporate solvency. A failure in battery cycle life would trigger massive warranty exposure and brand destruction. Without a regulatory gun to the customer's head or a spike in fuel prices, the economic value proposition is negative.

Alternatives:
1. Do nothing: Preserves capital, avoids warranty catastrophe, waits for market signals.
2. Limited pilot: Deploy a small fleet to specific regions to gather real-world durability data before committing to tooling.
3. Technology licensing: Develop the system but delay mass production until durability is proven.

The "do nothing" or "limited pilot" options offer the same potential upside (learning, future readiness) with drastically lower risk. The proposed path beats neither. The cost of delay is negligible given the lack of competitors and mandates; waiting a quarter to verify battery life is prudent, not costly. The cost of being wrong is existential.

Blockers:
1. Unproven battery durability combined with a fixed mass-production date creates a high-probability warranty crisis.
2. No demand signal and cheap fuel negate the economic case for immediate volume production.
3. A fixed public schedule removes the flexibility needed to solve novel engineering problems.

Risks:
1. Competitor entry (low probability given current landscape).
2. Future regulation (uncertain timing).

This plan confuses "ambition" with "strategy." It locks the company into a failure mode where engineering delays become public scandals. The correct move is to validate the technology privately before staking the corporate schedule on it.

DECISION: NO-GO
