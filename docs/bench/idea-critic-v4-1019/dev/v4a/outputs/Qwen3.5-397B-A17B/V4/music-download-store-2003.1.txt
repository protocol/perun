First call: NO-GO. The proposal bets the company's hardware dominance on a low-margin software service that intentionally excludes the majority of potential customers (non-own computer users) while facing entrenched free alternatives and powerful suppliers (labels) who have incentive to undermine the model or raise costs later.

Steelman: A curated, legal, high-quality ecosystem that converts casual pirates into paying customers by offering unmatched convenience and integration, securing label partnerships through strict copy protection to prevent cannibalization of physical sales.

Attack:
1.  **Market Fit:** Consumers accustomed to "free everywhere" are unlikely to pay 99 cents per song for a library restricted to a single hardware brand. The friction of payment plus the limitation of device compatibility creates a weak value proposition against free, universal file-sharing.
2.  **Supplier Risk:** Major labels hold all the leverage. They can withdraw licenses or demand higher royalties once the store gains traction, squeezing the already thin margin. The proposal admits "little margin," leaving no buffer for supplier aggression.
3.  **Strategic Myopia:** Rivals are betting on subscriptions (access over ownership), which aligns better with music consumption habits and offers recurring revenue. Betting on per-song sales locks the company into a declining transactional model.
4.  **Adoption Barrier:** Restricting the store to "only on the company's own computers" artificially caps the total addressable market. If the goal is to combat piracy, limiting the legal alternative to a subset of users ensures piracy remains the default for everyone else.

Blockers:
*   The economic model relies on label cooperation despite having zero leverage and offering them a channel that competes with their physical sales, with no stated mechanism to prevent future royalty hikes that would erase margins.
*   Restricting access to only company-owned computers contradicts the goal of widespread adoption against free, universal alternatives.

Risks:
*   Labels withdraw licenses or raise fees.
*   Consumers reject per-song pricing in favor of emerging subscription models.
*   Hardware sales do not increase enough to subsidize the low-margin store.

The proposal fails because it creates a fragile, low-margin dependency on hostile suppliers while intentionally limiting customer access. It does not beat "do nothing" or the rival subscription approach on cost, risk, or reversibility.

DECISION: NO-GO
