First call: NO-GO. The proposal commits $7.5B in stock to a loss-making entity with no permanent CEO, facing active community hostility and a viable free alternative, without a defined mechanism to arrest defection or prove ROI.

Steelman: Acquiring the dominant platform now prevents a competitor from doing so and secures the primary developer workflow before the free alternative matures, betting that independence preserves the network effect despite cultural friction.

However, the claim fails the blocker test. The target is leaderless and losing money; the buyer is historically distrusted. The "independent operation" promise is unproven against a vocal community already mobilizing toward a free competitor. In software ecosystems, trust is the primary asset; once developers perceive an existential threat, migration to a free alternative accelerates regardless of corporate assurances. The $7.5B price tag assumes the status quo holds, but the very act of acquisition triggers the collapse of the asset's value (the community).

There is no evidence presented that the buyer can operate the platform better than the status quo or halt the churn to the free rival. The lack of a permanent CEO at the target suggests internal instability that a massive acquisition will exacerbate, not solve. The risk of developer defection is not a future possibility but an immediate reaction to the announcement, rendering the revenue projections invalid.

A cheaper, safer alternative exists: wait for the target's valuation to correct due to its leadership vacuum and losses, or build a partnership rather than a full acquisition. Paying a premium for a distressed, hostile asset violates basic capital allocation discipline. The "cultural fit" risk is actually a business model risk; if the users leave, the revenue disappears, making the multiple indefensible.

Blockers:
1. Paying a premium ($7.5B) for a loss-making company with no CEO and active user revolt.
2. No mechanism provided to prevent immediate migration to the free competitor upon announcement.
3. The core asset (community trust) is directly threatened by the buyer's history and the deal structure.

Risks: Antitrust scrutiny, integration failure, brand contamination.

The plan fails because it assumes the asset remains stable post-announcement, which contradicts the stated community distrust and availability of a free alternative.

DECISION: NO-GO
