First call: GO. The proposal leverages asymmetric strategy by rejecting a direct hardware arms race in favor of unique interaction models and price segmentation.

**Steelman:** By deploying a dual-screen, touch-enabled device at a lower price point while maintaining the legacy product, the company creates a distinct market category that appeals to non-core users, rendering the rival's superior graphics irrelevant to the new value proposition.

**Attack & Alternatives:**
1.  **Do Nothing:** Ceding the narrative to the rival's "power" metric guarantees a slow erosion of the install base as developers chase the highest specs.
2.  **Match Hardware:** Attempting to out-graph the well-funded rival invites a capital-intensive war with low differentiation and high risk of being the "also-ran."
3.  **The Flaw in Risks:** The concern that "unusual hardware confuses developers" is actually the primary moat; if it were easy to copy, the rival would have done it. "Unproven" touch games represent the exact blue ocean required to broaden the audience beyond core players. The cost of two screens is mitigated by targeting a lower price tier than the rival's premium device.

**Pre-mortem:** Failure occurs only if the company tries to force core-gamer expectations onto this device or fails to secure even one "killer app" that demonstrates the necessity of two screens. If the device is marketed as a weaker version of the rival rather than a different kind of toy, it will be dismissed as a gimmick.

**Blockers:** None. The facts provided show a coherent strategy: distinct form factor, lower price, audience expansion, and portfolio depth (keeping the old unit). The risks cited are inherent to innovation, not factual contradictions. The proposal explicitly addresses the cost concern via pricing strategy and the audience concern via feature differentiation.

**Kill Criteria:** If within 12 months no software title sells >1M units leveraging the touch/dual-screen mechanic, the hypothesis of "broadening appeal" is false, and the form factor should be abandoned in the next iteration.

**Decision:** The plan survives the attack because it changes the basis of competition from raw power (where the rival wins) to interaction and price (where the rival is absent).

DECISION: GO
