First call: GO. The proposal addresses a critical vulnerability: commodity pricing driven by buyer ignorance. If buyers cannot distinguish the component, they will not pay a premium for quality, forcing competition solely on price.

Steelman: By shifting demand pull from the computer maker to the end consumer, the chip maker forces computer makers to include the superior chip to satisfy customer requests, thereby securing pricing power and shelf space despite the unprecedented nature of component branding.

Alternatives considered:
1. Do nothing: Continue competing on price and specifications alone; margin erosion is inevitable as clones mimic part numbers.
2. Exclusive contracts: Likely anti-trust violations and would alienate the very computer makers needed for volume.
3. Technical superiority alone: Insufficient if buyers cannot identify the technology inside the closed box.

Fit: Moves the north star from "units shipped to manufacturers" to "consumer preference," directly attacking the clone threat.

Pre-mortem: The campaign fails if computer makers uniformly refuse the co-op advertising terms or if consumers remain indifferent to the logo. However, the financial incentive (paid advertising) aligns interests; refusing free marketing budget is irrational for most partners. The risk of resentment is outweighed by the leverage of consumer demand.

Evidence & Verification: The proposal relies on the stated fact that buyers currently do not know which chip they own. This ignorance is the root cause of the commodity trap. The mechanism (co-op advertising) is a standard channel incentive, merely applied upstream. The cost is high, but the cost of margin collapse is higher.

Blockers: NONE. No factual contradiction exists in the proposal. The risks (unprecedented approach, resentment) are execution challenges, not logical flaws that invalidate the strategy. The "unprecedented" nature is the source of the potential moat, not a reason to abort.

Risks: High budget consumption; potential partner friction. These are managed by the kill criteria inherent in the pilot nature of such campaigns (stop if sales lift does not materialize).

The strongest attack—that this wastes budget on an uninterested audience—fails because the goal is not immediate consumer education but creating a signal that computer makers cannot ignore. If even 10% of buyers ask for the logo, manufacturers must comply.

DECISION: GO
