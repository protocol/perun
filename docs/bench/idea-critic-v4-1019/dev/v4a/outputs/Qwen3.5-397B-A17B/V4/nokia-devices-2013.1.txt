First call: NO-GO. The proposal attempts to solve a software ecosystem deficit with a hardware acquisition in a declining business, a mismatch that historically destroys value.

Steelman: Acquiring the handset maker secures the primary distribution channel for the operating system and prevents its immediate defection to the rival platform, buying time to cultivate the app ecosystem.

However, the facts provided reveal fatal blockers. First, the target's smartphone sales are already falling; buying a shrinking asset at €5.4 billion to solve a growth problem contradicts basic turnaround logic. Second, the core problem is identified as an app-ecosystem deficit (lack of developers/apps relative to the market leader). Hardware ownership does not automatically generate software demand; it merely adds the burden of managing a losing hardware business to the existing software struggle. The "integration of a large workforce" and "other handset makers' reaction" are severe compounding risks: alienating other device manufacturers who use this OS could fragment the very ecosystem the deal aims to save, while the falling sales suggest the brand cannot compete on merit.

Alternatives: "Do nothing" avoids sinking capital into a failing unit. A "cheaper alternative" would be direct investment in developer incentives or software tools to solve the root cause (app scarcity) without the drag of hardware manufacturing. The proposed path fails to beat "do nothing" because it introduces massive execution risk (integration) and financial drag (falling sales) without a proven mechanism to fix the stated root cause (ecosystem).

Blockers:
1. The target's sales are falling, making it a depreciating asset rather than a growth engine.
2. The solution (hardware control) does not address the stated core failure mode (software ecosystem/app scarcity).
3. High risk of alienating other handset partners, potentially accelerating ecosystem collapse.

Risks: Integration failure, continued brand decline, capital misallocation.

This is a one-way door with a high cost of delay only if the manufacturer defects immediately, but the falling sales suggest the manufacturer is already losing relevance. The plan commits real money to a capped, declining upside without a clear path to reversing the software deficit.

DECISION: NO-GO
