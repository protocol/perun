First call: GO. The demographic shift creates a clear opening for affordable style, and leveraging existing mechanicals drastically reduces development cost and time-to-market.

**Steelman:** Launch a visually distinct, customizable sporty car using proven economy underpinnings to capture the emerging youth market at minimal risk, supported by aggressive marketing to define the segment.

**Options:**
1.  **Do nothing:** Cede the youth segment to competitors; rely on aging buyers for dull compacts. High long-term risk of brand irrelevance.
2.  **New Platform:** Build a dedicated sports car chassis. Prohibitive cost and time; misses the immediate demographic wave.
3.  **Proposal (Modified):** Proceed but cap initial ad spend to 50% until first-month sales data validates volume demand.

**Fit:** Aligns perfectly with the "north star" of capturing the postwar boom generation. Reuses existing assets (mechanicals) to fund the style upgrade.

**Pre-mortem:** Failure occurs if the "cheap" parts perception outweighs the styling appeal, or if the options list confuses buyers rather than enticing them. However, the low base price acts as a floor; even if options fail, the entry point remains competitive. The door is two-way: if sales lag, production can scale back quickly since tooling is shared. Delay costs the entire model year cycle with this specific demographic.

**Evidence:** The proposal relies on the verified existence of the baby boom cohort and the manufacturer's current compact architecture. No new engineering facts are assumed beyond the feasibility of restyling.

**Blockers:** NONE. The risk of "unproven demand" is inherent to all new products but is mitigated here by the low capital exposure (shared parts) and the low base price. The risk of "looking cheap" is addressed by the specific design directive (long hood, short deck) which historically signals sportiness regardless of underpinnings.

**Risks:** Marketing overspend before validation; brand dilution if quality perception drops. These are managed via the proposed "long options list" strategy which shifts margin risk to the customer's choice.

**Decision:** The potential upside of defining a new market segment ("pony car") vastly outweighs the limited downside of shared-component development. The plan turns a weakness (economy parts) into a strength (affordability).

DECISION: GO
