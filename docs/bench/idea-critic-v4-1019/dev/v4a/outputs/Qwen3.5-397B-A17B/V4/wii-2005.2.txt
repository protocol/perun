First call: GO. The proposal targets an uncontested market segment (casual/non-gamers) while rivals fight a saturated hardware war, creating a blue ocean where low cost and novel interaction outweigh graphical fidelity.

Steelman: By abandoning the graphics race, the console achieves a price point and form factor accessible to households, using motion control to lower the skill floor for gaming, thereby expanding the total addressable market beyond traditional gamers.

Alternatives:
1. Do nothing: Remain third in a shrinking race against superior HD hardware; likely irrelevance.
2. Compete on power: Require massive R&D spend with high risk of failure against entrenched leaders; margin compression.
3. Proposed path: Lower upfront risk via modest hardware, betting on software innovation and volume sales over hardware margins.

Fit: Moves the north star from "best graphics for core gamers" to "gaming for everyone." This redefines the competition rather than joining it.

Pre-mortem: Failure occurs if the motion controller is seen as a gimmick without compelling software, or if third-party developers refuse to support the non-HD architecture. However, these are execution risks, not structural blockers. The "toy" perception is a feature for the target demographic, not a bug. Delay costs first-mover advantage in the casual space.

Evidence: The proposal relies on the verified fact that rivals are locked into a high-cost, high-margin HD trajectory, leaving the low-end/casual gap open. The thin hardware margin is acceptable if software attach rates compensate, a standard model shifted to a new audience.

Blockers: NONE. No fact presented proves the casual market doesn't exist or that the price point is unsustainable. The skepticism of hardcore gamers is irrelevant to the stated goal of targeting non-gamers.

Risks: Third-party adoption speed, controller durability, and maintaining software quality. These become kill criteria: if launch titles do not demonstrate the controller's value within 6 months, or if attach rates fall below 3:1, the strategy must pivot.

The plan beats "do nothing" and the "power race" by changing the rules of engagement. It is a two-way door; if the casual bet fails, the company can retreat or pivot, having lost less than a failed HD gamble.

DECISION: GO
