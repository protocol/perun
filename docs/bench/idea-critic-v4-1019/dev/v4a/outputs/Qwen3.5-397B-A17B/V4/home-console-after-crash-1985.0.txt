First call: NO-GO. The market is declared dead, retailers refuse stock, and the financial exposure from unlimited buybacks combined with zero brand equity presents an immediate liquidity trap.

Steelman: Repositioning the hardware as a "toy" with strict quality control via lockout chips and risk-reversal for retailers could bypass the "video game" stigma and rebuild trust.

However, the proposal fails the blocker test on three verified facts within the text:
1.  **Financial Blocker:** The "promise to take back unsold stock" without a cap creates uncapped liability. In a market where retailers are actively clearing inventory, this guarantees massive returns that a company with "no console track record" cannot absorb.
2.  **Channel Blocker:** Retailers are explicitly "refusing to stock video games." Styling it as a toy does not automatically override a hard refusal from buyers who view the entire category as toxic inventory. The proposal offers no evidence that "toy" classification bypasses this specific buyer mandate.
3.  **Competitive Blocker:** "Cheap home computers" are cited as playing games. A locked-down, expensive toy bundle cannot compete on utility or price against open, multifunctional devices already in homes.

Alternatives:
*   **Do nothing:** Avoids certain financial loss in a collapsed market.
*   **Licensing only:** License the technology to an established Western toy maker to bear the inventory risk.
*   **Arcade-first:** Rebuild brand reputation via arcades where the company may have presence, before attempting home retail.

The risks listed (developer anger, returns exposure) are not mere possibilities; the return exposure is a structural flaw in the current plan. The "lockout chip" restricts supply when the primary need is proving demand exists at all. Without a capped buyback clause or proof that "toy" aisles are open to this specific vendor, the plan relies on hope rather than mechanics.

Blockers: Uncapped financial liability from buybacks; explicit retailer refusal regardless of branding; superior utility of home computers.
Risks: Developer backlash, execution failure in a new region.

DECISION: NO-GO
