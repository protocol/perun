First call: NO-GO. The proposal asks a computer and music player maker with zero phone experience to launch a premium, keyboard-less device in a market dominated by keyboard business phones, while accepting limited reach via exclusivity and no third-party apps. The risks of market rejection and operational inexperience are immediate blockers based on the stated facts.

Steelman: A unified device leveraging existing music dominance could redefine the category if users prioritize touch and web over physical keyboards, and if carrier revenue sharing offsets exclusivity limits.

However, the "better-way" analysis reveals a fatal flaw in the proposed constraints. The market is led by keyboard business phones; launching a premium device without a keyboard and without third-party applications removes the two primary value drivers for the target premium segment (business/users accustomed to keyboards and extensibility). A cheaper, safer alternative exists: license the software to multiple carriers to maximize reach while retaining design control, or launch a hybrid device. The proposed "single carrier exclusivity" combined with "no apps" and "no keyboard" creates a product that fails to meet the stated market leaders' features while restricting its own distribution.

Pre-mortem: The device launches, but business users reject the lack of a keyboard and apps, while consumers balk at the premium price for a phone with limited carrier availability. Sales stall, eroding the music player brand without gaining phone share.

Evidence check: The proposal admits "no phone-industry experience" and "no third-party applications." These are not risks; they are stated facts that contradict the requirement to beat established keyboard-led competitors. The claim that a single carrier will pay a revenue share for a non-standard, app-less device is an unsupported assumption given the carrier's usual power to dictate design.

Blockers:
1. The product lacks the defining features (keyboard, apps) of the current premium market leaders, yet charges a premium price.
2. Exclusivity limits reach precisely when the brand has no phone reputation to overcome adoption friction.
3. No evidence supports the assumption that a carrier will cede design control and pay revenue share for an unproven, feature-deficient handset.

Risks: Total brand erosion, inventory write-downs, failure to gain carrier support elsewhere post-exclusivity.

DECISION: NO-GO
