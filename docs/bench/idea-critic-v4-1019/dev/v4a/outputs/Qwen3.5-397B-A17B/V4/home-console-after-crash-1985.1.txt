First call: NO-GO. The market is declared dead, retailers refuse stock, and the proposal admits zero track record outside Japan while exposing the firm to unlimited liability via unconditional stock returns.

Steelman: Repositioning the console as an "entertainment system" with non-game toys and a strict quality lockout can bypass the "video game" stigma, secure retail shelf space through perceived novelty, and prevent a repeat of the 1983 software glut that caused the crash.

However, the financial structure is fatal. The promise to take back *all* unsold stock removes all risk from retailers while transferring catastrophic inventory risk to the manufacturer. In a market where demand is unproven and the brand is unknown, this invites retailers to over-order speculatively, knowing they can return everything. If the "entertainment" angle fails to convince consumers, the company faces massive reverse logistics costs and worthless inventory, potentially bankrupting the division before it gains traction.

Alternatives:
1. **Do nothing:** Avoids immediate capital loss but cedes the eventual recovery to competitors.
2. **Limited pilot:** Launch in a single test city (e.g., New York) with strict inventory caps and no blanket return policy, using data to prove demand before national rollout. This reduces exposure while testing the "entertainment" narrative.
3. **Licensing only:** License the hardware to an established US toy manufacturer to handle distribution risk, sacrificing margin for survival.

The proposal fails the blocker test. The "take-back promise" is a specific fact in the plan that creates a negative feedback loop: it encourages the very over-stocking that led to the 1983 crash, without the demand to clear it. The lockout chip may anger developers, further starving the system of software, making the returns even more likely. Without a capped liability model or a proven demand signal, the financial exposure outweighs the strategic opportunity. The plan relies on retailers behaving rationally in a panicked market while offering them a risk-free arbitrage at the manufacturer's expense.

Blockers: Unlimited liability via unconditional stock returns in a collapsed market; no verified demand to justify national scale.
Risks: Developer alienation, toy bundling failing to mask console identity, home computer competition.

DECISION: NO-GO
