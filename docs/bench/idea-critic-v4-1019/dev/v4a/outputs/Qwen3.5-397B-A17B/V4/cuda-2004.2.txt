First call: NO-GO. The proposal asks a company dependent on gaming revenue to incur immediate, certain costs on every unit sold to chase a non-existent market, while simultaneously fighting a primary rival in its core business.

Steelman: Embedding general-purpose compute capabilities now creates an uncontested monopoly in scientific acceleration before competitors react, transforming the company from a component vendor into a platform owner.

However, the blocker is fatal under the stated facts. The plan raises unit costs for a mass-market product where price sensitivity is high, yet there are "no committed customers" and "no large paying market." This violates basic capital allocation: you cannot fund a massive ecosystem play (compiler, toolchain, university programs) by taxing a core business that is already under threat from a main rival. The engineering distraction risks losing the gaming race, which provides 100% of current revenue, to subsidize academic experiments that generate zero income.

Alternatives:
1. Do nothing: Preserve margins and focus engineering on winning the gaming war.
2. Wait for signal: Build programmability only after a specific, paying customer segment emerges.
3. Partnered approach: Fund toolchain development only if a major scientific lab or government entity co-funds the silicon area.

The pre-mortem is clear: The company burns cash on silicon area and software for years, loses market share in gaming due to distracted engineering and higher prices, and the scientific feature remains unused because the software ecosystem never reaches critical mass without early adoption incentives that don't exist yet.

This is a one-way door with high delay cost only if the rival is also doing it; the prompt implies they are not. The cost of delay is negligible because the market does not exist. The cost of action is immediate margin erosion and strategic focus loss.

Blockers:
1. Negative unit economics: Raising costs on every unit for a feature with zero current revenue.
2. Strategic conflict: Diverting engineering resources from an active, existential competitive race to a niche with no customers.

Risks:
1. Academic interest fails to convert to commercial demand.
2. Toolchain adoption lags hardware availability.

DECISION: NO-GO
