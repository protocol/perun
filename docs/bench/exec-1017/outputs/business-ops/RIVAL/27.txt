**Recommendation: Pause the price increase to $10.99 until the premium title licensing fee is disclosed.**

Proceeding with a price hike while a major cost driver (licensing) remains unknown exposes the library to margin compression. Furthermore, your current baseline price of $9.99 is likely too low for a sustainable public service model, creating a "cheap" anchor that will make future increases difficult. Secure the cost data first; if margins hold, consider a larger increase (e.g., to $12.99) to establish a proper value floor rather than a incremental 10% bump.

**Three Main Failure Modes:**
1.  **Margin Inversion:** The undisclosed licensing fee for premium titles exceeds the $0.25–$1.00 marginal revenue gained from the price hike, causing every new download to lose money.
2.  **Conversion Collapse:** The assumption of 80% conversion from print to digital is unrealistic for a paid pilot; if actual conversion is <40%, the revenue base vanishes regardless of the price point.
3.  **Churn Spike:** Raising prices before proving the value of "premium" titles triggers immediate churn among the existing pilot base, violating the 70% retention assumption.

**Stop/Rethink Triggers:**
*   **Churn >10%** within 60 days of any price change announcement.
*   **Cost per download >$1.50** (breaching the margin safety zone).
*   **Actual conversion <50%** of the projected 2,500 members by June 1, 2024.

**Critical Arithmetic & Assumption Issues:**
*   **Revenue Calculation Error:** Your estimated annual revenue of $299,700 assumes 100% retention for 12 months. However, you stated only 70% stay after six months. A realistic projection must account for this drop-off mid-year, reducing the actual estimated revenue significantly (closer to ~$255,000).
*   **The "$9.99 Trap":** Starting at $9.99 attracts price-sensitive users who are unlikely to tolerate *any* increase. This validates the "false traction" risk; you may have volume now, but it is not price-resilient.
*   **Cost Logic Flaw:** You list a "$0.75 per-download cost" but calculate revenue purely on subscriptions. If heavy users download 20 books/month ($15 cost) but only pay $9.99, the library loses $5.01 per user/month. The model requires a usage cap or a flat licensing fee, not a per-download variable cost against a fixed subscription.
