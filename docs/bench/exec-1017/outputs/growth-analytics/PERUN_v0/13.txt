**Recommendation: Do not roll out nationwide yet.**

The experiment design invalidates the "win." You compared two different cities (Seattle vs. Portland) rather than randomly assigning users within the same market. Portland's higher conversion (13.5% vs. 12.0%) likely reflects local market differences, demographics, or competitor activity, not the fee structure. Additionally, checking your math: 5,000 orders at 12% conversion implies ~600 completed transactions? Or is 12% the completion rate of total sessions? Assuming the revenue figures ($150k vs $158.2k) are correct, the variant generated only $8,200 extra revenue on 200 additional orders. With an average fee increase of $0.50 per order across 5,200 orders, the price hike accounts for ~$2,600 of that gain. The rest is volume noise. Without a proper A/B test (randomized control), you cannot attribute the lift to the fee change. Rolling out based on this risks alienating price-sensitive markets that behave like Seattle, not Portland.

**Main Failure Modes:**
1.  **Selection Bias:** The "lift" is purely a Portland market characteristic; applying the fee to Seattle-like markets drops conversion below 12%.
2.  **Hidden Churn:** The 30-day repeat order churn (currently unknown) exceeds the one-time revenue gain, destroying Lifetime Value (LTV).
3.  **Elasticity Misread:** The small sample masks a cliff-edge effect where a specific customer segment stops ordering entirely at >$5.00.

**Stop/Change Triggers:**
*   **Churn > 2%** in the first 30 days post-rollout (measured against baseline repeat rates).
*   **Profit per order < $4.50** after accounting for the fee increase and any support costs from confusion.
*   **Conversion drop > 0.5%** in any major market upon localized rollout.

**Immediate Next Step:** Re-run the test with proper randomization within Seattle and Portland simultaneously for 14 days, pre-committing to a fixed sample size to avoid peeking.
