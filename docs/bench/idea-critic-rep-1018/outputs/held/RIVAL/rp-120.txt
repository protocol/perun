**Go Recommendation:** **NO-GO** in current form. The unit economics are too fragile to present to a CFO without stress-testing the labor variable. While the top-line margin looks healthy at 20%, the model relies entirely on the $5 labor estimate holding true. Without data on actual service duration, you are effectively guessing your largest variable cost. Presenting this as a "20% margin business" rather than a "hypothesis requiring validation" risks credibility if the first 100 bikes reveal a $15 labor cost instead of $5.

**Top Three Collapse Scenarios:**
1.  **Labor Creep:** The $5 estimate assumes a 20-minute tune-up. If real-world wear-and-tear requires 45 minutes (common for neglected bikes), labor costs triple to $15/bike, wiping out 100% of your gross margin and turning every subscriber into a loss leader.
2.  **Adverse Selection:** Your marketing attracts owners of old, high-mileage bikes rather than new owners. These bikes require 3x the parts and labor, causing your $12 parts estimate to skyrocket while churn remains low (because they *need* the service), trapping you in a high-cost, low-margin loop.
3.  **Logistics Friction:** The model assumes efficient routing. If technicians spend 30 minutes driving between scattered subscribers rather than working, your effective hourly labor rate doubles, destroying the unit economics regardless of repair speed.

**Trigger Metric:** **Average Net Profit Per Active Subscriber < $5.00** after Month 3.
*Why:* Your model targets ~$8 profit ($49 - $12 parts - $5 labor - ~$4 overhead buffer). If you drop below $5 by Month 3, it confirms that either labor or parts are structurally higher than modeled, or churn/acquisition costs are eating the buffer. Do not wait six months; early bleed indicates a broken unit model, not a scaling issue.

**What You Are Not Seeing & What I Would Do Differently:**
You are treating the $5 labor cost as a constant rather than a variable dependent on bike age and usage. You also haven't accounted for the "free bike" competitor bundle—they are likely subsidizing the service to sell hardware, a game you cannot win if you are selling service standalone.

**The Fix:** Run a **50-bike beta pilot** immediately with *time-tracking mandatory* for technicians. Do not pitch the 5,000-bike rollout until you have actual mean-time-to-repair data. Price the pilot at $59 to test elasticity and build a buffer.

**Result That Should Make You Abandon the Plan:**
If the pilot reveals that **more than 15% of service calls exceed 40 minutes**, abandon the flat-fee subscription model. It indicates the fleet is too heterogeneous for a fixed price, and you should pivot to a tiered model (Basic vs. Premium) or a pay-per-visit membership to avoid bleeding cash on complex repairs.
