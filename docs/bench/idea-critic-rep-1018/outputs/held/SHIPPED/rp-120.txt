**Recommendation: GO-IF**
Proceed with a capped, 90-day pilot limited to 500 subscribers in a single dense geographic zone. Do not launch the full 5,000-bike rollout until the pilot confirms that the average technician time per call is under 45 minutes and net profit per subscriber exceeds $22/month.

**Top Three Collapse Scenarios**
1.  **Labor Variance Explosion:** Your $5 labor estimate assumes a specific, unverified service duration. If the unknown "average time per call" exceeds 45 minutes due to parts availability or bike complexity, labor costs will double, erasing your 20% margin entirely.
2.  **Adverse Selection & Churn:** Customers who anticipate high repair needs will join first. If actual quarterly churn exceeds 5% (e.g., hits 8% due to poor service speed or high deductibles elsewhere), the cost to acquire replacements will outpace lifetime value, making the $49 price point unsustainable.
3.  **Logistics Drag:** The model ignores travel time and parts logistics between dispersed bikes. Unlike a shop-based model, field service inefficiencies could silently inflate the $12 parts cost (via emergency shipping) and the $5 labor cost (via travel), turning profitable units into loss leaders.

**Trigger Metric (Kill Criteria)**
Abort or re-engineer the program if, after six months of the pilot, the **Average Monthly Net Profit per Active Subscriber falls below $15**. This threshold accounts for a moderate increase in labor costs while preserving enough margin to cover overhead; falling below it indicates the unit economics are fundamentally broken at the $49 price point.

**Critical Blind Spots & Adjustments**
You are betting the entire model on an unquantified variable: technician time. Without this data, your $5 labor figure is a guess, not a cost. I would delay the broad pitch to run a "shadow pilot" tracking actual time-on-task for 50 random repairs immediately to validate the $5 assumption. Furthermore, your competitor comparison is flawed; a free bike bundle changes the customer's total cost of ownership calculation, making your standalone service appear expensive unless you prove superior speed or coverage. To abandon the plan completely, you need evidence that even at maximum efficiency (minimum tech time), the density of bikes in any given area is too low to support profitable travel times.
