**How This Could Fall Apart:**
1.  **Default Rate Sensitivity:** Your $800k profit projection likely collapses if defaults exceed 8-10%. A 5% rough estimate from a neighboring city is not valid evidence for Lagos; if local economic shocks push defaults to 12%, the loan book becomes toxic, wiping out principal and operational costs.
2.  **Hardware Reality Check:** The assumption of "10-year life with no performance drop" is technically fragile in informal settlements. Dust, heat, and theft can degrade panels/batteries in <2 years. If replacement costs hit earlier than year 3, the unit economics break.
3.  **Hidden Operational Drag:** You allocated $250k for trainers but may have underestimated the cost of *collection*. Recovering small weekly payments in informal settlements often requires significant field agent time not captured in "trainer" salaries.

**Early Warning Sign (Kill Criteria):**
Pull the plug immediately if **collections fall below 85% of the due amount for two consecutive months** within the first quarter. This indicates the repayment model is mismatched to local cash flows, making the 3-year loan structure unsustainable.

**Arithmetic & Missing Costs:**
Your math has a glaring gap: **Working Capital for Loan Disbursement**.
*   You listed $250k (trainers) + $300k (spares) = $550k operational costs.
*   You have a $2.5M budget. This leaves ~$1.95M for purchasing and distributing the kits themselves.
*   If targeting 150,000 households, that is ~$13 per kit for hardware and logistics. This is likely insufficient for a solar kit capable of meaningful impact.
*   **Missing Cost:** There is no line item for **loan loss provisions** (bad debt reserve). Standard microfinance models require setting aside 5-10% of the loan portfolio value upfront to cover expected defaults. Without this reserve, a spike in defaults creates an immediate cash flow crisis, preventing you from paying trainers or buying spares.
