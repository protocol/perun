To rigorously test the sensitivity of your smart-pacemaker-lead-integrity monitor given the scarcity of historical failure records, you should generate synthetic data scenarios that simulate the specific electrical signatures of subtle lead fractures. These scenarios must cover the full spectrum of potential failure modes, ranging from early-stage micro-fractures to intermittent connection losses, ensuring your anomaly detection models can distinguish between normal physiological variance and critical faults.

**1. Micro-Fracture Progression Scenarios**
Generate datasets simulating the gradual degradation of lead integrity. Start with baseline normal signals and introduce incremental increases in impedance spikes and signal noise over thousands of cycles. These scenarios should mimic the physical wear of a conductor beginning to fray, characterized by low-amplitude, high-frequency noise bursts that occur sporadically rather than continuously. The goal is to test if the system detects the trend before a complete fracture occurs.

**2. Intermittent "Make-Break" Contact Events**
Create scenarios where the electrical connection is momentarily lost and restored rapidly (milliseconds). These "make-break" events often happen during specific patient movements. Synthesize data where signal loss correlates with simulated high-amplitude myopotential noise (muscle movement) to challenge the system's ability to differentiate between external interference and actual lead failure. Include variations in the duration of the break (e.g., 10ms, 50ms, 100ms) to define the detection threshold.

**3. Oversensing and Undersensing Ambiguity**
Produce signals that mimic fracture-induced noise being misinterpreted as cardiac activity (oversensing) or true cardiac signals being masked by noise (undersensing).
*   **Oversensing:** Inject high-frequency noise spikes that resemble R-waves to see if the system incorrectly inhibits pacing.
*   **Undersensing:** Superimpose fracture noise over low-amplitude intrinsic cardiac signals to test if the system fails to detect the heart's natural rhythm due to signal obscuration.

**4. Postural and Activity-Induced Stress**
Since lead fractures are often position-dependent, generate datasets tied to simulated patient activities. Create signal profiles that remain normal during rest but exhibit characteristic fracture signatures (e.g., sudden impedance shifts) only when specific "postural" parameters are active in the synthetic model. This tests the system's context-awareness and prevents false positives during static monitoring.

**5. Edge-Case Physiological Confounders**
Develop scenarios combining rare cardiac arrhythmias with subtle fracture signatures. For instance, simulate a lead fracture occurring simultaneously with atrial fibrillation or premature ventricular contractions. This ensures the anomaly detector does not attribute the irregular signal morphology solely to the arrhythmia, missing the underlying hardware fault.

**6. Sensor Drift and Baseline Shifts**
Synthesize long-duration recordings where the baseline impedance slowly drifts due to tissue encapsulation or minor lead movement, overlaid with occasional micro-fracture spikes. This tests whether the system can adapt to a shifting baseline without losing sensitivity to acute fracture events.

**Implementation Note:** Ensure every synthetic scenario includes a "ground truth" label indicating the exact onset and duration of the simulated fault to precisely calculate sensitivity and false-positive rates.

**Validation Note:** Prioritize scenarios that blend noise with normal physiology, as pure noise injections often yield overly optimistic performance metrics compared to real-world clinical data.
