BerlinBreath Technologies Team,

Given your critical 48-hour certification window and the life-safety nature of your smart-oxygen-sensor, you must shift from broad functional testing to a targeted "failure-mode" strategy. Since you cannot test everything, prioritize scenarios where the system fails silently or delivers delayed alerts, as these pose the highest risk to patients.

Here is your prioritized list of edge cases to simulate, ranked by immediate patient safety impact:

**1. The "Blackout Gap" (Power Loss During Transmission)**
*   **Scenario:** The device detects a critical low level and initiates an alert sequence, but loses battery power *mid-transmission* before the caregiver receives confirmation.
*   **Why Prioritize:** If the device dies while trying to send an alert, the family receives nothing. You must verify that the device either completes the transmission before shutting down or has a non-volatile memory flag that forces an immediate "missed alert" re-broadcast the second power is restored or a backup kicks in.
*   **Simulation:** Cut power physically at 10%, 50%, and 90% of the transmission cycle.

**2. The "Silent Reboot" Loop (Brownout Conditions)**
*   **Scenario:** Battery voltage drops just below the operational threshold but above the "dead" threshold, causing the device to repeatedly reboot without ever reaching the network stack.
*   **Why Prioritize:** The device appears "on" (LEDs might flicker), but it never connects to send the alarm. This creates a false sense of security.
*   **Simulation:** Use a variable power supply to hover the voltage at the reset threshold for extended periods while tank levels are critical.

**3. The "Store-and-Forward" Delay (Extended Outage)**
*   **Scenario:** Network connectivity is lost for 2 hours while the tank is critical. Connectivity returns briefly for 30 seconds, then drops again.
*   **Why Prioritize:** Does the device send the alert immediately upon the brief return? Or does it wait for a "stable" connection that never comes? In home care, a 30-second window must be sufficient to push the critical alert.
*   **Simulation:** Introduce intermittent network availability (e.g., 30s on, 5m off) during a critical event.

**4. The "Time-Drift" Failure (RTC Loss on Battery Death)**
*   **Scenario:** The device loses power completely, then restores. When it finally sends the delayed alert, the timestamp is incorrect or missing.
*   **Why Prioritize:** Caregivers may ignore an alert if the timestamp suggests it is old or erroneous. Accurate timing is crucial for triage.
*   **Simulation:** Drain the battery fully, wait 24 hours, restore power, and check the metadata of the resulting alert.

**5. The "Full Buffer" Deadlock**
*   **Scenario:** The device logs extensive diagnostic data during a network outage. When the network returns, the attempt to upload this large log block prevents the high-priority alarm from being sent first.
*   **Why Prioritize:** Low-priority data should never block high-priority safety alerts.
*   **Simulation:** Fill the local storage with dummy logs, trigger an outage, then restore network and monitor transmission queue order.

**Execution Strategy for 48 Hours:**
Do not run these sequentially. With eight developers, split into four pairs. Each pair takes one scenario above and runs it against three different network conditions (Wi-Fi, Cellular, and Edge/2G) simultaneously. Document only the pass/fail status and the exact time-to-alert. If any scenario results in a missed alert, stop and fix that specific race condition immediately before moving to the next iteration.

Focus strictly on the path from "Critical Level Detected" to "Caregiver Phone Rings." Everything else is secondary for this certification.

*Ensure your test logs capture exact timestamps synchronized to an external clock to prove latency metrics to the certifiers.*
*Have a "go/no-go" criteria defined before hour 1; if the "Blackout Gap" fails, the launch risk may be too high regardless of other tests.*
