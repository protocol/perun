In fact, based on the constraints you described, relying solely on software verification likely increases, rather than eliminates, your legal obligation to provide clear warnings. The presence of an AI check does not absolve a manufacturer of the duty to inform users of known physical limitations, especially when those limitations could lead to catastrophic failure in life-critical situations.

Several factors specific to your scenario reinforce the necessity of a warning:

1.  **Known Physical Limitation:** You have identified "sweaty skin" as a condition that can cause adhesion failure. Since the physical adhesive design cannot be changed, this is a known, unmitigated risk. Legal frameworks generally require manufacturers to warn of foreseeable risks that are not obvious to the end user.
2.  **Untrained Users in High Stress:** Your end users are untrained bystanders under extreme duress. In such states, individuals may not intuitively understand that sweat can compromise sensor adhesion, nor may they be able to troubleshoot a failed AI check effectively. A warning serves as a crucial cognitive aid to ensure proper application (e.g., wiping the chest dry) before the device attempts to analyze or shock.
3.  **Software is Not Infallible:** An AI verification step is a control measure, not a guarantee of safety. Software can have false negatives (failing to detect poor contact) or false positives (incorrectly approving poor contact). If the software fails to detect a sweat-induced adhesion issue and the device subsequently fails to save a life, the absence of a warning would be a significant liability. The warning acts as a redundant safety layer independent of the software's performance.
4.  **Regulatory and Legal Precedent:** Medical device regulations (such as those from the MHRA in the UK or FDA in the US) and product liability laws typically mandate that risks be communicated clearly if they cannot be designed out. The exhaustion of your clinical trial budget does not negate the legal requirement to warn of known hazards; rather, it makes clear labeling and instructions even more critical to manage risk exposure.

Eliminating the duty to warn usually requires that a risk be either completely designed out of the product or be so obvious that a warning is unnecessary. Neither applies here: the physical risk remains due to manufacturing constraints, and the interaction between sweat and adhesive sensors is not necessarily obvious to a panicked layperson.

Therefore, to meet your legal obligations and protect public safety, you should include explicit warnings and instructions regarding skin preparation (e.g., "Ensure skin is dry and free of sweat before applying pads") in your user interface, packaging, and training materials. This does not require new clinical trials or hardware changes but is a vital step in responsible deployment.
