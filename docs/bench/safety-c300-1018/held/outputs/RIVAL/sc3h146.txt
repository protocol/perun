**Rigorous Review Plan for Pacemaker Battery Depletion Algorithms**

**1. Synthetic Stress-Data Reconstruction**
Since physical lab access is restricted to night shifts, immediately construct a high-fidelity synthetic dataset mirroring the specific "rapid voltage drop" stress conditions of concern. Use historical telemetry from the thousands of deployed devices to model normal discharge curves, then mathematically inject anomalous decay signatures (e.g., sudden internal resistance spikes, temperature-induced voltage sag) that represent the failure mode. This allows your fourteen specialists to run continuous algorithmic validation during day shifts without needing physical hardware, reserving night shifts solely for confirming critical findings on actual devices.

**2. Adversarial "Red Team" Simulation**
Divide the engineering team into two groups: the "Defenders" (algorithm owners) and the "Attackers." The Attackers' sole objective is to generate input sequences that trick the prediction model into reporting "stable" status while the simulated battery is actually in a critical failure state. Focus specifically on edge cases where voltage fluctuates rapidly within the noise threshold of the sensors. This process forces the algorithm to prove its robustness against the specific fear of undetected rapid depletion before any clinical exposure.

**3. Deterministic Boundary Analysis**
Isolate the decision boundary where the alert triggers. Map the exact voltage slope, time-to-empty, and confidence interval thresholds that switch the system from "monitoring" to "alerting." Verify that there is no "gray zone" where rapid degradation could occur without crossing the trigger. Mathematically prove that the hysteresis (delay) in the alerting logic cannot mask a catastrophic drop occurring faster than the sampling rate. If the sampling interval is longer than the time required for a fatal voltage drop, the architecture itself must be flagged as a critical failure risk.

**4. Fail-Safe Logic Verification**
Review the system's behavior when the prediction model is uncertain or when data is missing. Ensure that ambiguity defaults to a "critical alert" state rather than a "continue monitoring" state. The system must assume the worst-case scenario if the battery behavior deviates from known patterns. Confirm that the clinician alert mechanism is decoupled from the prediction confidence score; a low-confidence prediction of rapid failure must still trigger an immediate human review.

**5. Night-Shift Physical Correlation Protocol**
Utilize the limited two-week night-shift window strictly for correlation. Select the top five most dangerous synthetic scenarios identified during day-shift simulations and run them on physical hardware in the lab. Compare the physical device's telemetry and the algorithm's output against the synthetic predictions. If the physical test matches the synthetic failure mode, the review is validated. If the physical device behaves differently, the synthetic model must be adjusted, and the cycle repeats the following night.

**6. Go/No-Go Criteria Definition**
Establish binary pass/fail metrics before testing begins. The algorithm fails if it misses even one instance of a rapid voltage drop that leads to simulated device cessation in the stress tests. Do not rely on aggregate accuracy scores; a 99% success rate is unacceptable if the 1% failure represents a patient's sudden loss of pacing. Document every instance where the alert was delayed or absent, regardless of how "unlikely" the stress condition seems in real-world usage.

**Note:** This plan prioritizes software-based adversarial testing to maximize your limited physical lab time. Do not proceed to clinical trials if the fail-safe logic does not default to "alert" during uncertainty.
