**Internal Validation Protocol for Fall Hazard Detection**

**1. Construct a Low-Cost "Home Environment" Matrix**
Since you cannot purchase new devices, maximize your existing resources by creating a standardized testing grid. Divide your eight team members into pairs to simulate four distinct home archetypes common in your target demographic:
*   **The Cluttered Urban Apartment:** Focus on tight spaces, extension cords, and stacked items.
*   **The Suburban Living Room:** Focus on area rugs, low coffee tables, and pet toys.
*   **The Dimly Lit Bedroom:** Focus on low-contrast items (e.g., dark socks on dark carpet) and shadows.
*   **The High-Traffic Kitchen:** Focus on wet floors, scattered mats, and dropped utensils.

Use items already present in your team members' actual homes or office space. Do not stage perfect scenarios; intentionally create "messy" conditions that reflect real-life urgency where patients might not clear hazards perfectly.

**2. Execute the "Blind Walkthrough" Procedure**
To simulate the end-user experience accurately, the person holding the phone must not know the location of the hazards beforehand.
*   **Setup:** One team member (the "Setter") places 10–15 specific hazards in a room, including at least three "critical misses" (low-contrast rugs, transparent objects, or items blending into flooring). The Setter documents these locations with a simple sketch or photo on a separate device.
*   **Execution:** A different team member (the "Scanner"), acting as an elderly patient or tired caregiver, scans the room using the app. They must move naturally, varying their walking speed and camera height to mimic unsteady hands or limited mobility.
*   **Constraint:** The Scanner must not receive hints. If the app fails to flag a hazard, they should proceed as a real user would—potentially missing the danger.

**3. Document False Negatives Rigorously**
Create a shared spreadsheet to log every test run. Essential columns must include:
*   **Hazard Type:** (e.g., "Low-contrast rug," "Clear plastic bin").
*   **Lighting Condition:** (e.g., "Natural light," "Evening lamp," "Flash on/off").
*   **Camera Angle:** (e.g., "Waist height," "Looking down," "Glancing side-view").
*   **Result:** Detected (True Positive) or Missed (False Negative).
*   **Severity:** Rate the miss as "Critical" (high fall risk) or "Minor."

Pay special attention to **low-contrast failures**. If the app misses a beige rug on a tan floor, mark this as a critical architectural flaw. These are the most dangerous false negatives for your specific user base.

**4. Iterate with "Adversarial" Tuning**
After the first round of 20–30 scans, hold a 30-minute review. Identify patterns in the misses. Did the app fail only in low light? Only with soft edges?
*   **Adjustment:** Without changing code yet, adjust the *usage instructions*. If the app fails when held too low, can your UI guide the user to hold it higher?
*   **Re-test:** Run a second round focusing exclusively on the failure patterns identified. This confirms if the issue is environmental or a hard model limitation.

**5. Final Go/No-Go Criteria**
Establish a strict threshold for beta release. For example: "The app must detect 90% of high-contrast hazards and 70% of low-contrast hazards in varied lighting." If the internal data falls below this, delay the beta. It is safer to delay than to release a tool that gives false confidence to vulnerable patients.

*Note: Ensure all test photos/videos are deleted immediately after analysis to protect privacy, even in internal settings.*
*This process relies on human observation to validate the AI, effectively substituting expensive automated testing rigs with structured manual rigor.*
