**Risk Mitigation Plan: Brake Detection Reliability Verification**

**1. Define Failure Modes and Safety States**
Explicitly document the "fail-safe" default: if the sensor signal is ambiguous, noisy, or absent, the lift mechanism must remain disabled. Define the specific failure mode where the system falsely interprets an unlocked brake as locked (False Positive). Establish that the only acceptable risk is a False Negative (system refuses to lift when brakes are actually locked), as this prevents movement rather than causing an unsecured lift.

**2. Characterize the Three Test Models as Archetypes**
Since testing all field models is impossible, select your three available wheelchair models to represent the extremes of mechanical variance found in the field:
*   **Model A (The Baseline):** A standard, modern wheelchair with typical lever-brake geometry and common metal finishes.
*   **Model B (The Outlier - Mechanical):** An older or heavy-duty model with non-standard brake lever positions, unusual spring tension, or distinct mechanical linkage play.
*   **Model C (The Outlier - Material/Environmental):** A model with materials that interfere with your specific sensor type (e.g., carbon fiber if using inductive sensors, or matte black finishes if using optical sensors) or one prone to accumulating debris near the brake assembly.

**3. Conduct "Boundary Stress" Testing on Archetypes**
Do not just test the "brakes locked" and "brakes unlocked" states. Test the transition zones where errors occur:
*   **Partial Engagement:** Test the system when brakes are 50%, 75%, and 90% engaged. Record the sensor threshold where the system flips from "unsafe" to "safe."
*   **Vibration and Shift:** Simulate the vibration of a motorized bed or staff movement. Verify that transient sensor noise does not trigger a false "locked" signal during the engagement check.
*   **Environmental Interference:** Introduce common nursing home contaminants (linen fibers, dust, moisture) to the sensor area on all three models to ensure they do not mimic a brake lever's presence.

**4. Develop a "Gap Analysis" Matrix for Untested Models**
Create a checklist for nursing home staff to visually verify compatibility before using untested wheelchairs. This matrix should compare untested field models against your three archetypes based on:
*   Brake lever shape and size.
*   Location of the brake mechanism relative to the wheel hub.
*   Material composition of the brake assembly.
*   Presence of aftermarket modifications (e.g., extended lever handles).
If a field model matches an archetype in all categories, it inherits that archetype's validation. If it differs significantly, it is flagged for manual staff verification (physical tug-test) before use.

**5. Implement a Dual-Verification Protocol**
Until broader testing is completed, mandate a procedural control:
*   **System Check:** The device performs its automated sensor scan.
*   **Human Confirmation:** Staff must physically attempt to roll the wheelchair slightly *before* the patient is transferred. If the wheelchair moves, the brakes are not engaged, regardless of what the sensor indicates. This human-in-the-loop step acts as the primary mitigation for sensor false positives on untested models.

**6. Document Residual Risk and Monitoring**
Formalize a reporting mechanism for any near-misses involving untested models. Log the specific wheelchair make/model and the sensor reading versus the actual state. Use this data to refine the sensor thresholds or update the "Gap Analysis" matrix post-certification.

**Note:** Prioritize the "human confirmation" step as your primary safety net for untested models until more data is gathered. Ensure your certification documentation explicitly lists the three tested archetypes and the logic used to extrapolate safety to other models.
