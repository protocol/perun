**Synthetic Data Scenarios for Pacemaker Lead Integrity Validation**

Given the scarcity of historical failure records, your validation must rely on high-fidelity synthetic data that mimics the physiological and electrical complexity of rare lead fractures. Generate the following scenario classes to test sensitivity to subtle signal interruptions:

1.  **Intermittent Micro-Fractures (The "Flicker" Effect):**
    *   **Scenario:** Simulate transient open-circuit events lasting only 10–50 milliseconds, occurring randomly within the cardiac cycle (e.g., during the T-wave or QRS complex).
    *   **Goal:** Test if the model distinguishes these from normal sensing noise or myopotential interference. These are the most likely to cause missed pacing or inappropriate inhibition.

2.  **Insulation Breach with Variable Impedance:**
    *   **Scenario:** Model gradual insulation degradation causing erratic impedance drops that fluctuate with patient movement (simulated via signal modulation) but return to baseline when "static."
    *   **Goal:** Ensure the system detects the trend of instability rather than waiting for a hard threshold breach, which may never occur before a clinical event.

3.  **Connector Pin Micro-Motion:**
    *   **Scenario:** Create signal artifacts characterized by high-frequency spikes superimposed on the near-field electrogram, mimicking loose set-screw contact that resolves spontaneously.
    *   **Goal:** Differentiate mechanical connection issues from true lead body fractures or far-field R-wave oversensing.

4.  **Fracture During High-Output Pacing:**
    *   **Scenario:** Simulate fractures that only manifest when the device delivers high-voltage therapy or high-output pacing, causing immediate post-shock signal loss or noise reversion.
    *   **Goal:** Verify detection logic holds under high-stress electrical conditions where signal-to-noise ratios shift dramatically.

5.  **Physiological Confounders (False Positive Traps):**
    *   **Scenario:** Generate robust datasets of non-fracture anomalies that mimic fractures, including severe myopotential inhibition (pectoral muscle flex), external electromagnetic interference (EMI) from MRI/diathermy simulations, and T-wave oversensing.
    *   **Goal:** Rigorously test specificity. A system that flags every noise artifact as a fracture will cause unnecessary clinical alerts and patient anxiety.

6.  **Lead Maturation/Healing Artifacts:**
    *   **Scenario:** Simulate the changing signal morphology typical of the first 30–90 days post-implant (fibrosis formation) to ensure the model does not flag normal healing variations as early fractures.
    *   **Goal:** Prevent high false-positive rates in the immediate post-implant window.

**Implementation Note:** For each scenario, vary the signal amplitude, noise floor, and patient heart rate to create a multidimensional test matrix. Since you cannot verify "safety" with synthetic data alone, pair these simulations with a **human-in-the-loop gate**: any alert generated by the model for a "rare fracture" in a clinical trial setting must be reviewed by a specialist before any patient notification or device adjustment occurs. Do not assert a specific detection rate (e.g., "99% accurate") without clinical validation; label performance metrics on synthetic data as **UNVERIFIED** regarding real-world clinical sensitivity.

**Constraint Adherence:** Your strict limit on historical data means your synthetic generation must be adversarial—specifically designed to break the model, not just train it. Prioritize the "Intermittent Micro-Fractures" and "Physiological Confounders" as these represent the highest risk of harm (missed diagnosis) and operational failure (alert fatigue).
