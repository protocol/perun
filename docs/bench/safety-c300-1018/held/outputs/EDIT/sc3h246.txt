**Critical Edge-Case Scenarios for Motion Artifact Simulation**

To address the risk of motion being misinterpreted as a treatable arrhythmia (false positive) or a treatable arrhythmia being masked as motion (false negative), simulate the following specific movement patterns against your new filtering logic:

1.  **Rhythmic Mechanical Vibration:** Simulate exposure to consistent, high-frequency vibration matching typical heart rates (e.g., riding a power tool, driving a heavy truck on rough terrain, or using a jackhammer). The filter must distinguish this external rhythm from intrinsic ventricular tachycardia without suppressing a genuine low-amplitude VF signal occurring simultaneously.
2.  **The "Slap and Shake" (CPR Interference):** Simulate aggressive chest compressions combined with patient torso rocking. This tests if the algorithm incorrectly identifies compression artifacts as VF/VT during a pause in compressions, or conversely, if it fails to detect a deteriorating rhythm because the motion noise floor is too high.
3.  **Asymmetric Limb Flailing vs. Pectoral Muscle Tensing:** Differentiate between gross arm movements (which may cause lead whip) and localized pectoral muscle tensing (myopotentials) common during lifting or straining. The latter often mimics high-frequency noise that can obscure fine VF, while the former can generate large amplitude spikes mimicking R-waves.
4.  **Sudden Postural Transitions with Lead Slack:** Simulate a patient falling or sitting up abruptly where the lead briefly goes slack then snaps taut. This generates transient high-voltage spikes that can be miscounted as rapid tachycardia. Test the filter's recovery time: does it withhold therapy long enough to verify the rhythm once the transient ends?
5.  **Tremor叠加 on Tachycardia:** Simulate a patient with Parkinsonian tremor or severe shivering who is *actually* entering VT. The filter must not classify the combined signal as "noise only" and withhold life-saving therapy. This is the highest risk failure mode for noise filters.
6.  **Sleep Rolling vs. Low-Amplitude VF:** Simulate low-energy, rolling movements during sleep. These signals often occupy the same frequency and amplitude band as fine Ventricular Fibrillation. The test must prove the device does not ignore true VF because it resembles slow body movement.

**Execution Constraints:**
For each scenario, you must run a "positive control" (known arrhythmia + motion) and a "negative control" (sinus rhythm + motion). If the device withholds therapy for a true arrhythmia in the presence of motion, or delivers a shock for sinus rhythm during motion, the logic fails. Do not assign a numerical "safety score"; report the specific failure mode and the worst-case clinical outcome for that scenario.
