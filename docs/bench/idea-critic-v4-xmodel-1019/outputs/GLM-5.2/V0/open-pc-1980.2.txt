**Steelman:** An open, commodity personal computer reaches the market in ~12 months with minimal capital, lets outside software and retail grow the ecosystem, and positions the mainframe maker for the segment shift without risking its core business.

**Strongest attack survived:** "No retail experience and outside software dependence mean the company cannot control the customer experience, so the product fails." This fails because the proposal deliberately uses independent retail and outside software as the distribution and ecosystem strategy; the dependence is the design, not a flaw.

**Assumptions:**
1. The small-computer segment is durable growth, not a fad — **assumed**.
2. The company's mainframe margins and customer base remain intact during the PC effort — **assumed**.
3. Outside software and retail partners will support a new entrant — **assumed**.

**Better ways:**
1. **Do nothing.** Zero cost, zero risk, but concedes the fastest-growing segment entirely.
2. **Acquire a small-computer maker.** Buys retail and software expertise, but integration risk and a ~12-month timeline make it more expensive and not faster.
3. **Build inside the mainframe division with existing processes.** Slower, culture-bound, and likely to produce a closed, over-engineered product at mainframe margins.

**Kill criteria:**
1. If the PC is not demonstrable within 18 months, stop the separate team.
2. If outside software partners have not committed at least three titles within 12 months of launch, the open-design thesis is dead — pivot to in-house software or exit.

**Remaining risk:** Brand dilution if the PC underwhelms; low margins may never improve without software revenue.

**Dissent ledger:** Margin and control concerns are real but are features of the open-commodity model, not blockers.

DECISION: GO
