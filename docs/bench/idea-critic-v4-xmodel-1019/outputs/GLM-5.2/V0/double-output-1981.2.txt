The strongest case for doubling output: production scale drives unit cost down, dealers locked into inventory must keep selling your car, and aggressive supply signals confidence to investors and suppliers. That is the steelman.

Against it: the company has 4,500 unsold cars already — more than a full year of sales at the current run-rate. Doubling production means adding roughly 1,250 cars/month to a pile that is already growing. Every unsold car is frozen cash. With limited cash on hand, rising interest rates, and a recession eroding demand, the financing burden compounds precisely when the ability to carry inventory is weakest. Dealers facing the same high rates and softening demand cannot absorb more stock; pushing it onto them risks channel collapse or forced discounting that destroys margin and brand.

The proposal conflates production capability with market absorption. Scale economies only help if the units sell; otherwise they multiply the loss per period. The "lock in the dealers" argument is real but cuts both ways — dealers drowning in unsold inventory stop ordering and may switch allegiance.

Cheaper alternative: hold production flat or trim it, use available cash to extend dealer financing terms or stimulate retail demand, and wait for the macro picture to clarify. This preserves optionality at far lower cash burn.

Kill criteria the proposal lacks: a defined ceiling on unsold inventory (say, 60 days of sales), a floor on realized retail price below which production halts, and a cash runway trigger. Without these, doubling output is an irreversible cash commitment into a deteriorating market with no stated exit.

The recession, the rates, the existing 4,500-car overhang, and the thin cash position together form a concrete failure scenario the proposal does not address.

DECISION: NO-GO
