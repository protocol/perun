**Steelman:** A sub-scale computer maker can enter the consumer market with near-zero capital at risk by licensing its platform to a partner with toy/game distribution, earning recurring per-unit royalties while the licensee bears marketing, inventory and channel cost.

**Strongest attack survived:** The licensee will under-market the product because it bears the cost but the licensor captures brand upside, leaving the platform stranded in a category it does not own. This fails as a blocker: the royalty structure means the licensor's downside is capped at lost opportunity, not capital loss, and the licensee's own margin incentive constrains free-riding. The brand-association risk is real but cuts both ways — weak association protects the core computer brand if the console flops.

**Assumptions:**
- The platform is technically suitable for a low-cost game/internet console without expensive re-engineering.
- The licensee has the capital and retail relationships to actually ship at volume.
- Per-unit royalty, even small, exceeds the marginal cost of licensing support.

**Better ways:**
- **Do nothing.** Status quo: small computer market share, no consumer play, no royalty stream, no brand risk. Cheapest; zero reversibility cost.
- **Build in-house.** Higher capital and marketing risk; greater control and brand capture. Reversible only at write-off cost.

**Kill criteria:** If unit shipments in the first holiday season fall below the licensee's own volume forecast, or if royalty revenue in year one is below the licensor's platform-support cost, terminate or renegotiate the license.

**Remaining risk:** The licensor is structurally dependent on a partner whose incentives are not fully aligned, and small per-unit royalties may not cover ongoing platform obligations if the product underperforms but does not fail outright.

DECISION: GO
