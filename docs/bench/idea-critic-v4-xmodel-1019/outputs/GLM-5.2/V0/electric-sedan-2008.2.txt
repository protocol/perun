The strongest case for the proposal: betting everything on electric when the world runs on gas is exactly how a new entrant without combustion-engine legacy wins; a big factory is the only way to get to the volume that makes battery cost fall.

The sharpest attack: the company is betting its whole existence on two things it has never done — build a car at scale, and manage a battery supply chain — while starting with a capital base that the proposal itself admits is far too small. The factory does not solve the experience gap; it amplifies it. And if the battery cost curve is wrong, there is no margin to absorb it. This is a one-way-door decision: committing to BEV-only cannot be quietly unwound after the factory is bought and tooling sunk. The proposal names the risks but gives no kill criterion, no capital ceiling, no fallback if the battery curve or factory ramp fails. "We need more capital than we have" is not a risk to manage later; it is the plan's first failure mode, and nothing here shows it is closed.

Do-nothing is not free either: without a factory and a committed platform, the company stays a design shop, burns its cash on prototypes, and never reaches the volume where battery costs fall — which is the whole thesis. So the status quo loses slowly, and the proposal loses fast if capital or ramp breaks. That still does not approve as proposed: the bet may be right, but the proposal as written asks for a blank check with no stop condition.

A sound version would cap the first capital tranche, set a factory-ramp milestone with a date, and name the pivot (license the platform, partner on production) if the ramp or battery curve misses. That is a revise-first call, not a go.

DECISION: NO-GO
