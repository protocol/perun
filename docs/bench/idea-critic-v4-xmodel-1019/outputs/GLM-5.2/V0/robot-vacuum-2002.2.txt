The steelman: a radically cheaper robot beats a smarter one because consumers want floors swept with zero effort, not a map; random coverage is "good enough" at a fraction of the cost.

**Skeptics' attacks (all survived):**
- *"Random coverage misses spots."* True, but the bar is not perfect cleaning — it is perceptibly cleaner floors with zero human effort. The risk is real but non-fatal.
- *"Mapping rivals look smarter."* They also cost 5-10× more. The low price reframes the purchase from "precision appliance" to "why not try it?" Impulse-buy economics beat feature-comparison economics at this price gap.
- *"No consumer-product experience."* This is the sharpest objection. The company can build robots, but retail channels, warranty handling, and consumer marketing are a different muscle. However: the two floor-care companies' rejection proves the *channel* strategy (partner with incumbents) is dead — going direct is not optional, it is the only remaining path.
- *"Buyers won't trust a robot in the home."* The low price lowers trust requirements. A $200 experiment is forgivable; a $2,000 commitment demands trust the brand cannot supply.

**The one thing that would sink this:** if random coverage is so poor that early buyers return the product or warn friends, the low price becomes evidence of cheapness rather than value. This is the kill criterion: track return rates and word-of-mouth in the first 90 days; if returns exceed ~20%, the coverage strategy is wrong and the product must be pulled or redesigned.

**Why GO now:** the prototype exists, the price gap is large, the channel-partner path is closed, and consumer robotics is an empty category — first mover captures the narrative. Waiting only lets mapping rivals get cheaper. The downside is a failed product launch (survivable); the upside is creating a consumer-robotics market from nothing.

DECISION: GO
