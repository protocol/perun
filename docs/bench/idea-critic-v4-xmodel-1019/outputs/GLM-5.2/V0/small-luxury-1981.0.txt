Steelman: a compact on a shared mass-market platform, premium-trimmed and sold under the luxury marque, captures buyers already leaving American brands for smaller sedans — the fastest-growing segment of the luxury market.

Strongest attack survived: "buyers see through the shared platform and the premium collapses." It fails because the evidence says the target buyer is cross-shopping American-brand compacts, not European luxury cars. Against a Ford Escort or Chevy Citation, a Nissan Sentra underpinnings with leather, better trim, and a prestige badge is not a transparent compromise — it is an upgrade. The platform-sharing risk only bites if the comparison set is Mercedes or BMW, which the data says it is not.

Better way considered: build a clean-sheet compact on a unique platform. Rejected: the entire margin logic of the proposal depends on amortized tooling. A unique platform at compact scale, at 1981 luxury volumes, almost certainly loses money per unit. "Do nothing" cedes the segment entirely to American brands and the Japanese compacts arriving in the US market.

Kill criteria: if the compact fails to reach a unit-volume threshold within 18 months of launch, or if dealer surveys show salespeople refusing to use the marque name, pull the trim package back to a lower-volume, higher-margin positioning rather than continuing to push volume through a diluted badge.

The prestige-dilution risk is real but bounded: one compact model does not reframe a marque built on larger sedans and grand tourers, provided the flagship line stays uncompromised. The salesperson-naming concern is a training and incentive problem, not a structural flaw in the product.

The proposal reads the buyer shift correctly, prices the platform-sharing risk against the right comparison set, and uses shared tooling to make a small luxury car profitable at a volume a clean-sheet platform could not sustain.

DECISION: GO
