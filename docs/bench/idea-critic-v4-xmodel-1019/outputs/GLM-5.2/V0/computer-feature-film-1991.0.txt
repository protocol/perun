Strongest case for the deal: a feature cements the company's technology as a medium rather than a novelty, and the publisher relationship is the only realistic path to distribution for a firm with no feature track record.

But the proposal as stated is missing the one thing that determines whether this is survivable: **deal economics and IP control.** The listed risks are real but manageable if the terms are right; they are fatal if the terms are wrong. "The studio as publisher" with "the larger partner controls distribution" describes a structure in which the small company bears production risk — slow, costly rendering on a longer-than-short timeline — while the large studio captures distribution upside and can dictate marketing, release windows, and sequels. A small graphics firm that has never made a feature could easily commit to a multi-year build, miss milestones, and lose both the film and the underlying characters to the publisher's contracts.

The counterfactual matters: turning down the deal preserves optionality but also preserves the status quo — short films and commercials — in a niche that may not scale. Doing nothing is cheap today but may foreclose the only window in which a computer-animated feature is a first-mover advantage rather than a late entry.

The deal should not be approved as proposed. It needs a term-sheet gate: minimum guaranteed payment covering production cost, the small company retaining story and character IP, a cap on the publisher's distribution cut, and a reversion of rights if the studio does not release within a fixed window. Without those four terms, the proposal asks the executive team to accept all downside and concede the upside to a partner that already tried to poach its lead animator.

DECISION: NO-GO
