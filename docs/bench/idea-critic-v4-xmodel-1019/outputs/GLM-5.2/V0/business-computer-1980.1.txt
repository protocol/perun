The strongest case for this proposal: the current product's limitations are not speculative—they are the documented reason business buyers reject it. Adding lowercase and an 80-column display directly closes that gap. If the new machine ships on time and works, it captures a market the current product cannot serve. That is a real opportunity, not an invented one.

But the proposal asks the company to bet its revenue on an unproven design, built on a tight schedule, while phasing out the product that pays everyone's salary. Three of the four named risks are existential: the hardware may not be reliable, the schedule may slip, and existing software vendors may not port their titles. The fourth—ending the current product prematurely—turns a manageable risk into a binary one. If the new machine is delayed or defective, the company has neither the old revenue nor the new one. The proposal provides no evidence that the schedule is realistic, no fallback if the hardware fails, and no transition plan that protects current customers and developers. Phasing out the successful product before the successor is proven in the field is a one-way-door decision made as if it were reversible.

The cheaper path exists: build the business machine, but keep the current product in production and the developer base intact until the new hardware has shipped, sold, and demonstrated reliability in customers' hands. This preserves the option to course-correct if the schedule slips or the hardware underperforms. The cost of that hedge is modest—continued production of a known-good product—and it eliminates the catastrophic failure mode where both products fail simultaneously.

A sound version of this proposal would commit to the new machine as an addition, set a kill criterion tied to a hardware reliability milestone before any phase-out begins, and protect the current revenue stream during the transition.

DECISION: NO-GO
