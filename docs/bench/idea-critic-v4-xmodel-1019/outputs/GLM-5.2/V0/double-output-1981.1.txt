The strongest case for doubling production: a young carmaker must reach scale to survive, recession is temporary, and building inventory now positions the company for the recovery.

That case fails on the company's own numbers. It has built 7,500 cars and sold 3,000: 4,000 unsold units, over a year of inventory at the current sales rate. Doubling monthly production accelerates the gap between what is built and what is sold. Every unsold car ties up cash in steel, labor, and warehousing — cash the company has already said is limited.

The recession and high interest rates do not merely reduce demand; they attack both sides of the equation simultaneously. Consumers face higher loan payments and defer purchases. Dealers face higher floor-plan financing costs and cannot absorb additional stock. The proposal asks the company to spend scarce cash building cars that fewer people can buy and that dealers cannot finance — during the exact window when carrying costs are rising fastest.

The alternatives are cheaper and reversible: hold production flat and offer dealer or consumer financing incentives to move the existing 4,000-unit overhang; or cut production to match current sales, conserving cash until demand signals recover. Either preserves the option to scale up later without first burning through reserves.

The kill criterion writes itself: if the company doubles production and the sales rate does not at least double within one quarter, the incremental inventory will exceed financing capacity and force a distress sale or shutdown. Nothing in the proposal addresses how the additional output would be sold, financed, or stored. Doubling production into a recession with a large and growing unsold inventory, limited cash, and constrained dealers is a bet on timing the company cannot afford to lose.

DECISION: NO-GO
