Strongest claim: a one-year, skunkworks PC built from off-the-shelf parts, an open architecture and outside software, sold through retail, lets an incumbent enter a fast-growing adjacent market before the window closes.

The sharpest attack: standard parts plus an open design mean the only durable asset is brand and distribution — yet the company has no retail experience and is ceding the software layer. Rivals copy the hardware; the incumbent becomes a low-margin box-maker in a channel it does not control. That is not a feature gap, it is a moat gap: the proposal bets on exactly the things the company is weakest at (retail, outside relationships, low-margin volume) and gives away the thing it is best at (vertical integration).

Against "do nothing": doing nothing preserves mainframe margins but concedes the segment that is cannibalizing the installed base — a real cost, but survivable for years. The cheaper alternative — an internal skunkworks prototype without a public retail commitment — buys learning and optionality at a fraction of the blast radius and reversibility cost. The proposal skips that step and goes straight to a public, open, retail-channel launch.

Pre-mortem: it is 1982 and the PC is a modest success in units but a strategic failure — margins diluted, no software lock-in, clones everywhere, and the mainframe business now has a competitor inside the house for engineering talent and management attention.

Kill criteria are absent: the proposal states no metric at which management stops, pivots or shuts down. "Build it in a year" is a schedule, not a kill switch.

The idea is directionally right — the incumbent cannot ignore small computers. But as proposed it is an irreversible, open-architecture, retail-channel bet with no stop condition and a moat that works against the company's own strengths. Approving it as written commits to the riskiest path before a cheaper probe is attempted.

DECISION: NO-GO
