The strongest version of this claim: a studio that just tried to poach your lead animator is now offering a deal instead — that shift signals they value what the team can do more than any single hire. First-mover advantage in a new medium is rare and defensible.

But the risks are not balanced; they are lopsided against the small company. The studio controls distribution, holds the stronger negotiating hand, and the small company has never produced anything longer than a short. Rendering costs for a feature are an order of magnitude beyond anything they have run. No one has shipped an all-CGI feature, so there is no proven pipeline, budget, or timeline — only hope.

The sharpest objection: the studio that tried to hire away the lead animator may be structuring this deal to acquire the team's output at a discount, capture distribution upside, and lock in IP — all while the small company bears the production risk and cost overruns. If the small company cannot self-distribute or walk away with the IP, the deal is a slow-motion acquisition on unfavorable terms. That objection survives because the proposal names no floor on the small company's share, no IP reversion, and no cap on the studio's downside.

Cheaper alternative: negotiate a shorter pilot or co-production first to prove the pipeline and establish leverage before committing to a feature-length deal. The proposal does not reject this alternative; it ignores it.

The proposal has no kill criteria, no budget cap, no IP terms, and no fallback if rendering proves infeasible at scale. It asks the small company to bet everything on an unproven medium with the larger partner holding the cards. The upside is real, but the structure is wrong: accept the bet only with IP protection, a capped first step, and a distribution floor — none of which are in this proposal.

DECISION: NO-GO
