The strongest version of the claim: browser-based maps with satellite imagery and routing becomes the default way people find places, and the company's search dominance lets it win distribution for free.

Attack that. The distribution advantage is real but not decisive: established map sites have the users and the data relationships. The cost risk is understated — licensing map data and satellite imagery at web scale, with no revenue model, is a sustained cash burn, not a one-time bet. Browser graphics limitations in 2004 are a technical risk that could make the product meaningfully worse than the desktop original. Each of these is a concrete scenario the proposal does not address.

But the alternatives are worse. Doing nothing leaves a acquired asset unused while competitors extend their lead. Building a paid product limits adoption and underuses the search funnel. The free-browser play is the only path that could make maps a mass-market utility and feed search intent — the strategic upside dwarfs the licensing cost if the company can subsidize it from its core business. The browser-speed objection is the weakest: it is a performance problem, not a feasibility wall, and the pace of browser improvement makes it transient.

The proposal's real gap is not the risks it names — those are manageable — but the absence of a kill criterion. Without one, a costly, revenue-free project can drift. The fix is a time-boxed pilot with a defined stop: build the browser version for a limited geography, measure whether users adopt and return at rates that justify the licensing spend, and kill or expand at a fixed date.

Given the strategic fit, the asymmetry of upside versus cost for a company with search-scale revenue, and the reversibility of a pilot, the call is to proceed — gated on a pilot with a kill date.

DECISION: GO
