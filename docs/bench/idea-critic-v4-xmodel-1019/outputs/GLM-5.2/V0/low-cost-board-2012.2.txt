**Steelman.** A $35 board, sold through existing distributors, creates the cheapest viable entry point to real programming; it leverages the charity's brand trust without requiring it to become a manufacturer.

**Assumptions checked.**
1. *Two distributors can handle fulfilment and returns.* Plausible but unverified — the charity's support exposure depends entirely on this contract.
2. *Schools want a bare board.* The risk that buyers must source their own display, keyboard and power supply is the proposal's weakest point: a school without a spare HDMI TV cannot use it, making the $35 headline misleading. The total cost of ownership is closer to $60–75.
3. *No manufacturing experience.* Mitigated by outsourcing assembly, but QA and warranty risk transfers to a charity with no repair infrastructure.

**Better ways.**
1. *Do nothing / partner.* The charity could curate and endorse existing cheap hardware (refurb laptops, tablets) rather than designing its own board — lower risk, faster to market, but cedes control of the software stack and margins.
2. *Pilot first.* Sell 1,000 boards to a self-selected enthusiast audience, measure attach rate of accessories and support tickets, then decide on school rollout. Cheaper than a mass launch; the downside is slower momentum and a competitor filling the gap.

**Pre-mortem.** The board launches, schools buy it, but 40% return it because they cannot source compatible power supplies or displays cheaply. The charity's reputation is damaged and distributors refuse a second batch.

**Kill criteria.** Run a 2,000-unit pilot through the two distributors. Kill or revise if: (a) accessory attach rate is below 50% (schools cannot use the bare board); (b) support tickets exceed 1 per 5 units sold; (c) the second distributor places no reorder within 90 days.

**Fit.** The charity's north star is rekindling programming interest. A cheap, hackable board aligns directly — but only if the total cost of ownership stays within school budgets.

**Strongest attack survived.** "Ordinary laptops are already cheap" — but they are closed boxes that discourage low-level tinkering; the board's value proposition is access to the metal, not price-per-MIPS.

**Decision.** Go, but as a capped pilot (2,000 units, 90-day review window) through the two licensed distributors, with the kill criteria above. Do not commit to mass production until the pilot's accessory attach rate and support load are measured.

DECISION: GO
