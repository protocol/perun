The steelman: a royalty-on-everything model lets a capital-light start-up scale distribution through multiple hardware partners while still capturing software economics — the same logic that later worked for Microsoft's Xbox and Sony's PlayStation.

The sharpest attack: this proposal misprices the console and misunderstands the competitive structure. At ~$700, the console is priced for early adopters but must also let licensees profit on hardware. That double-margin is structurally impossible against incumbents who subsidize hardware and earn on games. The start-up is asking licensees to do what Sony and Sega explicitly do not do — make money on the box — while charging a premium price. No licensee rationally accepts that risk.

The "set the standard" goal requires market share, not just a beautiful machine. A $700 console with licensee hardware margins cannot reach the installed base needed to attract game developers, which is the actual network effect. The royalty model only works if volume is high enough to make the per-unit royalty worthwhile for licensees. At this price, it won't be.

The pre-mortem: the console launches, early adopters buy in modest numbers, licensees see thin margins and slow sell-through, they stop building or demand price concessions, game developers see a small installed base and don't port titles, and the platform dies. The kill criterion — a sales threshold by month six — would likely fire, but by then the licensing relationships and brand are damaged.

The better way: control the hardware relationship directly, subsidize the console to reach installed-base critical mass, and earn on game licensing. That requires more capital than this start-up likely has, which is itself the tell: if you can't afford to subsidize hardware, you can't set the standard in 1993's console market.

DECISION: NO-GO
