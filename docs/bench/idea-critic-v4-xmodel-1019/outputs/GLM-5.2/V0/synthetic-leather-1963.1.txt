The core issue is a strategic mismatch: a durable, leather-like synthetic is proposed for the premium shoe-upper market, where comfort, fashion flexibility, and established factory processes all work against adoption. The very properties cited (durability, synthetic) are not the levers that move premium buyers, who prioritise feel, aesthetics, and brand heritage.

Concrete failure scenario: a shoe maker trials the material, finds it stiffer and less breathable than leather, and rejects it—exactly the "less comfortable" risk raised, which is not addressed by heavy marketing to manufacturers. Marketing spend cannot solve a product-fit problem; it amplifies waste when the fit is wrong.

The proposal also lacks a kill criterion: no threshold for adoption rate or trial-to-conversion ratio at which spending stops. Approving an open-ended heavy-spend plan without a defined stop condition is imprudent.

Cheaper, more reversible paths exist: test the material in a narrower premium segment where durability is valued (e.g., work boots, certain accessories), or run a small paid pilot with two or three manufacturers to measure trial conversion before committing to heavy spend. These alternatives surface real adoption signal at a fraction of the risk.

The steelman—durability plus leather-like appearance is a genuine value proposition that premium manufacturers might pay more for—does not survive the comfort and fashion-flexibility objections, both of which are structural to the proposed market.

DECISION: NO-GO
