**Steelman:** A radically cheaper robot, sold direct, can win a mass market that premium mapping robots will never reach — random coverage need only be *good enough* on average.

**Strongest attack survived:** "Random movement can't guarantee full coverage, so the product is fundamentally flawed." This fails because the standard is not deterministic completeness but acceptable average coverage over multiple runs; a low price resets buyer expectations from "perfect" to "useful."

**Assumptions:**
- Manufacturing cost supports the low price at volume (assumed; unverified).
- Random coverage reaches an acceptable average over repeated runs (assumed; unverified).
- Consumers will tolerate a non-mapping, "dumb" robot if cheap enough (assumed; unverified).

**Better ways:**
- Do nothing: keep selling prototypes to OEMs. Cheapest, but two rejections signal weak fit.
- License the tech to an existing consumer brand: lower risk, but cedes the margin and the brand.

**Pre-mortem (why it failed badly):** The robot covered 60% of a room on average; reviewers mocked it; returns spiked; the company burned its capital on a product no one wanted.

**Kill criteria:** Pre-launch pilot in 50 homes for 30 days. **Kill if** median coverage falls below 70% OR return-intent exceeds 20%. Leading metric: repeat-use rate at day 14.

**Questions parent must resolve:** What is the verified BOM and can it hit the target retail? What is the actual average coverage in a real living room?

**Dissent ledger:** No consumer experience; no brand; mapping rivals may dominate perception. These are real but addressable through the price wedge and a pilot gate.

**Remaining risk:** The coverage assumption is the whole bet; if it is wrong, the product is unsellable at any price.

DECISION: NO-GO
