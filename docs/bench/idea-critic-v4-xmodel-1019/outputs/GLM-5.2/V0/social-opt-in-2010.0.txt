**Steelman.** Webmail has scale and daily engagement; auto-enrollment gives instant network density; visible connections drive the social loop.

**Strongest attack survived.** "Exposure is irreversible and default-on" — the sharpest objection. It fails to save the proposal because the objection is *correct*: once connections are visible, users cannot un-disclose them, regulators cannot un-see them, and trust cannot be rebuilt by a toggle.

**Alternatives.**
1. **Opt-in** — same feature, user chooses; near-zero trust risk, slower density.
2. **Default-private** — auto-enrolled but nothing visible without consent; reversible.
3. **Do nothing** — no social layer; zero exposure risk.

The proposal loses to all three on reversibility and trust.

**Kill criteria.** The tripwire fires on Day 1: any user sees a connection they did not explicitly share → the default-on exposure has already done irreversible damage. No monitoring window helps; the harm is the launch.

**Pre-mortem.** "It failed because users discovered colleagues, exes, or competitors could see who they email, panicked, and the press framed it as 'the search company that read your inbox and told your friends.' Regulators opened inquiries within 72 hours. The team had no rollback that re-collected already-exposed data."

**Fit.** A search company's asset is *trust in handling private data*. A social network inside an inbox trades that trust for engagement. The north star is misplaced.

**Decision: one call.** Do not launch as proposed. If the social layer is worth pursuing, ship it **opt-in with default-private connections**, measure adoption at 30 days, and kill if under 10% of active mail users join.

**Dissent ledger.** Opt-in may never reach network density — accepted; density without consent is the failure mode this avoids.

**Remaining risk.** Even opt-in carries regulatory exposure if "visible connections" reveal sensitive correspondence patterns; legal review is required before any variant ships.

DECISION: NO-GO
