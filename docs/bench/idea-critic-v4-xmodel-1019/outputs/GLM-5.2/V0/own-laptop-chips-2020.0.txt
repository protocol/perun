**Steelman.** A phone-processor designer already controls the stack that matters most; extending that to laptops compounds the advantage instead of starting fresh.

**Fit.** Vertical integration is the stated north star. The proposal moves toward it — assuming "same design approach" actually scales from phone thermal envelopes to laptop ones.

**Pre-mortem.** The laptop line fails to ship competitive performance inside the transition window. The most likely cause: phone design instincts underserve sustained laptop workloads, and the software-compatibility gap proves larger than scoped. A supplier relationship ends regardless.

**Skeptic.** "Same design approach as in its phones" is an assumption dressed as a plan. Phone chips optimize for bursty, thermally constrained workloads; laptop chips must sustain performance and meet a different price-performance bar. The proposal asserts continuity where the burden of proof is showing the design philosophy actually transfers. No evidence is offered that it does.

**Better-way.** The strongest alternative is a staged transition: ship the self-designed laptop chips in a single lower-volume product line first, keep the supplier relationship for flagship laptops during the pilot, and cut over only if the pilot clears a performance and compatibility bar. This preserves the strategic direction while making the failure mode observable and bounded.

**Kill criteria.** No stop condition is named. A transition this expensive and this visible needs a pre-committed trigger: if the pilot product cannot match the supplier's chips on a defined sustained-performance benchmark by a fixed date, or if software compatibility falls below a stated threshold, revert to the supplier.

The proposal asserts a strategic direction without naming how the design philosophy transfers, how software compatibility is guaranteed, or when to stop if it fails. Those are not details to work out after approval — they are the approval.

DECISION: NO-GO
