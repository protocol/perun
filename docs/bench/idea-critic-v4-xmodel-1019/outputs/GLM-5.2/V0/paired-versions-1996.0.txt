**Strongest attack tried:** the two-version split looks like a price grab, and trading needs a second player and a cable — so a large share of buyers who can't trade are paying for an uncompletable game, which could sour word-of-mouth and cut sales.

**Why it failed:** the objection assumes the game's value is the collection's completion. But the real hook is the trading *itself* — a social mechanism that turns a single-player cartridge into a reason for kids to meet, link up, and compare. The two versions don't gate the core experience; they create the social friction that makes the game a shared activity rather than a solo one. The cable requirement is a feature, not a bug: the physical act of linking two consoles in the schoolyard is the experience the game is selling. A single version would be easier to complete — and that ease would kill the social pressure to trade, talk, and recruit friends.

The declining-handheld risk is real but is mitigated by the fact that the game itself is the reason to keep the console. The price-grab perception is manageable through marketing that frames the two versions as parallel adventures, not halves of one game. And the publisher's uncertainty is exactly why the two-version strategy is valuable: it doubles the SKU count, creates organic buzz through trading, and gives the game a built-in virality mechanism that a single version wouldn't have.

The alternatives — single version, or waiting for the next hardware — don't capture the social-trading dynamic. The single version is the safer play but gives up the thing that would make this game remembered. Waiting doesn't help: the social mechanic works precisely because the hardware is cheap and ubiquitous among the target audience.

No kill criteria are possible at this stage; the bet is that the trading mechanic creates word-of-mouth. The downside is bounded (a small developer, an aging console), and the upside is a franchise-defining social loop.

DECISION: GO
