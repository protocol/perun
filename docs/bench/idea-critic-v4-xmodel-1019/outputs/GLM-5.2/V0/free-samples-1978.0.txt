**Steelman:** The strongest defensible version of the claim is: free samples to one mid-sized city's offices would surface genuine demand at a fraction of the national rollout cost, so the decision hinges on whether "most recipients say they would buy it" is a reliable proxy for actual purchases.

**Skeptic:** The kill criterion is self-reported purchase intent, which overstates actual demand: recipients may say they would buy it to be polite, because it is free, or because they have not considered the price. A survey of free-sample recipients is not a market test.

**Better-way:** The four-city test already ran and gave disappointing sales. The cheaper path is to re-examine *why* it failed — was it the name, the price, the channel, or genuine lack of need? — before spending on free samples. If the name was the problem, a retest in one city under the new name, at retail, with real purchases measured, is more informative than free samples.

**Kill-criteria:** "Most recipients say they would buy it" is not an observable metric of purchases. The kill criterion should be: a threshold share of sample recipients who *actually reorder* at a stated price within 30 days. Without that, the national rollout has no stop condition.

**Strongest attack survived:** The proposal's core failure is that it substitutes a survey of free-sample recipients for a market test, and survey-validated intent is a known weak predictor of paid purchases — so the national rollout would be gated on a metric that cannot reliably prevent it.

**Decision:** no-go. Revise the kill criterion to a measured reorder rate at a stated price, and retest under the new name in one city at retail before any national commitment.

DECISION: NO-GO
