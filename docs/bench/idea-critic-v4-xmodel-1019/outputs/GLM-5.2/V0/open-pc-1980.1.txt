**Steelman:** A fast, cheap entry into the fastest-growing segment of computing, using commodity parts and outside software to compress time-to-market, with an open design that builds an ecosystem moat.

**Strongest attack survived:** "Open design + commodity parts means rivals copy it immediately, destroying any advantage." This fails because the moat was never the hardware — it was the ecosystem the open design would attract. Rivals can copy the box but not the installed base of users and software. The very openness that makes copying easy also makes the platform valuable. The attack assumes hardware differentiation is the prize, when the real prize is being the standard.

**Assumptions:**
- Open design will attract enough outside software to create a defensible ecosystem (assumed; would need to verify developer interest).
- Lower margins are acceptable because unit volume and ecosystem lock-in compensate.
- Retail inexperience is a solvable operational problem, not a strategic disqualifier.

**Better ways:**
- *Do nothing:* preserves current margins and avoids retail learning curve — but cedes the fastest-growing market entirely and lets rivals build the ecosystem standard.
- *Build a closed, proprietary small computer:* higher differentiation, higher margins — but much slower (defeating the purpose) and still requires retail and software the company lacks.

**Kill criteria:**
- If outside software titles committed by launch are below a threshold sufficient to seed an ecosystem, stop.
- If unit sales through retail channels do not reach a volume target within 18 months of launch, exit or pivot.

**Remaining risk:** The company bets that ecosystem value exceeds hardware-copyability risk. If the open design fails to attract software developers, the company has a low-margin, easily-copied product and no moat. This is a real bet, but the alternative — letting rivals define the standard in the fastest-growing market — is strategically worse.

DECISION: GO
