Steelman: a rebranded free-sample test is the cheapest way to falsify "no one wants this" before spending national-rollout money.

Strongest attack survived: "survey intent overstates real demand." True, and that is exactly why the bar is "most recipients say they would buy" — a high threshold on a biased signal. If the biased signal still clears a high bar, the underlying demand is likely real enough to justify a larger test.

The key problem: the proposal conflates a biased survey with a purchase decision. Free samples generate gratitude; surveys generate politeness. "Would you buy it?" after a free sample measures sentiment, not willingness to pay. The original four-city test already measured actual purchase behavior and the answer was disappointing.

A better test: sell the renamed product at full price in two cities with the free-sample distribution in one of them. If paid purchases beat the original four-city baseline, expand. This directly measures whether the rename and sampling convert to revenue, which is the actual decision being made.

The national rollout conditioned on survey results is the critical flaw. Scaling production, marketing spend, and distribution nationally on the basis of survey responses to free samples would be an irreversible, high-cost commitment built on a weak evidence base.

Recommendation: reject as proposed. Run a small paid-purchase test with the rename, compare against the four-city baseline, and use actual revenue — not survey intent — to decide on broader rollout.
