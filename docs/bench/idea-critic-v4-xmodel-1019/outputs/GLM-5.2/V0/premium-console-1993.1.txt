The strongest version of this proposal is that licensing shifts manufacturing risk to partners, royalties create recurring revenue, and high-end graphics differentiate in a crowded market.

The sharpest attack: the economics are internally contradictory and externally uncompetitive. Established console makers sell hardware at a loss and profit on software. This proposal requires licensees to profit on hardware AND pay a royalty on each console. At $700, the console is roughly double the price of competing systems. Licensees face a brutal margin squeeze: the Bill of Materials for high-end graphics is already high, they must build in profit, and they must pay the start-up a per-console royalty. No rational electronics maker accepts this when the consumer price is already uncompetitive against subsidized rivals. The royalty stacking—console royalty plus game royalty—compounds the problem, making every unit more expensive than the incumbent model at every layer.

The kill criterion is already visible in the proposal's own numbers: at $700 against $300 subsidized competitors, early adopters alone cannot sustain a platform, and licensees will not commit to volumes that make the royalty model viable. The pre-mortem writes itself: licensees decline or demand concessions that destroy the margin model; the few who sign produce small volumes; developers see a tiny installed base and skip the platform; the console becomes a footnote.

No alternative was considered against this plan—most obviously, the start-up building and subsidizing the hardware itself to control the platform economics, or targeting a lower price point with more modest graphics. The proposal did not survive its own arithmetic.

DECISION: NO-GO
