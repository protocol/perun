Steelman: a compact on a shared mass-market platform, priced premium, captures conquest buyers already leaving European luxury for smaller American sedans, at a fraction of dedicated-platform cost.

The strongest attack: buyers and salespeople see through the shared platform, the premium price collapses against rivals' smaller sedans, and prestige contamination spreads upline to the main brand. This is a one-way door on brand equity for a car line that may not hold price.

But the decisive fact is the buyer shift: the growth segment is American-brand customers wanting a smaller sedan, not cross-shoppers against European luxury. That reframes the bar — the compact does not need to beat a European rival on prestige, it needs to be credible enough to conquest a mass-market buyer into the brand. A shared platform with added trim clears that lower bar; salespeople resist new names in every launch and is a training problem, not a structural flaw.

The prestige-contamination risk is real but bounded: the compact sits beneath the main brand, and the contamination flows only if the compact's execution is visibly cheap, not merely shared-platform. Cadillac's own Cimarron failure (a rushed, thinly disguised Cavalier) is the cautionary data — but it argues for execution discipline, not abstention, and this proposal's "added trim and features at a premium price" is the discipline.

The alternatives — do nothing, or build a dedicated compact platform — lose. Do nothing cedes the conquest segment. A dedicated platform for a segment whose buyers are price-sensitive mass-market defers the payoff past the 1981 market window.

Kill criteria: if the compact cannot hold within 15% of its premium sticker at 18 months, or if main-brand sedan sales drop more than 10% in the year after launch, pull the trim back to a clearly differentiated sub-brand and stop expanding the line.

Leading metric: conquest rate from American-brand owners, reviewed at 12 months.

DECISION: GO
