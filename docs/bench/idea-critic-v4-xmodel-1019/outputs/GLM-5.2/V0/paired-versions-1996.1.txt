**Steelman:** Two versions with trade-exclusive creatures turn the link cable from a niche peripheral into a viral, peer-to-peer distribution and engagement engine — the "cost" of needing a friend is the feature, not the bug.

**Strongest attack survived:** "Selling two versions looks like a price grab and alienates players." This fails because the social-trading mechanic is the actual innovation: it creates playground buzz, extends playtime, and makes the game a shared experience rather than a solo one. The perceived price-grab risk is real but subordinate to the network effect.

**Assumptions:**
- The link cable is already manufactured and available (assumed; if not, cost rises).
- The handheld's decline is gradual, not terminal within the release window (unverified).

**Better ways:**
- **Single version, post-release trade expansion:** Cheaper to produce, but loses the launch buzz and the "which version did you get?" conversation.
- **Do nothing:** Zero risk, but the game likely ships and dies quietly on a declining platform.

**Pre-mortem:** The game launches in two versions. Six months later, sales are poor because the handheld's installed base collapsed faster than expected, and the trading mechanic never reached critical mass. The publisher spent double on cartridge production for a shrinking market.

**Kill criteria:** If combined sell-through across both versions is below [threshold] within 90 days of launch, halt further cartridge production and cut losses.

**Dissent ledger:** The handheld decline is the sharpest risk; the proposal bets that social mechanics overcome platform headwinds — plausible but unproven. The "price grab" perception is manageable with marketing but not free.

**Remaining risk:** The mechanic's success depends on network density (enough nearby players with cables); low density means the core loop breaks.

DECISION: GO
