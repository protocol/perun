**Steelman:** Electric drivetrains are simpler and more reliable than combustion ones; a clean-sheet carmaker can leapfrog incumbents burdened by legacy engine programs, and battery costs will fall with scale.

**Strongest attack survived:** The capital gap is real but not fatal — the company doesn't need to close it alone. A compelling product and a credible factory plan attract the co-investment and government loans that bridge the gap. The factory purchase is the lever: it converts a startup pitch into a manufacturing-capable company, which is what partners and lenders fund. Scarcity of charging stations and incumbent responses are industry-level risks that affect all entrants equally; they don't distinguish this company's bet from any other. The mass-production inexperience is the sharpest objection, but it is addressable by hiring experienced operators, and it is a smaller risk than never building the car at all.

**Assumptions:** Battery cost per kWh will decline meaningfully within the product's first five years (assumed; industry trend lines support it). The company can secure enough external capital to reach production (unverified but plausible given the factory asset). Experienced automotive manufacturing talent is available for hire (assumed).

**Better ways considered:** Building a lower-cost sports car first to prove drivetrain technology — but that doesn't solve the capital or scale problem, and the sedan is the volume product that justifies the factory. Partnering with an incumbent for manufacturing — but that cedes the core differentiation and control. Doing nothing — preserves capital but guarantees irrelevance in a market moving toward electrification.

**Kill criteria:** If the company cannot raise a named capital threshold by a fixed date, stop and pivot to a contract-manufacturing model. Leading metric to watch: cost per kWh of battery packs at the production-design lock-in date, reviewed against a declining target curve.

**Remaining risk:** The company is betting that capital, talent, and battery-cost curves all move in the right direction simultaneously. If any one lags, the others may not compensate.

DECISION: GO
