**Steelman:** A firm that already designs phone/tablet silicon can extend that stack into laptops, capturing margin, differentiating performance-per-watt, and controlling its roadmap end-to-end.

**Origin:** agent-originated proposal to the executive team.

**Blast:** high. Ends a supplier relationship, commits multi-year engineering capacity, and risks stranding laptop users on broken software.

**Reversibility:** low once launched. Software-compatibility failures surface only at volume; a supplier severed is not quickly re-engaged.

**Alternatives considered:**
1. **Do nothing** — cheapest, zero transition risk, keeps supplier relationship intact.
2. **Co-design with supplier** — captures some performance gains without owning the full stack.
3. **Internal laptop chip, phased** — same destination, lower blast radius per stage.

**Skeptic:** The claim that the phone design approach transfers to laptops is unproven; laptops run a heavier, legacy software base where binary-compatibility breaks are costly. The transition cost is cited qualitatively ("may cost engineering time") with no figure, and buyer confusion is dismissed without evidence. The supplier relationship ending is treated as a footnote, not a strategic loss.

**Better-way:** A phased transition — developer kits first, then a single product line — captures the learning with far lower blast radius than a full line move.

**Kill-criteria:** None stated. No measurable threshold for "unproven chips are competitive enough," no date by which software compatibility must hold, no rollback condition.

**Strongest attack survived:** The proposal conflates capability (designing phone chips) with strategic fit (owning laptop silicon), and presents no kill criteria, cost figures, or compatibility evidence. This is a one-way-door commitment being approved as if it were reversible.

**Questions parent must resolve:** What is the quantified transition cost and payback period? What measurable software-compatibility threshold must hold before full rollout? What is the rollback plan if the first laptop chip underperforms?

**Remaining risk:** Executing a high-blast, low-reversibility transition with no kill criteria, no cost figures, and no compatibility evidence.

DECISION: NO-GO
