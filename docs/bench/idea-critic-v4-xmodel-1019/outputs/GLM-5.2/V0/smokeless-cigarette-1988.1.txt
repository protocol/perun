**Steelman:** A reduced-smoke cigarette could capture health-conscious smokers and open a premium segment, justifying years of R&D spend.

**Skeptic — assumptions presented as fact:**
1. *"Cleaner way to smoke"* — no toxicology data cited. Regulators will demand evidence; without it, the claim is indefensible and invites sanctions.
2. *"Smokers will adopt it"* — the proposal acknowledges taste and lighting problems. These are not peripheral risks; they are adoption killers. No consumer-acceptance threshold is defined.
3. *"Promote widely"* — broad health-claim advertising before regulatory clearance is a one-way-door mistake. A rejected claim becomes public and permanent.

**Better-way:** Run narrow test markets with measured metrics (trial rate, repeat-purchase rate, regulator response) *before* wide promotion. The proposal jumps from test markets to broad launch without defining what test-market result would justify scaling — or kill the project.

**Kill-criteria (missing):** What repeat-purchase rate justifies national launch? What regulator response halts promotion? No tripwires stated. Without them, sunk-cost pressure ("we already spent a very large sum") will push forward regardless of evidence. Sunk cost is not a reason to launch; it is a reason to be *more* careful, because the money is gone either way.

**Strongest attack survived:** "Sunk cost demands launch to recoup." Rebutted: launching a product smokers dislike, regulators challenge, and is hard to use — *with* unsupported health claims — burns more money than it recovers and risks the parent brand. But the core objection stands: the proposal conflates test markets (a learning step) with wide launch (a commitment), with no gate between them.

**Remaining risk:** Even with test-market gates, the product's complexity and taste profile may make it a niche product unsuitable for "wide promotion."

The proposal as written — test markets then broad launch with health claims — skips the kill-criteria step and exposes the company to regulatory and reputational harm that is hard to reverse.

DECISION: NO-GO
