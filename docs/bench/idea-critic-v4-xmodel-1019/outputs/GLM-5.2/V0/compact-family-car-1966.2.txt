The strongest case for the proposal: the new plant is a sunk, fixed-cost asset that only earns its keep near capacity; a dedicated dealer network removes channel conflict with the existing small-car line; and a compact 1100 cc family car fills the gap between the home-market minicars and larger rivals, capturing first-time buyers who will trade up later.

Sharpest objection actually tried: the proposal bundles three independent bets — a new car, a new plant's utilization, and a new dealer network — and finances the first two with the third. If the dedicated network under-builds (costly, slow) or buyers defect to a cheaper or larger car, plant utilization collapses and the fixed-cost burden falls back on the carmaker's existing profitable lines. The proposal as stated names every one of these risks but gives no kill criterion, no capacity threshold, and no fallback if the network lags.

That objection fails to break the claim for one reason: the risks are real but **reversible and staged**. The dealer network can be built region by region; the 1100 cc car can launch into one segment first; plant capacity can ramp rather than open cold at full output. The bet is a two-way door with a cheap first step, not an all-or-nothing commitment. Waiting a quarter costs nothing the new plant has not already cost, and the compact-family segment is large enough that a staged entry can still capture share.

Remaining risk: if the carmaker treats the launch as binary — full network, full capacity, one shot — the bundled-bet failure mode stands. The mitigated path is a capped, regional first pilot with a named utilization floor and a stop-or-expand date.

DECISION: GO
