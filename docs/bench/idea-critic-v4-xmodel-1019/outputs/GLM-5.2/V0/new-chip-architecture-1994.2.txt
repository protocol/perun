**Steelman:** A clean 64-bit design with compiler-scheduled parallelism can outperform any backward-compatible extension of the dominant architecture, and emulation buys compatibility during the transition.

**Strongest attack tried and survived:** Rivals extend the dominant architecture to 64 bits, preserving native compatibility with the huge installed software base. The proposal's response—emulation—depends on compilers that are explicitly flagged as hard to write, and on emulation performance that is itself a raised risk. The steelman survives only if emulation is fast enough *and* compilers arrive on time; both are unverified premises, not settled facts.

**Unresolved gaps:**
- No emulation speed target stated. Without a number, "may run slowly" is untestable and the compatibility bridge is unvalidated.
- Compiler difficulty is acknowledged but undated. If compilers slip past silicon, the performance premise collapses.
- The rival-response risk is named but not priced: no analysis of how likely a 64-bit extension of the dominant architecture is, or how fast it could ship.
- No kill criterion. The proposal should state one observable metric with a threshold and a date—e.g., emulated legacy workloads at ≥X% of native speed by Q3 1995, or compilers hitting Y% of hand-scheduled performance by a named milestone.

**Verdict:** REVISE

The idea is strategically sound—breaking compatibility to leapfrog a dominant architecture is a legitimate bet, and the risks are the right ones to name. But presenting it to the executive team without an emulation performance target, a compiler-delivery date, and a pre-committed kill criterion asks for approval on faith rather than evidence. Add those three things and the proposal is ready.

DECISION: NO-GO
