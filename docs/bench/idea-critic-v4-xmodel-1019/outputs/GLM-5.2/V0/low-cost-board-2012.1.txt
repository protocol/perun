The strongest case for this is that the stated goal — rekindling interest in programming — is not served by a finished appliance. A cheap laptop running a locked-down OS, or a tablet whose owner cannot touch the stack, gives consumption, not experimentation. A bare board at $35 forces the student to assemble a working machine: that assembly is the pedagogy. The risks cited are real but mostly reversible or non-fatal. A small first batch selling out is not a failure of demand; it is a signal to manufacture more. The lack of manufacturing experience is mitigated by using licensed distributors rather than building a factory. Lack of support experience is a concern, but the target audience of hobbyists and educators has high tolerance for self-service troubleshooting, and a community forum can absorb much of the load. The existence of cheap laptops and tablets is not a substitute; none of them at $35 offer a bare-metal, Linux-based, GPIO-equipped platform for learning both software and hardware. The one-way-door risk is minimal: the charity is not committing to a large manufacturing run or long-term contracts upfront, and the financial exposure is capped by the small initial batch. The leading metric to watch is sell-through of the first batch within the first quarter; if it stalls, stop. The kill criterion is straightforward: if the first batch does not sell through within 90 days, abandon the project. Given the low cost, the educational alignment, and the capped downside, this is a bet worth taking.
