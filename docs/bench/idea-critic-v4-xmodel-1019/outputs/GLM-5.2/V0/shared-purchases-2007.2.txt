The strongest case for this: behavioral targeting is where ad spend is migrating, friend endorsements outperform anonymous ads, and a notification-plus-decline design gives users meaningful control. A first mover here could lock in partner inventory before competitors do.

The sharpest attack: the notification step assumes users will read, understand, and act before their first sensitive purchase is broadcast. That is a fragile assumption resting on user vigilance rather than system safety. Purchases include pregnancy tests, mental health books, medical devices, gifts meant to be surprises, and items revealing sexual orientation or infidelity. A single such purchase shown to a friend list — before the user finds and exercises the decline option — produces harm that no after-the-fact notification fixes. The proposal treats "option to decline" as sufficient protection, but the default path (purchase happens → shared to feed → friend sees it) runs automatically and irreversibly. The user's control arrives after exposure, not before.

The reversibility concern is real but secondary: once partners integrate and users adapt, withdrawing becomes a public retreat. The primary failure is that the consent design is default-expose rather than default-ask, and the proposal does not address why that ordering is safe for sensitive categories.

A safer version preserves the revenue thesis: default to opt-in per partner (or per category), require an affirmative choice before any sharing begins, and exclude a defined sensitive-purchase list. That narrows blast radius at launch while still capturing the friends-see-friends value proposition. The cost of waiting one quarter to build that is low; the cost of launching default-expose and harming users on day one is high and reputationally irreversible.

DECISION: NO-GO
