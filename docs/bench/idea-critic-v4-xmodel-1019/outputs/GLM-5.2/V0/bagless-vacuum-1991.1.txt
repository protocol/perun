The steelman: a bagless cyclone vacuum priced at a premium can capture a durable, defensible niche of affluent buyers who value performance and zero consumables, with the option to expand downstream. That is a strong claim.

The strongest attack actually tried: incumbents with wide retail distribution copy the cyclone, undercut on price, and the new company is crushed before it builds brand. Why it fails here: the inventor has thousands of prototypes, meaning the hard engineering is largely de-risked; incumbents are structurally slow to cannibalize their own high-margin bag business; and premium pricing is itself a filter that targets buyers who pay for demonstrated suction advantage, not bargain shoppers. The copy risk is real but it is a timing and execution problem, not a reason to never start.

Alternatives considered: license the patent to an incumbent — cheaper, faster revenue, but cedes brand and margin permanently, and incumbents have every incentive to shelve a threat to their bag business. Do nothing — the prototypes and IP sit idle, the inventor earns nothing, and an incumbent eventually patents around him. Founding the company is more expensive and slower, but it is the only path that keeps both the technology and the brand.

Reversibility: this is a two-way door. The company can be wound down, the IP sold, or the strategy pivoted to licensing if manufacturing proves unviable. The cost of delay is meaningful: patents expire, incumbents move, and first-mover advantage in a premium niche erodes.

Kill criteria: if the first production run cannot hit the target suction performance at the projected unit cost within 18 months, stop manufacturing and pivot to licensing. Leading metric: pre-orders from the target affluent segment; review at 6 months.

The risks are real but survivable, the alternatives are worse, and the door is two-way with a clear exit.

DECISION: GO
