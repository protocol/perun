The strongest case for this launch: webmail already has a social graph (contacts, frequent correspondents) and a trusted brand; surfacing connections adds value at near-zero acquisition cost, and the default-visible setting is what drives network effects.

The sharpest attack that survives: automatic enrollment with default-visible connections is a one-way door. Once exposed, those connections cannot be un-seen by anyone who looked, screenshot, or scraped. The blast radius is the entire user base simultaneously, with no staged group to catch breakage. This is precisely the configuration least recoverable from a privacy backlash — and the proposal names the backlash as a risk without changing the configuration that produces it.

The cheapest alternative — opt-in enrollment with default-private connections — preserves the trusted brand, sidesteps the regulator-attention risk, and costs only the network-effect velocity that default-visible provides. That velocity is the real bet being made, and it is being wagered against the entire installed base's trust at once.

The pre-mortem: the launch already failed badly. Why? Users opened webmail and saw connections they never agreed to share. A subset had professional and personal correspondents cross-linked in ways that embarrassed or harmed them. Regulators received complaints within 48 hours. The company's search brand — its core asset — was now associated with a privacy breach. The kill criterion ("hard to reverse once connections are exposed") fires exactly when reversal is impossible, which means it is not a kill criterion; it is a description of the worst case.

Nothing in the proposal as stated sets a numeric stop threshold, a staged enrollment, or a default-private starting position. The one-way-door configuration with simultaneous blast and no recoverable stop does not clear the bar to approve as proposed.

DECISION: NO-GO
