The steelman: a business computer with lowercase and 80 columns directly addresses documented user complaints, opens the enterprise market, and replaces a product whose long-term ceiling is visible. That is a strong claim worth taking seriously.

But the proposal bundles four independent risks—complex new design, tight one-year schedule, unproven hardware reliability, and cannibalization of the only revenue source—without naming a single kill criterion, fallback, or phased de-risking path. "Launch within the year" is asserted as a constraint, not earned by evidence. "Aim to phase out" is vague enough to mean either a managed transition or an abrupt cut. None of the risks is individually fatal; the failure mode is that they compound: a rushed, complex, unproven machine that also disrupts the current revenue stream.

The strongest attack tried: the proposal asks the team to bet the company's only proven revenue on a complex new design with an unproven reliability profile and a schedule that leaves no slack—and it does not say what observable result would stop or redirect the effort. That attack fails only if a capped, time-boxed first step with a named kill criterion is added. It is not in the proposal as written.

The cheaper alternative the proposal does not beat: build the lowercase and 80-column features as an upgrade or variant on the existing, proven platform first, measure business-user adoption, then commit to the full successor. This reduces schedule risk, preserves revenue, and still tests the core hypothesis.

As proposed—simultaneously build, rush, phase out, and bet revenue without a stop condition—the risks are unmanaged. The idea is sound; the plan is not ready.

DECISION: NO-GO
