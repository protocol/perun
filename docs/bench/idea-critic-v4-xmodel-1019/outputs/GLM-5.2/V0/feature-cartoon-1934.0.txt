**Steelman.** A feature-length cartoon creates a new product category the studio can dominate before competitors notice, turning thin per-short margins into one large-ticket release with merchandising and re-release tail revenue.

**Fit.** The studio's north star is presumably sustainable profit from animation. A ten-times-budget feature concentrates risk but does not clearly move that star better than shipping more shorts at known margins.

**Pre-mortem.** Assume it failed badly. Most likely cause: 1934 audiences have no precedent for sitting through a feature-length cartoon, so they treat it as a novelty or a children's matinee, and the ten-times budget never earns back. Second cause: budget overrun on unproven feature production, draining the shorts business that funds operations.

**Evidence.** The proposal asserts budget scale and insider opposition but provides no revenue model, no comparable precedent, no cost-to-completion estimate, and no audience-research basis for the claim that viewers will sit through a feature. Every load-bearing number is absent.

**Alternatives.** Do nothing: keep earning thin but steady short profits, zero concentration risk. Cheaper path: produce one extended short at two-to-three-times standard cost to test audience tolerance before committing ten times. This is reversible and informative; the full feature is not.

**Decision.** The core uncertainty—will audiences sit through a feature-length cartoon—is directly testable at a fraction of the cost. The proposal asks the studio to risk roughly ten shorts' worth of budget on an unverified audience-behavior claim, with no kill criterion, no revenue model, and no cost-cap beyond "roughly ten times." Insider opposition is not dispositive, but the absence of any cheaper test or stated stop condition is.

DECISION: NO-GO
