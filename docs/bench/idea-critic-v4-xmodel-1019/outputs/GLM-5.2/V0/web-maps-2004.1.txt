The strongest case for the proposal: browser-based mapping removes the download barrier that caps desktop adoption; satellite imagery plus route planning is a feature combination no incumbent then offered; and a search company with existing traffic and ad infrastructure can monetize indirectly at scale.

The strongest attack actually tried: licensing costs plus no direct revenue model means the project loses money for an unbounded period, and browsers circa 2004 cannot smoothly render interactive map tiles, so the core experience is technically infeasible. This fails to kill the proposal because the stated risks are the *known* costs of a land-grab, not a hidden flaw. The search company already operates an ad network, so "no direct revenue model" conflates *no line-item price* with *no monetization path*. Browser limitations are real but addressed by tiled image delivery—static images fetched on pan/zoom—rather than full client-side rendering. Incumbent user bases are a weak barrier to a free product distributed through a search homepage with millions of daily visitors.

What would have to be true for this to work: map and imagery licenses are obtainable at a cost the broader business can carry; tiled static images deliver acceptable interactivity; and indirect ad revenue plus strategic traffic retention justify the spend. The first is a standard commercial negotiation; the second is an engineering proof-of-concept runnable in weeks; the third is a judgment the acquiring company's executives are positioned to make.

Kill criteria: if a two-week tiled-rendering prototype cannot achieve sub-second pan response on a contemporary consumer machine, stop. If licensing terms cannot be closed within 90 days at a carryable cost, revisit scope. Leading metric to watch: unique users engaging with map features within 30 days of launch, reviewed at day 45.

DECISION: GO
