The strongest case for GO: the inventor has already de-risked the hardest part — thousands of prototypes means the cyclone separation actually works and he understands the engineering deeply. First-mover advantage on bagless technology, combined with patent protection, could create a defensible market position. Premium pricing is viable if the technology genuinely outperforms.

The sharpest objection that must survive: **the proposal conflates a working prototype with a viable business.** Manufacturing at scale, building retail distribution from zero, and convincing consumers to pay a premium for "bagless" (a category that doesn't yet exist in their minds) are three separate, hard problems. The inventor has solved none of them. Every risk listed — no manufacturing record, incumbent copying, price resistance — is real and none has a mitigation in the proposal.

A cheaper alternative exists: **license the patent to an established manufacturer.** This captures the invention's value without taking on manufacturing risk, distribution build-out, or retail resistance. The inventor skips the problems he has no experience solving and still gets paid. The proposal does not address why self-manufacturing beats licensing.

The pre-mortem: the company launches, burns through capital setting up a factory and retail deals, incumbents introduce competing bagless models at lower price points using their existing distribution, and the premium-positioned product is stranded. This is not speculative — it is the predictable outcome of entering a capital-intensive, distribution-heavy market against entrenched players.

**Decision method — run in this order:**

1. **Steelman, then attack.** Thousands of prototypes validate the technology; they do not validate the business model. The claim "he should manufacture and sell it himself" is the part under attack, not the invention.
2. **Options.** (a) Self-manufacture at premium price — high capital, high risk, full upside. (b) License to an incumbent — low capital, low risk, capped upside. (c) Do nothing — zero risk, zero return. The proposal must beat (b), not only (c), and it does not address (b) at all.
3. **Fit.** Assumed north star: capture value from the bagless invention. Self-manufacturing fits only if the inventor can also build distribution and manufacturing capability — neither is evidenced.
4. **Pre-mortem, door, delay.** Most likely failure: incumbents copy and underprice before the new company reaches scale. This is a one-way door (significant sunk capital in factory and inventory). Cost of delay: low — the patent protects against immediate copying, and a few months to negotiate a license or find a manufacturing partner does not forfeit the market.
5. **Evidence.** The proposal asserts premium pricing is viable but provides no consumer research, willingness-to-pay data, or competitive pricing analysis. The manufacturing capability is assumed, not demonstrated. Key figures — capital required, break-even volume, gross margin — are absent.

**Kill criteria:** if a licensing deal (or partnership with an established manufacturer) can recover even 30% of the invention's projected standalone value at a fraction of the capital risk, self-manufacturing is the wrong call. The proposal should be revised to compare self-manufacture against licensing, with real capital and distribution-cost estimates.

DECISION: NO-GO
