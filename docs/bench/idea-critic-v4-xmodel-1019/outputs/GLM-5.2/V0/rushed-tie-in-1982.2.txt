**Steelman:** A recognizable film license plus a guaranteed Christmas shelf date can move millions of cartridges even at mediocre quality — the brand sells, not the code.

**Strongest attack survived:** The five-week timeline is physically near-impossible for a polished game. But the proposal doesn't require polish; it requires a playable cartridge with the film's name on it, on shelves before December. That's achievable at low quality — and the attack actually *strengthens* the case for a large run, since poor quality only matters if reviews reach buyers before purchase, which in 1982 they largely don't.

**Skeptic:**
- Assumption: brand recognition alone drives sales. Plausible in 1982 but unverified.
- Assumption: one programmer can deliver *anything* shippable in five weeks. High risk.
- Assumption: the large run's unit economics work even if sell-through is 50%.

**Better-way:**
- *Do nothing:* forfeit the license fee already paid. Worse.
- *Small run first:* preserves capital, but misses the Christmas window — the entire premise. The film's theatrical momentum is now; by Q1 1985 the license is cold.
- *Delay to next Christmas:* gives development time but abandons the current licensing deal's value window.

**Kill-criteria:**
- One-way door: the production run commits real capital irreversibly.
- Pre-mortem: the game ships broken, retailers refuse restock, and the large run becomes landfill — *E.T.* on Atari, 1982, which triggered the industry crash.
- Tripwire: cap the first production run at 50% of the proposed volume. If the programmer delivers nothing playable by week 4, cancel the run. If sell-through is below 60% in the first three weeks, halt reprint.

**Remaining risk:** The historical parallel is exact and catastrophic. The proposal as written — full large run, five weeks, one programmer — mirrors the Atari strategy that nearly destroyed the industry. The kill-criteria above are the minimum revision; without them, the blast radius is the company itself.

DECISION: NO-GO
