The proposal's own numbers break it. At ~$700 with licensees required to earn a hardware profit, the console lands roughly double the price of competing systems — and those competitors *intentionally* sell hardware at a loss, recouping through game sales. This start-up plans to take a royalty on *both* consoles and games, meaning licensees must profit on hardware *and* surrender game revenue. That stacks two margins onto a price already uncompetitive against subsidized rivals.

The target market — early adopters drawn to high-end graphics — is small and price-sensitive. Software developers write for installed base; a $700 console with multiple competing licensees (fragmenting marketing and inventory) will struggle to reach the volume that attracts third-party titles. Without those titles, even early adopters defect. The pre-mortem writes itself: high price suppresses adoption, low adoption suppresses software, missing software kills the platform — and the start-up has no fallback because it has licensed away hardware control.

The better way exists and is proven: control the hardware, subsidize it, earn on software. That is the actual standard for this generation, not a licensing model that asks hardware partners to accept thinner margins than competitors who own their platforms.

No kill criterion is offered, no price-elasticity evidence, no installed-base threshold for developer commitment. The royalty structure has not been tested against what licensees would actually accept. Approving now commits to a model whose failure path is visible in the proposal's own risk section.

DECISION: NO-GO
