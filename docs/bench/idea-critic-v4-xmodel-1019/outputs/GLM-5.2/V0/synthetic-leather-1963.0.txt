**Steelman.** A durable leather-look synthetic, marketed hard to shoe makers as a premium upper, captures share before competitors catch up — if the three risks break favorably.

**Skeptic.** Every load-bearing claim is unsupported: the durability margin over leather, the "premium" price buyers will pay, and the conversion cost shoe makers face. "Premium" and "costs more than leather" sit in direct tension — the same buyer who rejects a price-up material is the one the premium pitch targets. Comfort is a wear-test question, not a launch question; shipping without that answer risks a first-season reputation collapse the heavy marketing spend would amplify, not absorb.

**Better-way.** Two cheaper doors beat the proposed one. (1) Sell the same material into applications where leather substitutes are already accepted — upholstery, trim, accessories — where the durability claim is testable and the buyer's switching cost is lower. (2) Pilot one shoe-upper line with one maker under a co-marketing agreement; measure take-up, reorders and returns before committing the full marketing budget. The proposal's "launch premium + spend heavily" is the most expensive way to learn whether the three risks are real.

**Kill-criteria.** The proposal names no stop condition. A heavy upfront spend with no kill metric is a one-way bet dressed as a launch: if first-season adoption is weak, the sunk marketing cost pushes the company to double down rather than exit. A defensible version caps the spend, sets one observable threshold (e.g. share of target makers placing a second order within six months), and pre-commits the pivot or stop.

**Strongest attack survived.** "Heavy marketing locks in shoe makers before competitors respond" — but the proposal gives no evidence that maker lock-in is real, that the window is closing, or that the spend size is tied to it. Without that, the urgency is assumed, not shown.

DECISION: NO-GO
