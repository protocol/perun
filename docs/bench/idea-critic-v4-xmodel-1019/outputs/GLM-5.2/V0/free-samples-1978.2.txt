**Steelman:** A renamed product, sampled to offices, tests whether the original failure was branding/awareness rather than fundamental demand — a cheap, fast, reversible read before any national spend.

**Skeptic:** The core assumption — that the 1978 product failed on *name*, not *need* — is untested. "Would you buy it?" after receiving a free sample measures gratitude and hypothetical intent, not purchasing behaviour; survey-intent overstates real demand by a wide margin. The first four-city test already showed little demand; this proposal re-runs a softer version and calls the answer new.

**Better-way:** The cheaper, more decisive test: sell the renamed product at full price in *one* office-supply channel in one city. A handful of real purchases at retail price settles "is there demand?" far better than free-sample survey responses. This costs less than sampling a whole city and gives a market signal, not a sentiment signal.

**Kill-criteria:** Stop the national rollout — do not approve it — unless a named channel sells ≥ N units at full price within a fixed window. Free-sample "would buy" responses are not a tripwire; they are the failure mode.

**Strongest attack survived:** "Free samples to one city, then national rollout" conflates *trial* with *demand* and uses a vanity metric ("most recipients say they would buy") as the gate. The attack succeeds: the proposal as written should not be approved.

**Remaining risk:** Even a disciplined full-price test in one channel may miss the later mass-market adoption that made the product famous; but that is a reason to test cheaply, not to bet nationally on survey intent.

DECISION: NO-GO
