The strongest case for launching is that the two floor-care companies' rejection is itself the market signal: incumbents with mapping-based roadmaps saw no margin in a dumb, cheap robot, which is exactly the gap a low-cost entrant can own. Randomized coverage is a feature at the price point, not a flaw: buyers comparing a $200 robot to a $1000 mapping rival will tolerate missed spots, and multi-pass coverage over time closes most of the gap. No consumer-product experience is real but cheaply addressed through a retail partner and aggressive warranty; the lack of an installed base means there is no legacy to protect.

The sharpest attack: the two companies may have rejected it because *consumers won't want it*, not because incumbents were blind. If randomized coverage produces visibly poor results in a first-week home trial, returns spike, retail partners pull the unit, and the category is poisoned for the company. The mitigation is not to map — it is to set a kill criterion: if first-90-day return rate exceeds X% or sell-through falls below Y units per store per month, pull the product. The price point and simplicity make this a two-way door: the downside is capped at one product cycle and the tooling investment, while the upside is defining a new consumer-robotics category at a price no mapping rival can match.

The pre-mortem failure mode — "returns killed it" — is observable early and reversible. No alternative considered (licensing, mapping, doing nothing) beats launching a cheap, simple robot into a gap the incumbents just confirmed exists.
