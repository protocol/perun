The strongest case for this proposal: a durable leather-look synthetic gives shoe makers a way to cut input volatility and break tannery dependence, so a heavy push could capture share before competitors catch on.

The sharpest attack that survives: every risk named — factory change, higher unit cost, comfort, fashion — is a reason shoe makers will say no *now*, and the proposal commits heavy marketing spend before any of them are tested. The blast is real (heavy spend, brand positioning) and the door is one-way: once launched as premium, repositioning is costly.

Two alternatives beat "launch premium and spend heavily":
1. **Pilot one product line with one maker.** Proves factory fit, comfort, and buyer response at low cost; keeps the premium positioning option open.
2. **Do nothing now.** Wait for a tannery shortage or cost crossover that makes switching obvious.

The proposal fails the better-way test: it jumps from material invention to heavy market spend with no evidence shoe makers will switch, no cost-crossover timeline, and no comfort data from actual wear.

Kill criteria missing: the proposal states no threshold at which it would pull back (e.g., if fewer than X of Y makers sampled it in 6 months, stop). Without that, heavy spend can run far past the point of clear failure.

The core unresolved question: **has any shoe maker agreed to trial it?** If no, heavy marketing spend is premature — you'd be promoting a material to makers who haven't confirmed it works in their factories or on feet.

DECISION: NO-GO
