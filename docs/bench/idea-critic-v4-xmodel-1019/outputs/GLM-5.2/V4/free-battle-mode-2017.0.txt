First call: GO. The upside is a category at its peak and the cost is reused assets in two months; the risks are the standard risks of any fast follower, not a blocker.

Steelman: a studio with a shipped engine, art and netcode can be the fast follower in a genre whose winner has proven demand but not yet locked the market. The door is two-way: a separate free title can be sunset without touching the paid game.

Blockers: NONE.
- "Rival owns the genre" is a market risk, not a fact in hand that shows failure. PUBG itself was a fast follower of earlier battle royale mods.
- The proposal's own figures survive: reused assets + two months is tight but not self-contradictory, and the separate-title structure protects the paid product from direct cannibalization.
- "Hasn't run F2P before" is an execution risk, not a blocker.

Risks: two-month timeline, F2P monetization inexperience, potential paid-game cannibalization, incumbent's head start.

Kill criteria: launch within the two-month window or hold; cosmetic revenue per daily active user below a set threshold within 30 days post-launch triggers pivot or sunset. Leading metric: day-7 retention on the free title, reviewed at the two-week mark.

Strongest attack survived: "Two months is too short to ship a polished battle royale mode, so the launch will be broken and the brand damaged." This fails because the proposal explicitly reuses existing assets and engine from a shipped game, the separate-title structure isolates brand risk, and the genre was still early enough that a fast follower with proven infrastructure could compete.

Better ways considered: do nothing (safe but cedes a proven market), build the mode into the existing paid game (slower, risks the installed base), or wait to learn from the rival's mistakes (costs the fast-follower window). The separate free title is the cheapest reversible path that still captures the upside.

DECISION: GO
