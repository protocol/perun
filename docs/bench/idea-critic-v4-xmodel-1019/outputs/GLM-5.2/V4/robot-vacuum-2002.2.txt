First call: GO. Two floor-care companies passed, but neither rejection is evidence the product is wrong — it is evidence that incumbents with mapping strategies had no reason to cannibalize them. The core claim is that a cheap, simple robot beats doing nothing and beats expensive mapping rivals in a market that does not yet exist. That survives the sharpest attack: random coverage misses spots. Yes — but the comparison is not "perfect cleaning," it is "a room that gets swept every day by a robot vs. one that gets vacuumed weekly by a human." Daily randomized passes will, in aggregate, cover most of the floor most of the time. The kill criterion is observable: return rate and complaint volume in the first 90 days. The price point makes the bet small for consumers and reversible for the company. No consumer-product experience is a real risk, but it is solved by hiring, not by abandoning the product. The strongest alternative — license the technology to an incumbent — has already been tried and failed. Do nothing means watching a category emerge without a position in it. The pre-mortem is clear: this fails if the cleaning quality is so poor that buyers feel cheated, or if the price cannot hold at a profitable margin. Both are testable in a pilot. The door is two-way: a failed launch in 2002 does not foreclose a revised attempt, and the capital at risk is modest relative to a robotics R&D budget. The proposal is not a bet on random coverage being optimal; it is a bet on "good enough, cheap, and first" beating "perfect, expensive, and later." That bet, on the facts given, is worth taking. Cap the first run at a fixed unit volume, set the 90-day return rate threshold, and go.

DECISION: GO
