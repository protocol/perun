First call: GO. The genre is validated, assets exist, and standing still hands the market to the rival.

Steelman: a studio with a shipped game and reusable assets can enter a proven, exploding market at a fraction of the rival's cost, and cosmetics monetize engagement without splitting the player base of the paid title.

Better-way: do nothing. The rival owns the genre today, but the paid game's revenue is the studio's lifeline; spending two months on a free mode risks both. Still, in a winner-take-most landgrab, defending the existing title is slow-motion surrender. The separate-title approach beats bolting the mode onto the paid game, which would directly cannibalize it.

Skeptic: the two-month timeline is the sharpest objection. But the proposal's core logic is reusing existing assets, not building from scratch. The timeline is tight, not impossible, and speed is the entire point: every week of delay is market share ceded to the incumbent.

Kill criteria: if the free title cannot retain players past week one, or if cosmetic conversion is near zero at the thirty-day mark, pivot or sunset. Leading metric: day-seven retention; review at thirty days.

Pre-mortem: the free title launches buggy and unfinished, gets review-bombed, and damages the studio's brand while pulling no one from the rival. This is real but survivable; a separate title insulates the paid game from direct contamination.

Blockers: NONE. The numbers work, the market is proven, the assets exist, and the downside is a separate title that fails without sinking the studio. The risks—incumbent dominance, cannibalization, timeline pressure, inexperience with free-to-play—are all real, but none shows the plan fails as proposed; they become the kill criteria above.

DECISION: GO
