**First call: GO.** The company's core asset is computer-animation talent; a feature is the only path that turns that talent into ownership of a new medium, and the downside is bounded by the publisher model.

**Steelman:** The studio that tried to hire the lead animator is signaling that the talent is more valuable inside the company than poached. A production deal lets a small shop attempt a category-defining first (all-CG feature) while the larger partner bears distribution risk. The toy's-point-of-view concept is small in scope (few characters, controlled environments), which fits a team scaling up from shorts.

**Alternatives:** *Do nothing* — keep making shorts and commercials; safe, but the company stays a service shop and the medium's first feature goes to someone else. *Hire deal* — the lead animator leaves for the studio; the company loses its irreplaceable asset. The proposal beats both.

**Pre-mortem:** The film runs over budget and schedule; rendering costs balloon; the studio exercises distribution control and the small company sees little upside. One-way door on the talent: if the lead animator walks during production, the project fails.

**Blockers:** NONE. Every risk listed is a *risk*, not a blocker: no prior all-CG feature is the opportunity, not the objection; the company has shipped computer-animated shorts, so the capability is demonstrated, not assumed.

**Risks → kill criteria:**
- Rendering cost per finished minute exceeds 2× the short-film baseline by the first 30 seconds of final output → cap spend or revise pipeline.
- Production schedule slips past 24 months before 50% completion → restructure or stop.
- Lead animator departs → project cannot continue as scoped.

**Leading metric:** cost per rendered frame against the short-film benchmark; review at first 90 seconds of final-quality output.

**Dissent ledger:** distribution control and the power asymmetry are real but are the price of entry; they become negotiation terms, not reasons to decline.

DECISION: GO
