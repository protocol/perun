**First call: NO-GO.** The proposal's own evidence contradicts its core claim: a four-city test showed disappointing sales, yet the plan treats office-worker reception as the deciding signal. The same population that didn't buy in the test is being asked a weaker question — "would you buy it?" — where social-desirability bias and the gift effect inflate yes-rates far above actual purchase behavior. The kill criterion (most recipients say yes) measures intent, not revenue.

**Blockers:**
1. **Numbers contradict the claim.** The test already ran in four cities and failed. The proposal doesn't say what was learned, what changed, or why a weaker signal (survey) would be more reliable than the stronger signal already in hand (actual sales). A second test must explain the first failure.
2. **The kill criterion doesn't kill.** "Most recipients say they would buy" has no purchase data behind it. Free samples generate gratitude, not demand. A yes-rate of 60% on a free-sample survey could correspond to 3% actual repurchase — indistinguishable from the first test's result. The criterion needs a threshold tied to repeat purchase or paid conversion, not stated intent.

**Risks (become kill criteria):**
- Free samples in one city won't reveal whether *households* (the national market) adopt, only offices.
- No stated marketing budget for national rollout; if the budget doesn't exist, the test has no downstream value.
- Rename alone doesn't address why the first launch failed (positioning? distribution? price? awareness?).

**Strongest attack survived:** None — the attack that the proposal's own prior test already disproves demand, and the proposed criterion measures a weaker proxy, was not refuted by any fact in the proposal.

**Recommendation:** Don't approve as proposed. The right next step is a paid pilot (small charge, not free) in offices *and* households, with a kill criterion of X% repeat purchase within 30 days at price Y. If the company can't name X, Y, and the budget, the project isn't ready.

DECISION: NO-GO
