First call: NO-GO. Every risk raised is a self-admitted blocker the proposal does not answer.

**Claim:** A ~$1,000 living-room player for interactive, music and photo discs, positioned between a game console and a multimedia PC.

**Steelman:** Convergence is coming; whoever owns the TV-adjacent platform early locks in the installed base and the title pipeline before PC multimedia closes the window.

**Blockers (facts stated in the proposal itself):**
1. **Price vs. the anchor buyers know.** ~$1,000 is 3-5× a game console. The proposal names this gap and offers no answer (no subsidy, no bundle, no lower-cost SKU). A product priced 3-5× its nearest substitute, whose differentiation buyers "may not understand," has no demand path at launch.
2. **The title library is small — and small is the killer.** Interactive discs are the *only* feature separating this from a CD player or a PC. If the library is small at launch, the one differentiating benefit is absent, so the $1,000 premium has no justification. The proposal states this as already true, not as a risk it mitigates.
3. **PCs are gaining multimedia ability.** This is a one-way door in slow motion: the substitute is improving toward the same outcome, which compresses the window for a premium, single-purpose device.

**Risks (not blockers, but unresolved):** buyer confusion (no positioning answer given); no named launch-title partner; no cost-reduction roadmap.

**Strongest attack survived:** none — the attack was not survived. The proposal's own facts show the core claim fails: the differentiating content is small at launch, the price is multiples of the nearest substitute, and the substitute is converging on the same function. No kill criterion, pilot, or cap is offered to test whether the demand path exists.

The proposal as stated has no demand path at the stated price, and the one feature that justifies the premium is self-admittedly absent at launch.

DECISION: NO-GO
