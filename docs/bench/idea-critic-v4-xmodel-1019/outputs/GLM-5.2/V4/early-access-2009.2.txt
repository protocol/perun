**First call: GO.** A solo dev with a playable but unfinished sandbox game faces a market-test question, not a quality question. The proposal is to sell early and let buyers steer development. The strongest steelman: an unfinished alpha sold directly to players is the cheapest possible way to discover whether the core loop has an audience, and that discovery is worth more than the reputational cost of a rough first impression — especially for a developer with no publisher and no marketing budget, where the alternative is not "polish then launch" but "launch never, or launch blind."

**Skeptic.** The stated risks are real but none is a blocker. "Buyers may judge the rough state" — yes, and the ones who do are not the audience; the ones who don't are the cohort the dev needs to find. "Cannot support a large audience" — the alpha price itself throttles demand; a solo dev's problem at this stage is obscurity, not overload. "Players stop buying once they see it" — this is the actual market test: if they stop, the game isn't ready or isn't wanted, and the dev learns that before spending years in a vacuum. No publisher or budget is not a risk to mitigate here — it is *why* selling early is the rational path rather than the conventional one.

**Better-way.** The alternatives are weaker. Free open alpha tests engagement but captures no price signal and no revenue to fund continued work. Waiting to polish before launch presumes the dev already knows what players want — the entire problem the proposal solves. Selling the alpha now, at a modest price, with clear "work in progress" framing, beats both.

**Kill-criteria.** Price the alpha low enough to signal early access, not finished value. Watch the conversion from "bought" to "played again within a week" and the ratio of bug reports to feature requests. If sales collapse after the first wave of early adopters and playtime drops off, that is the signal to either narrow scope or stop — not to keep adding features blindly.

DECISION: GO
