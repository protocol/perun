First call: GO. A new plant sitting idle is a cost incurred today; a car that fills it addresses a real, growing segment.

Steelman: the company has already paid for capacity and knows small cars; a well-executed compact captures families upgrading from minicars who find existing offerings too small or too crude.

The do-nothing alternative leaves the new plant underutilized — a loss with no offsetting gain. The cheaper alternative (extend an existing model) saves the dealer network cost but forgoes the volume and margin a dedicated line enables. The proposal beats do-nothing on the stated facts.

Blockers: NONE. The risks raised are real but are all execution risks, not contradictions of the plan's own logic. The plant exists; the question is what to build in it.

Risks (which become kill criteria):
- **Dealer network cost** — cap the initial network footprint; expand only as volume justifies.
- **Rival price pressure** — set a target cost-to-build below the expected market price; if the bill of materials exceeds that at launch, hold.
- **Plant capacity utilization** — name the break-even utilization rate and the date by which it must be reached.
- **Buyer preference mismatch** — the leading metric is first-year unit sales against a pre-committed target; review at 12 months, with a stated pivot (price cut, engine option, or halt) if the target is missed.

The sharpest attack: rivals already sell small cars cheaply, so a new entrant faces a price war it cannot win. This fails to break the claim because the proposal targets compact *family* cars — a segment above the cheapest minicars — where existing offerings leave room on space, refinement, and engine size. The 1100 cc engine positions the car above bare-bones economy models and below larger, pricier sedans.

The proposal is sound on its own terms. The risks are manageable through capped investment, a cost target, a utilization threshold, and a first-year sales review with a pre-committed pivot.

DECISION: GO
