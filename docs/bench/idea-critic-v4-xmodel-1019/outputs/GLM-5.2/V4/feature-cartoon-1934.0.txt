First call: GO. The shorts business funds the bet, the downside is capped at one film's loss, and a feature-length cartoon is a two-way door (release, learn, retrench) with a clear kill criterion: if early screenings show audiences won't sit through it, cut losses.

Blockers: NONE. The proposal's own numbers work — ten times a short's cost is large but not fatal for a studio already producing shorts at steady profit. No fact stated shows the plan fails as proposed.

Risks (become kill criteria):
- **Audience tolerance unproven.** Kill criterion: if a pre-release test screening shows walkouts above 20% or sustained restlessness past the 40-minute mark, re-edit to under 80 minutes or shelve.
- **Budget overrun.** Cap the spend at 12× a short's cost; if production hits the cap before 75% completion, stop and reassess.
- **Opportunity cost.** Shorts revenue diverted to the feature must not drop the studio below operating break-even in any quarter; if it does, pause new short production rather than the feature.

Strongest attack survived: "No one has paid feature prices for a cartoon; the category may not exist." This is the sharpest objection — but it is a *risk*, not a *blocker*. The proposal does not claim the category exists; it claims the studio can *create* it. A capped, test-screened first film answers the objection empirically. The family and industry insiders advising against it is exactly the consensus that a differentiated bet requires; if everyone agreed, the opportunity would already be priced in.

The do-nothing alternative — continue making profitable shorts — is safe but locks the studio into thin margins indefinitely. The feature is a one-time, capped spend with asymmetric upside: success creates a new category and a durable competitive moat; failure costs one film. On the stated facts, the expected value of the bet exceeds the expected value of stasis.

Leading metric: pre-release test-screening audience retention. Review date: 60 days before planned release.

DECISION: GO
