**First call: NO-GO.** The maker exits its own market, earns a small royalty on unproven demand, and depends on a licensee with no incentive to push hard. The stated risks are the proposal's own evidence against itself.

**Blockers:**
1. **The upside is a small royalty, not a platform.** The maker licenses its computer platform to a toy company but keeps only a small royalty per unit. If the product succeeds, the licensee captures the brand and the customers; the maker gets a fraction. If it fails, the maker gets nothing. The proposal states this royalty structure as already true, and it removes the maker's own route to the upside: there is no scenario described where the maker benefits beyond a small per-unit fee.
2. **The licensee has weak incentives to market hard.** The proposal states that the licensee "may not market the product hard" and that "consumers may not connect the product with the licensor's brand." These are not risks that could go wrong; they are negatives the proposal itself names as already true. A product whose marketing depends on a licensee with no stated commitment to push it, in a market dominated by established console makers, has no stated path to the volume needed for even small royalties to matter.
3. **The numbers do not work as stated.** The maker has a small market share, the royalties per unit are small, and the console market is dominated by established players. The proposal does not state a volume assumption, a price point, or a break-even analysis that would make the small royalty meaningful relative to the cost of licensing its platform to a third party.

**Risks:** Established console makers dominate the market; the licensee may not market the product hard; consumers may not connect the product with the licensor's brand.

**Kill criteria:** If the maker cannot negotiate a minimum volume guarantee or a larger royalty share, the deal should not proceed.

DECISION: NO-GO
