The proposal's own numbers contain the blocker. Licensees must profit on hardware (stated), and the console launches at ~$700 (stated). Established competitors sell hardware at a loss and recoup on software. A licensee pricing at $700 to earn a hardware margin is competing against subsidized rivals — the start-up's own licensing model makes the price problem structural, not transitional.

The royalty stack compounds this. The start-up takes a royalty on each console and each game. The licensee needs a hardware profit on top of the console royalty, and game developers face a game royalty on top of development costs. Every party in the chain needs margin that Sony, Sega, and Nintendo either absorb or don't charge. The proposal doesn't address how licensees hit a competitive price while covering both their margin and the console royalty.

The steelman — set the standard by proliferating licensed hardware, earning on royalties — fails because the proliferation requires licensees who can profitably sell against loss-leader incumbents. The proposal states the condition (licensees must profit on hardware) that removes the route to the upside (competitive pricing), and doesn't answer it.

Kill criteria the proposal lacks: a licensee-signed price target at or below incumbent retail; a game royalty rate that doesn't price out third-party developers; evidence that early adopters at $700 represent a self-sustalling installed base rather than a one-time spike.

The strongest alternative: build one reference console yourself, sell at a loss, earn on first-party software — the incumbent model. This reaches the stated upside (setting the standard) without requiring licensees to solve an impossible margin equation.

DECISION: NO-GO
