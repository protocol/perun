**First call: NO-GO.** The unit economics contradict the chosen business model.

**Steelman:** A royalty-based licensing model could create a broader hardware ecosystem than vertically integrated competitors, while the start-up captures software economics without manufacturing risk.

**Attack — the numbers fail as proposed:**

1. **The business model is self-contradictory on the stated facts.** Established console makers sell hardware at a loss and earn on games. Licensees must make a profit on hardware. Therefore licensees need both a hardware margin AND must pay a royalty per console — while competitors subsidize the hardware. The start-up's console launches at ~$700; competitors' subsidized hardware is far cheaper. The licensee's cost stack (manufacturing + margin + console royalty) makes it structurally impossible to reach competitive pricing. The proposal's own risk list names this contradiction and provides no answer.

2. **The game royalty collapses under the same logic.** The start-up earns a royalty on each game. But game developers already face a high-friction, high-price platform. With competitors owning the installed base and subsidizing access, the start-up's game-side royalties require a software ecosystem that the hardware pricing model actively prevents from forming.

3. **$700 to early adopters is a feature, not the problem — the problem is who eats the loss.** The real issue is that the model assigns the loss-taking to parties (licensees) who have no software revenue to recover it. No rational licensee accepts this; if they do, they exit at the first quarterly loss.

**Alternatives:** (a) The start-up manufactures itself, sells at a loss, earns on games — but this requires capital the proposal doesn't claim. (b) Licensees pay no console royalty, only game royalties — but this removes the start-up's hardware revenue stream. Neither is addressed.

**Blocker:** The proposal states as fact that competitors subsidize hardware, that licensees must profit on hardware, and that the console is priced far above competitors. These three stated facts, taken together, show the plan fails as proposed: no licensee can reach competitive pricing under the royalty structure described, and the proposal provides no mechanism that resolves this.

DECISION: NO-GO
