**First call: GO.** The market is moving and a small, separate team using commodity parts is the fastest way to learn whether it matters — the risks are real but they are execution risks, not blockers.

**Steelman:** A company with deep engineering and service capability can enter a fast-growing adjacent market quickly and cheaply by buying standard parts, opening the design, and leveraging outside software — trading margin and control for speed and market presence.

**Blockers:** NONE. The four risks are exactly the known costs of the commodity/open strategy, not contradictions of it. Margins being lower is a *reason* the mainframe business model doesn't fit, not a reason to stay out of a growing market.

**Risks (kill criteria):**
- **Copy risk** is inherent to open commodity designs; the moat is brand, service, and software ecosystem, not the hardware. Accept it.
- **Software dependency** means the machine must be attractive enough that outside developers write for it. Kill criterion: if no outside software commits within the first 6 months, the open-software premise is dead — pivot to in-house software or stop.
- **Margin pressure** means this product must be volume-driven. Kill criterion: if unit sales don't cover the small team's costs within 12 months, shut it down.
- **No retail experience** is mitigated by using independent retail stores rather than building a channel. Kill criterion: if fewer than N stores sign on in 90 days, the channel strategy fails.

**Strongest attack survived:** "A design built from standard parts will be copied immediately, so the company spends a year building something competitors replicate in 90 days — the open design is self-defeating." This fails because speed-to-market and brand/service positioning can capture share before copies land, and the alternative (proprietary closed design) is slower and contradicts the commodity-parts premise.

**Leading metric:** retail store sign-ups and outside software commitments within the first quarter; review at 90 days.

DECISION: GO
