**First call: NO-GO.** The proposal's own economics contradict its core claim.

**Steelman:** Rather than subsidize hardware and recover on software, license the design to OEMs who profit on hardware, while the start-up collects royalties on every console and game — a lighter-capital path to platform dominance.

**Blockers:**

1. **The numbers fail as proposed.** At $700, the console is roughly 2–3× the price of a competing unit. The proposal states licensees must profit on hardware, which pushes the price floor even higher. Early adopters will pay a premium, but the premium must be small enough that the installed base grows fast enough to attract third-party game developers. At this price point, the installed base stays too small for too long, and the royalty stream — the start-up's only revenue — never materializes. The proposal's own stated facts (price, licensee profit requirement, royalty-only revenue) show the plan fails.

2. **The business model contradicts the industry standard the proposal itself cites.** Established console makers sell hardware at a loss and earn on games. The proposal asks licensees to do the opposite: profit on hardware while the start-up takes the royalty. No electronics maker has an incentive to build and sell a $700 console for a thin margin when the start-up captures the ongoing revenue stream. The licensees' incentive structure is misaligned with the start-up's royalty model.

**Risks (kill criteria):** Third-party game developer adoption by month 12; installed base of 500K units by month 18; at least two signed licensees by month 6. If any of these miss, the platform is dead and the company has burned its capital with no hardware revenue to fall back on.

**Cheaper alternative:** The start-up could manufacture the console itself, sell at a loss, and recover on game royalties — the proven model. This requires more capital but aligns incentives and removes the licensee profit problem entirely.

DECISION: NO-GO
