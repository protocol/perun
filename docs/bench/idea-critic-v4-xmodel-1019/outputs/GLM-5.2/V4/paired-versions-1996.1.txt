First call: GO. Two-version trading is a network-effect play: the "inconvenience" creates the social mechanic that moves units. Declining handheld sales are a reason to ship a hit that sells hardware, not to wait.

Steelman: the link cable is unused installed capacity; two versions convert a single-player purchase into a multiplayer social phenomenon, where each player who buys also creates trading demand for others.

Do-nothing: one version, complete solo. Cheaper, but the link cable stays dormant and the game is a standard RPG in a crowded market. The two-version plan beats this because the trading friction is the feature, not a defect.

Cheaper alternative: one version with a rare-creature quest. Reaches solo completion but kills the social trading loop — the actual upside. Not equivalent.

Skeptic: the key assumption is that enough children have access to a friend with a cable and the other version. If the friend-density is too low, the mechanic frustrates rather than delights. This is unverified in the proposal.

Better-way: the strongest alternative is a single version with an optional link-cable bonus — lower risk, same hardware. But it dilutes the trading pressure that makes the concept viral. The all-in version is the better bet.

Kill criteria: if first-month sell-through is below the threshold where playground density sustains trading (roughly: fewer than X units per school catchment), pull the second version and ship a complete single edition. Leading metric: trade-frequency per unit sold in week one; review at 30 days.

Blockers: NONE. Risks: unverified friend/cable density; declining hardware base; perception of price-grab.

The numbers that matter — attach rate of the cable, playground density of the handheld — are not in the proposal. But the core claim survives: two-version trading turns hardware decline and link-cable dormancy into a social mechanic that sells software. The downside is capped (ship one version if two don't move), the door is two-way, and the upside is a franchise.

DECISION: GO
