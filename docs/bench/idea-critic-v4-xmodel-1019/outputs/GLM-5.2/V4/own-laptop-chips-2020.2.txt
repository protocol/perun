First call: GO — vertical integration in processors has historically been a winning bet for this company, and the phone/tablet design approach is proven.

**Steelman:** A company that already designs competitive phone/tablet processors can extend that architecture to laptops, capturing margin, differentiation, and cross-platform software unification that a supplier relationship cannot provide.

**Strongest attack survived:** The software compatibility risk is the sharpest objection — if existing laptop software doesn't run on the new chips, the transition fails. But this is a risk, not a blocker: the company has already solved cross-architecture software layers across its phone/tablet line, and the proposal's own design-approach reuse implies the toolchain and expertise transfer. The attack fails because the company has a demonstrated path to architecture migration; it hasn't been proven on laptops specifically, but the evidence on hand doesn't show failure — it shows relevant prior success.

**Blockers:** NONE. The proposal's own facts don't contradict its core claim. The risks raised are real but are risks, not blockers.

**Risks (kill criteria):**
- Software compatibility: set a measurable threshold — e.g., 95% of top-100 laptop applications run unmodified on a pilot device by a fixed date, or stop.
- Chip competitiveness: benchmark against the supplier's current part on perf-per-watt; if the self-designed chip is more than 15% behind at the same thermal envelope, do not ship.
- Buyer confusion: pilot a single laptop SKU first, not the whole line; measure return rates and reviews against the supplier-chip baseline.

**Better way considered:** Do nothing (keep the supplier). Cheaper and lower-risk today, but forgoes margin, differentiation, and the long-term software-unification upside. The proposal beats do-nothing on stated strategic value.

**Decision: go.** The transition is a two-way door with a reversible first step (one SKU, one pilot). The risks are manageable with capped, time-boxed kill criteria. The strategic upside — owning the laptop processor stack the way the company already owns its mobile one — is substantial and aligns with its demonstrated competitive advantage.

DECISION: GO
