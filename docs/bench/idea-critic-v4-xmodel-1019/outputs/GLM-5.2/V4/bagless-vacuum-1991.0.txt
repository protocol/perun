**First call: GO.** The stated facts show a working technology (thousands of prototypes) with a clear differentiator (no bag to buy), targeting a known pain point. The risks are real but are execution risks, not evidence the plan fails.

**Steelman.** A bagless cyclone vacuum, priced as a premium product and sold direct, captures margin incumbents can't match because their business depends on selling replacement bags.

**Strongest attack survived.** "Large incumbents will copy it and crush the new company." This fails on the stated facts: incumbents *sell bags* — their recurring revenue disappears if bagless succeeds, so they are structurally disincentivized from adopting it quickly. That's the inventor's moat, not his weakness.

**Blockers:** NONE. No stated fact contradicts the core claim. The risks listed (higher price, no manufacturing record, copy threat) are risks, not blockers.

**Risks → kill criteria:**
- *No manufacturing record:* the founder must either secure a manufacturing partner or build capacity, capped at a pilot production run.
- *Premium price resistance:* pilot in a market where disposable bags are expensive and annoying; kill if first-run buyers don't repeat-purchase or recommend.
- *Incumbent copy:* time-limited window — if a major brand ships a bagless cyclone within 18 months, pivot to IP licensing or niche premium.

**Leading metric:** units sold at full price to first-time buyers in the first 6 months of retail availability; review at month 9.

The proposal is a one-way door (founding a company, committing capital) but the *first step* is reversible: a capped pilot run sold through a single retail channel tests price acceptance and demand before full manufacturing scale-up. The inventor has de-risked the technology himself through thousands of prototypes; the remaining risks are commercial and addressable through a staged, kill-criteried approach rather than blocking the go.

DECISION: GO
