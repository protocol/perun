First call: NO-GO — the plan bets the franchise on an unproven machine in a tight window, while the product that funds the company works today.

Steelman: the current machine has hit its ceiling; a successor with the missing features captures the business market before competitors do.

The claim fails its own numbers. The schedule is tight and the design is complex — that is the proposal stating it cannot reliably ship within the year. Hardware reliability is unproven — stated as a fact, not a risk — yet the plan also phases out the proven machine. So the core route to upside depends on a machine the proposal itself says is unfinished and unvalidated, while removing the fallback. That contradiction is the blocker.

Do-nothing beats this: keep selling the working product, ship the successor when the hardware holds up. The cheaper alternative — add an 80-column card or a lowercase kit to the existing line — reaches part of the upside at a fraction of the cost and risk, and could be sold now.

Kill criteria if revised: no launch until the new hardware passes a defined reliability bar; keep the current product alive and selling until the successor meets a units-shipped threshold; a date by which, if the new machine isn't field-validated, revert to the existing line.

The strongest attack: ending the current product removes the company's only proven revenue and the installed base software makers target, leaving nothing if the successor slips. This did not fail — it is exactly what the proposal admits.

DECISION: NO-GO
