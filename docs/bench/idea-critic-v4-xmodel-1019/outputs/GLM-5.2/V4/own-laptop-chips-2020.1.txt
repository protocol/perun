**First call: GO.** A company that already designs phone processors has the core capability; laptop vertical integration is a two-way door with a multi-year payoff.

**Steelman:** If its phone chips are competitive, applying that design approach to laptops captures supplier margin, tightens hardware/software integration, and creates a differentiated platform — the same thesis that already worked in phones.

**Skeptic — assumptions:**
- *Phone chip success translates to laptop performance.* This is the load-bearing assumption. Phone chips prioritize power efficiency; laptops need sustained performance under thermal headroom. The design approach may transfer; the specific performance tier is unproven.
- *Existing software runs on the new architecture.* An instruction-set or architecture change risks breaking the laptop software library, which is the platform's value. No emulation or compatibility plan is stated.

**Better-way:**
- *Do nothing* keeps a working supplier relationship and avoids transition risk, but leaves margin and differentiation on the table permanently.
- *Hybrid transition* — co-design with the supplier for one generation while building internal capability — is cheaper and safer but delays the upside by years and splits engineering focus.
- The full transition beats do-nothing on margin and platform control if the compatibility assumption holds.

**Kill-criteria:**
- A compatibility benchmark: key laptop applications must run at acceptable performance on the new processors by a fixed date, or the transition pauses.
- A performance parity target: the self-designed laptop chip must match the supplier's on standard laptop benchmarks within one generation, or the supplier relationship is maintained.
- A buyer-confusion metric: if laptop sales drop beyond a threshold during the transition window, pause and re-message.

**Pre-mortem:** The transition fails if the new laptop chips cannot match the supplier's sustained performance, or if software compatibility breaks the platform's value proposition. The cost of delay is one generation of supplier margin and continued platform dependence — real but bounded.

**Strongest attack survived:** "Phone chip success does not guarantee laptop performance parity." This is a genuine risk, but the proposal does not claim guaranteed parity — it claims the design approach transfers, which is testable via the kill criteria above. The company has the capability; the question is execution, not feasibility.

DECISION: GO
