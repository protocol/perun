**First call: NO-GO.** Auto-enrolling an existing email user base into a social layer with default-exposed connections is a one-way door that bets user trust against an unproven engagement hypothesis — the downside is asymmetric and irreversible.

**Steelman:** The company already has the graph (contacts, frequency), the distribution (every user visits daily), and the switching cost (your inbox is your identity). A social layer could lock in engagement at near-zero acquisition cost.

**Better-way:** Opt-in enrollment with private-by-default settings captures willing users first, tests the engagement hypothesis honestly, and makes the privacy story a feature rather than a liability. The cost is a smaller launch-day number; the benefit is avoiding the trust collapse entirely.

**Skeptic:** The proposal conflates "we have the users" with "they want this here." Email is a tool people use for deliberate, private communication; a social layer inside it competes with the mental model users already hold. The strongest version of the claim — that the existing graph makes adoption trivial — is also the strongest objection: that same graph is exactly what makes auto-exposure dangerous. The privacy risk is not a side effect; it is the core mechanism being leveraged, and defaulting it to exposed is a choice, not a necessity.

**Kill-criteria:** If the company cannot answer "what metric, at what threshold, tells us to roll this back before regulators or press do" — the proposal is incomplete, not wrong. A one-way door without a named tripwire is a bet made without a stop loss.

**Pre-mortem:** The launch goes live, a journalist finds that a user's frequent contacts (including, say, an ex-partner or a therapist) are now visible to people who should not see them, the story spreads, regulators open inquiries, and the company is forced not just to roll back the feature but to issue a public apology that erodes trust in the core email product itself.

The proposal bets the company's existing trust capital on an unproven engagement hypothesis, through a one-way door, with no kill criteria named. The upside is real but speculative; the downside is concrete, asymmetric, and irreversible. A cheaper, safer, opt-in path reaches the same learning without the trust risk.

DECISION: NO-GO
