First call: GO. The proposal turns the game's roughness into a pricing advantage and makes buyers co-developers; nothing in the stated facts blocks this.

Steelman: an early paid alpha captures revenue now, builds a community that markets for free, and lets one developer prioritize features by real demand rather than guesswork.

Alternatives considered: finish privately then release (delays all revenue, no player feedback, solo dev may never "finish"), and free public alpha then monetize later (no revenue now, sets expectation that the game is free). The proposed path beats both on the stated facts: it generates income immediately and learns from paying customers.

The risks raised are real but none is a blocker:
- Buyers judging rough state: mitigated by pricing low and labeling it alpha; the audience that buys unfinished games self-selects.
- Solo dev cannot support large audience: the price gate limits audience size; a small paying base is the goal, not a problem.
- Players stop buying once they see it: this is a risk to monitor, not a reason not to start; early revenue itself tests whether demand holds.
- No publisher or marketing budget: the proposal does not depend on either; early adopters spread word.

Kill criteria: if sales drop to near-zero within the first month after the initial buyer surge, that signals the rough-state concern is real and the developer should pause new sales and finish the game. Leading metric: weekly paying conversions, reviewed at 30 days.

The strongest attack: players who buy the rough alpha will leave negative reviews that permanently damage the game's reputation, making later full release fail. This fails because the alpha price and labeling set expectations, the developer controls the storefront and can update the product, and the audience for paid alphas is small and forgiving by self-selection. The reputation risk is a note to manage, not a blocker.

DECISION: GO
