First call: NO-GO — the deal's own numbers, not its risks, sink it.

**Blockers (each rests on a fact already stated):**

1. **The financing structure defeats the business model.** The proposal states the purchase is financed with "heavy debt secured on the company" and that "interest payments could limit investment in stores and online sales." This is not a risk — it is the plan's own stated consequence. A toy retailer in 2005 needs store investment *and* online buildout to survive growing discount and online competition. The LBO structure taxes exactly the investment the business requires to remain viable.

2. **Seasonal cash flow + heavy debt = structural fragility.** The proposal concedes sales are "seasonal" and "a weak holiday season could strain debt payments." Heavy fixed debt against lumpy, seasonal revenue is a known failure pattern. The company has "little room for error" by the proposal's own admission — and the debt makes that room smaller, not larger.

3. **The premium rewards sellers; the debt punishes the operating business.** The $6.6B premium is a transfer to current shareholders. The debt service is borne by the company going forward. The proposal accepts a structure where the operating entity bears all downside risk while the sellers capture the premium upside.

**Kill criteria (if the board proceeds despite the above):**
- Holiday-season revenue must exceed debt service coverage of 1.5x by Q1 2006; below that triggers restructuring or sale of assets.
- Online sales must reach a stated % of total within 18 months or the competitive thesis has failed.

**Strongest attack survived:** "The premium is large; private owners can invest without quarterly pressure." This fails because the proposal's own financing — heavy debt secured on the company — creates *more* pressure than public markets, not less. The interest burden is a fixed cost public ownership does not impose.

DECISION: NO-GO
