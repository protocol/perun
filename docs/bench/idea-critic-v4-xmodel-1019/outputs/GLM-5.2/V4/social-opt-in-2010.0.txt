First call: NO-GO — automatic enrollment with default-exposed connections is the exact combination that turned a product launch into a privacy scandal (Buzz), and the stated risks are not mitigated.

Steelman: a search company already has users' most intimate data (email) and their social graph latent in it; surfacing connections could create a defensible social network at near-zero acquisition cost.

The strongest attack: automatic enrollment + default-visible connections is a one-way door that exposes who emails whom, and the proposal names this irreversibility as a risk without answering it. The pre-mortem writes itself — users discover their private contacts are being shared, press erupts, regulators intervene, trust in the core inbox product collapses.

Blockers:
1. **Irreversibility unaddressed.** The proposal states the launch is "hard to reverse once connections are exposed" but proposes no kill criterion, no staged rollout, no opt-in default. A one-way door with no tripwire is a blocker.
2. **Default-exposed privacy contradicts the core product.** Webmail's value proposition is private communication. Defaulting to visible connections breaks the implicit contract users have with their inbox. The proposal raises this risk but doesn't resolve it.
3. **No stated upside metric.** The proposal seeks approval to launch without naming what success looks like — engagement threshold, retention lift, or adoption rate — making it impossible to evaluate against do-nothing.

Risks (become kill criteria if addressed): regulator attention (FTC consent decree risk), low ongoing use (sunk cost of building the layer), user trust erosion in the core product.

What would change the verdict: default opt-in (not automatic enrollment), a capped pilot with a named engagement metric and stop date, and a kill criterion that fires if trust signals (retention, complaint rate) cross a threshold during the pilot.

DECISION: NO-GO
