**First call: GO.** The customer is already telling the company what to build: American-brand buyers want a smaller sedan. The compact-on-shared-platform is the cheapest way to answer that, and prestige risk is manageable if the car is genuinely good.

**Steelman:** A shared platform with luxury trim delivers 80% of the prestige at a fraction of the dedicated-engineering cost, capturing buyers who are already leaving American brands for something smaller and nicer.

**Alternatives:**
- Dedicated compact platform: higher cost, longer timeline, better prestige isolation. Overkill for a market signal still being tested.
- Do nothing: leaves the American-brand defectors to rivals. Fails the growth case.

**Fit:** Moves the north star — reaching buyers outside the European-luxury orbit — directly.

**Pre-mortem:** The car launches, buyers perceive it as a gussied-up mass-market compact, and the premium collapses. The one-way door is the brand damage, not the tooling.

**Blocker test:**
- *Blockers:* NONE. The risks raised are real but none is a fact-in-hand that sinks the plan as proposed. The shared-platform concern is a perception risk, not a proven dealbreaker.
- *Risks:* Prestige dilution (highest stakes); salesforce adoption; price justification vs. rivals.

**Kill criteria:** If compact-luxury unit sales fail to reach a stated first-year target (the company should set one), or if the main brand's transaction prices decline measurably in the first 18 months, pull the model or reposition.

**Strongest attack survived:** Buyers will see through the shared platform and refuse the premium. This fails because the target buyers are American-brand customers, not European-luxury connoisseurs scrutinizing underpinnings — they want a smaller, nicer car and will judge it on trim, features, and badge.

**Leading metric:** First-year compact-luxury unit sales vs. target; review at 12 months.

DECISION: GO
