First call: NO-GO. The proposal bets the company's only revenue stream on an unproven, complex machine built on a tight schedule, with no fallback if it fails.

Steelman: the current product has hit its ceiling; a business successor with lowercase and 80 columns captures the enterprise market before competitors do.

Skeptic — assumptions presented as fact:
- "Business users want these features" — assumed. Which customers, how many, at what price? No evidence of demand size.
- "Phasing out the current product is necessary" — unverified. The current product is the only successful one; killing it assumes the successor works, which is unproven.
- The schedule is "tight" — the proposal's own word. A complex design delivered under time pressure is a concrete failure scenario: ship late or ship broken.

Better-way:
- Add the features as an expansion or upgrade to the existing machine. Lower risk, preserves revenue, tests demand cheaply.
- Run both products in parallel during a transition period rather than phasing out the current one immediately.
- Do nothing: the current product is successful. Without evidence that business demand is large enough to justify the risk, this beats the proposal.

Kill-criteria (if pursued despite the above):
- Working prototype within 90 days; if not, stop.
- Pre-orders from 50 business customers before full production; if not, do not phase out the current product.
- Hardware reliability: 1,000-hour mean time between failures in testing before shipment.

Blocker: the proposal stakes the company's only revenue on an unproven machine with a self-described tight schedule, while simultaneously planning to phase out the one product that works. The numbers do not work because there are no numbers — no demand estimate, no cost projection, no revenue model for the transition. A complex design, a tight schedule, and no fallback is a one-way door through which the entire company walks.

DECISION: NO-GO
