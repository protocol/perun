First call: NO-GO. The price gap is a stated fact, not a risk, and the proposal does not answer it.

Blockers:
1. Price vs. reference category. Game consoles sell near $200. The proposal states $1,000 and positions the product "between a game console and a personal computer." A buyer comparing it to a console sees a 5× premium for a category that does not yet exist. The proposal names this gap and offers no mechanism to close it — no subsidy, no bundle, no segment that validates the premium. That is a blocker: the plan's own positioning contradicts its price.
2. Library at launch. Interactive discs are the differentiator, but the proposal concedes the title library will be small. A $1,000 player with few titles has no answer to "what is it for," which the proposal also concedes buyers may not understand. The core claim — interactive multimedia in the living room — is removed by the proposal's own stated condition.

Risks (non-blocking, become kill criteria):
- PCs gaining multimedia ability erodes the "between" position within 18 months.
- Consumer education cost is unpriced.

Kill criteria: fewer than 50 interactive titles available at launch, OR attach rate below 2 discs per player in the first 90 days → stop or reposition as a peripheral.

Cheaper alternative that blocks: launch at $299 as a console peripheral with 8–12 marquee titles, or license the format to an incumbent console maker. Either reaches the living room, tests demand, and avoids asking an unproven category to justify a $1,000 price.

The proposal asks buyers to pay a PC price for a category no one has validated, with a library it admits is thin. That is not a risk to manage; it is the plan failing on its own numbers.

DECISION: NO-GO
