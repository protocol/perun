First call: NO-GO — the upside is ad revenue, but the plan routes private purchases through a social feed where embarrassment and regret are the most likely user experience.

Steelman: targeted social sharing of purchase behavior increases ad relevance and conversion, so revenue rises.

The blocker is the plan's own evidence. It concedes purchases can be sensitive and the feature is hard to withdraw. A feed that exposes sensitive purchases to friends is a one-way door with no kill criterion named: there is no metric, threshold, or rollback described. The notification-and-decline framing shifts friction onto users after the fact, which does not remove the sensitivity problem the proposal itself states. The core claim — this earns more — is unverified against the churn and trust loss a privacy incident would trigger.

Alternatives considered: opt-in sharing (user initiates), aggregated anonymous purchase insights for advertisers, and do nothing. Opt-in reaches the same upside with lower blast radius; the proposal does not show why forced-default sharing beats it on the stated facts.

Pre-mortem: a user's sensitive purchase appears in friends' feeds before they notice the notification; a screenshot circulates; trust collapses and the feature cannot be cleanly withdrawn — exactly the risk the proposal concedes.

Blockers: the plan's own stated facts (sensitive purchases, hard to withdraw) show it fails as proposed, with no kill criterion, metric, or rollback answering that failure.

Risks: unproven revenue uplift vs. churn cost; partner embarrassment; regulatory exposure.

Strongest attack survived: none — the sensitivity-and-irreversibility objection was not overcome by the proposal's evidence.

Kill criteria if revised: a capped opt-in pilot, a named leading metric (e.g., net revenue per active user at day 30), a stop threshold, and a pre-committed rollback.

DECISION: NO-GO
