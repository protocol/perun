First call: GO. One-way door for the money, two-way door for the art.

Steelman: animated shorts are a commodity format with structural margin pressure; a feature-length animation creates a new category, establishes a moat, and captures premium theatrical distribution that shorts cannot.

The strongest attack tried: "audiences won't sit through a feature-length cartoon." This is a category-creation bet, and category-creation bets are uncertain by definition. But the proposal's own logic survives: the studio already produces shorts that hold audience attention, the technical and narrative extension is incremental rather than revolutionary, and the downside is bounded — if the feature fails, the studio retains its short-film revenue engine and the learned production capabilities. The family and industry insiders advising against it are exactly the voices whose priors are formed by the existing category, which is the category being disrupted.

Blockers: NONE. The budget is large but funded from existing earnings, not external debt that would sink the studio on failure. No stated fact contradicts the core claim.

Risks (become kill criteria):
- Audience satiation at feature length → pilot test: a 20-minute sequence shown to test audiences; if engagement drops below the short-film baseline, revise runtime or abandon.
- Budget overrun against a ten-shorts-cost envelope → hard cap at 1.5× the stated budget; exceeding it triggers a production review board.
- Opportunity cost of shorts capacity diverted to feature → leading metric: short-film output volume quarter-over-quarter; review at 90 days.

Decision: go-if — commit a capped first step (one test sequence, fixed budget envelope) before greenlighting full production. The bet is worth making because the upside is category-defining and the downside is survivable.

DECISION: GO
