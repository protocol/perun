First call: NO-GO. The proposal's own evidence — that buyers are coming from American-brand cars, not European luxury — does not support a premium-priced compact under the luxury marque. It supports a well-trimmed compact at a competitive price, which is a different product.

**Steelman:** A shared-platform compact with luxury trim lets the marque reach new buyers at lower cost and risk than a clean-sheet car, while the parent's volumes absorb the platform investment.

**Strongest attack survived:** None. The strongest attack — that the buyer profile the proposal cites (American-brand defectors, not European-luxury cross-shoppers) contradicts the premium-pricing strategy, because those buyers are price-sensitive comparison shoppers, not prestige-seekers — was not refuted by anything in the proposal. It breaks the claim.

**Blocker:** The proposal states as fact that the target buyers are American-brand customers seeking a smaller sedan. Those buyers are, by definition, comparing against mass-market and near-luxury compacts. A premium price above rivals' smaller sedans — the very risk the proposal raises — removes the route to the upside. The proposal does not answer why a price-sensitive defector pays a premium for shared-platform trim. The buyer profile and the pricing strategy point in opposite directions.

**Risks (non-blocking):** Brand dilution if the shared platform is visible; dealer resistance to a model that doesn't fit the showroom; cannibalization of the marque's larger sedans.

**Kill criterion if revised:** Set the compact's transaction price at or below the cheapest rival compact sedan in the target segment. If net transactions exceed that ceiling after 90 days, reprice or withdraw.

The core claim fails on its own evidence. The buyers described want a smaller sedan at a mass-market-to-near-luxury price point, not a premium-priced badge-engineered compact. Approving as proposed sends a luxury marque into a price war it can't win on a shared platform.

DECISION: NO-GO
