First call: GO. The proposal turns a download into a free browser app with satellite imagery and route planning. In 2004, the web is the distribution frontier and desktop software is a dead end; the acquired firm's core asset is its map rendering, which the web app preserves while removing the install friction that capped its reach.

**Steelman:** Free, browser-based maps with satellite imagery and route planning capture the coming shift to web services, build a mass audience the company can monetize later, and leapfrog incumbents who are still thinking in desktop terms.

**Skeptic:** The four risks are real but none is a blocker. Licensing costs are a cap problem, not a feasibility problem — the company has the balance sheet to negotiate. No revenue model is the correct 2004 move: Google's own later history proves that audience-first, monetize-later (AdWords on maps) wins over paywalling a free product. Established incumbents (MapQuest) have users but not the technical lead or satellite imagery differentiation. Slow browsers are the sharpest objection: interactive tile-based mapping in 2004 IE/Netscape is genuinely hard. But the acquired firm's desktop rendering engine is the exact asset that could be adapted to browser constraints via AJAX/DHTML techniques, and the company has engineering talent to solve this. This objection survives but does not block — it is an execution risk, not a structural flaw.

**Better-way:** Do nothing with the acquisition = wasted purchase price, no map capability. Ship as desktop only = stays in the dying distribution model. A cheaper pilot (imagery without route planning, or vice versa) reaches less upside and still faces the same licensing and browser challenges. The full proposal as stated beats all alternatives.

**Kill criteria:** If browser rendering cannot achieve sub-2-second tile loads in a pilot within 6 months, pivot to a thinner interface. If licensing costs exceed a capped budget, negotiate or reduce imagery scope before scaling. Leading metric: pilot user retention at 30 days; review by month 4.

The proposal is a one-way door (significant engineering commitment, public launch) but the upside — owning the web map layer — is exactly the kind of bet worth making. The risks are the plan's kill criteria, not reasons to defer.

DECISION: GO
