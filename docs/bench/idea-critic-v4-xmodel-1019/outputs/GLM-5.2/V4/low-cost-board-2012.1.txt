First call: GO. The unmet need (students who want to program but lack a machine they control) is real, the price is disruptive, and the blast radius of a small first batch is tiny.

Steelman: a $35 board that students own and can break reopens programming access that shared school laptops and locked-down tablets do not provide. The "add your own peripherals" objection is actually the cost-engineering that makes $35 possible.

Do-nothing: interest keeps declining; no cheap, hackable, student-owned device exists. Cheaper alternative: none — $35 is already the floor. Stronger alternative: a $50 bundled kit (board + peripherals) reaches more students who lack spares, but raises the entry price and inventory risk for a charity with no manufacturing base.

Pre-mortem: the first batch sells out instantly to adult hobbyists, schools never see units, and the charity is labelled a toy for makers. This is a two-way door: a small batch is reversible, and the charity learns demand before scaling. Cost of delay is low for one quarter but compounds if competitors fill the niche.

Evidence check: the $35 price is stated as fact; the risks are honestly enumerated. The strongest attack — that buyers must source their own display, keyboard and power supply — fails because that unbundling is exactly what lets the board hit $35 and because the target audience (students, schools) disproportionately has access to spare peripherals at home or in ICT cupboards.

Kill criteria: if the first batch does not reach at least 30% school/student buyers, or if average landed cost per board exceeds $35, pause and rebundle. Leading metric: units ordered by schools within 90 days of launch; review at that date.

The proposal is small, cheap, reversible, and aimed at a real gap. Approve the first batch, cap it, and measure who actually buys.

DECISION: GO
