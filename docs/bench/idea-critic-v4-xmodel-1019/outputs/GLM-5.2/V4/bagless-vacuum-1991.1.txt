First call: GO. The proposal states a working prototype (not a concept), a clear mechanism (cyclone separation), and a named differentiator (no disposable bag). The risks listed are all *risks*, not *blockers*: higher price is a positioning problem, incumbent copying is a competitive risk, no manufacturing record is an operational risk, and buyer willingness is a demand question. None of these are facts already in hand that show the plan fails as proposed.

The strongest attack: buyers will not pay a premium for a bagless machine, so the unit economics collapse. This is a real risk but not a blocker — the proposal states a working prototype exists, which means the core claim (cyclone separation works) has already been validated in practice. The demand question is exactly what a capped, time-boxed first step would test.

A cheaper alternative — licensing the patent to an incumbent — avoids manufacturing risk but forfeits margin and brand. It is a fallback, not a replacement for the founding plan, since the proposal's upside depends on owning the channel.

The pre-mortem: the company fails because incumbents copy the cyclone design, undercut on price, and lock the startup out of retail distribution. This is a one-way door (founding a company is hard to reverse) but the cost of delay is low — the patent clock is running and competitors are not idle.

Kill criteria: if a small pilot run cannot sell through at the premium price within 12 months, or if the patent does not actually protect the cyclone mechanism, stop and license instead. Leading metric: units sold at full price in the first pilot batch; review at 90 days.

The proposal survives its own listed risks. The prototype exists, the differentiator is real, and the demand question is testable with a capped first step rather than a full manufacturing build-out.

DECISION: GO
