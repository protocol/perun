First call: GO. A working prototype after thousands of iterations is the rarest and most expensive part of bringing a physical product to market. The remaining risks are execution risks, not idea-killing flaws.

The claim: a bagless cyclone vacuum priced above conventional machines can found a successful company. Steelman: the inventor has solved the hard technical problem through extreme iteration; the bagless mechanism is a genuine functional improvement (no loss of suction, no ongoing bag cost); premium pricing is defensible if the product is demonstrably superior; and incumbents' distribution advantage is offset by their inability or unwillingness to cannibalize their existing bag-replacement revenue streams.

Strongest attack tried: buyers will not pay a premium for a bagless machine, and incumbents will simply copy the cyclone idea, crushing the startup on price and shelf space. Why it failed: the disposable-bag business model is a recurring-revenue annuity that incumbents actively protect. They have structural incentive not to adopt bagless technology quickly, which creates a window. Meanwhile, the "no loss of suction" benefit is concretely demonstrable in a retail environment, which is the one marketing channel that can overcome a price premium on a new brand. The attack also assumes incumbents move fast on disruptive technology; the historical base rate is that they do not.

Kill criteria: if a retail pilot cannot convert demonstration viewers into buyers at the premium price point above a defined threshold within a set window, the go-to-market model needs revision before scaling manufacturing.

The risks cited are real but none is a blocker. Premium pricing is a feature, not a bug, when entering a market with no brand recognition, it signals quality. No manufacturing record is solvable through contract manufacturing or a hired operations lead. Incumbent copying risk is mitigated by patent protection and the incumbents' own cannibalization disincentive.

DECISION: GO
