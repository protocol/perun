First call: GO. Two established players passed, which is signal for a disruptive, lower-cost entrant, not a death knell. The randomized-movement approach trades guaranteed coverage for affordability — a reasonable bet for a first-generation consumer product entering an unproven category.

**Steelman:** A cheap, simple robot can create and dominate a new market segment that premium mapping robots cannot reach, making first-mover advantage matter more than technical perfection.

**Strongest attack survived:** "Random coverage means some spots get missed, so the product is fundamentally flawed and won't satisfy consumers." This fails because the bar isn't perfect cleaning — it's sufficient cleaning at a price point that feels like a no-brainer. Early adopters of a novel product category tolerate imperfection. The Roomba's eventual success confirmed that "good enough" at the right price beats "perfect" at a premium.

**Blockers:** NONE. No internal contradiction in the proposal's own numbers or logic. The risks raised are real but are risks, not blockers — they describe what could go wrong, not what already has.

**Risks (kill criteria):**
- Return rate exceeding X% within 90 days → revisit coverage algorithm or price
- Unit economics don't hold at the target retail price → no-go on manufacturing scale-up
- Consumer trust deficit (safety fears, home intrusion concerns) suppressing demand below Y units/month → pivot messaging or stop

**Better way considered:** Licensing the technology to an established floor-care brand. Rejected because two already passed, and licensing forfeits category ownership and brand-building opportunity.

**Fit:** This is a one-way door (capital commitment, brand entry into consumer market) with high upside if the category materializes. The cost of delay is losing first-mover advantage in a space competitors will eventually enter.

The proposal survives the sharpest attacks. The risks are manageable with clear kill criteria and a staged rollout. Approve.

DECISION: GO
