First call: GO — designing its own laptop processors is a one-way door with high execution risk, but the strategic upside (margin, differentiation, supply control) is large and the company has proven the design approach in phones.

The strongest attack: the company's laptop chips are unproven against the supplier's, and existing software must keep running — a failure on either front could damage the laptop franchise for years.

Why that attack fails to block: the company already designs phone and tablet processors using the same approach, so "unproven" applies to the laptop category, not to the design capability. The software-compatibility risk is real but is an execution risk with well-known mitigation (translation layers, dual-support periods), not a structural blocker. The supplier relationship ending is a consequence, not an argument against — it is the cost of capturing the margin and control the supplier currently holds.

Risks (not blockers): engineering time diverted from other work; buyer confusion during the transition; the possibility that first-generation laptop chips underperform the supplier's parts. Each is addressable: phase the transition, keep a product line on supplier chips during the overlap, and set a performance-parity bar before full migration.

This is a one-way door with multi-year payback. The right shape is a capped, time-boxed first step: commit to one laptop product on self-designed silicon, with a kill criterion of performance-parity-and-software-compatibility demonstrated by a fixed date, and a fallback of remaining on supplier parts for that product line if the bar is not met. The leading metric to watch is benchmark parity against the supplier's contemporaneous parts, reviewed at the first silicon return.

DECISION: GO
