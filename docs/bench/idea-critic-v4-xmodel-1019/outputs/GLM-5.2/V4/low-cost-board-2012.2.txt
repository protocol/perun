First call: GO — a $35 board, even with BYO peripherals, is an order of magnitude cheaper than any laptop and targets a gap no tablet fills.

Steelman: a bare board at $35 is cheap enough for a child to own, simple enough to tinker with, and disposable enough that a school can hand out a class set without flinching. The charity's lack of manufacturing muscle is real but not fatal — two licensed distributors handle logistics, and the mission is education, not hardware margins.

Better-way: "do nothing" leaves schools dependent on locked-down tablets or expensive laptops that students can't modify or truly understand. A cheaper, smaller, staged alternative? This *is* the cheaper, smaller, staged alternative — a first small batch is a pilot, not a failure.

Kill criteria: if the first batch sells through and schools report students engaging with programming, scale up. If it sits unsold, stop. The charity's downside is capped: no inventory commitment beyond a pilot batch, no retail footprint, no support obligations beyond a community forum.

The risks raised are all manageable: BYO peripherals is a feature (tinkering), not a bug; small first batch is prudent pilot design; no manufacturing experience is mitigated by licensed distributors; cheap laptops/tablets aren't competitors — they're locked-down consumption devices, not programmable learning tools.

The strongest attack: schools won't buy a board that requires sourcing separate peripherals, and the charity can't support what it sells. But the target isn't schools buying turnkey systems — it's students and hobbyists who already have HDMI TVs and phone chargers. The support gap is real but survivable for a $35 device with an active community.

This is a low-cost, reversible, mission-aligned pilot with capped downside. Approve.

DECISION: GO
