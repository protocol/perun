First call: GO. A studio just tried to hire your lead animator and instead came back with a production deal — that is the market telling you the talent and the pipeline are worth more than a salary. The strongest attack: you have never made anything longer than a short, rendering a feature is slow and costly, and the studio controls distribution, so you could spend years and lose both the film and the company. That attack fails on the proposal's own facts. The deal gives you the thing you cannot buy alone: a publisher with distribution, funding the production you have not yet scaled to. Your risk is execution inside a capped budget; their risk is capital and market. The hand is asymmetric, but the upside — first all-CG feature, owned creative, franchise potential — is on your side of the table.

The cheaper alternative is keep making shorts and commercials. That path is safe and small; it does not move the north star of a feature, and it leaves the lead animator one offer away from leaving. Do nothing and the studio finds another partner; the window for "first" closes once.

The real risks are not blockers but kill criteria: budget overrun, schedule slip, and the publisher's distribution control capping your return. Each is manageable if the deal sets a fixed production budget with a cap, a delivery date with a review gate, and a defined revenue share or buyout so the stronger hand cannot retroactively own the upside.

No fact in the proposal shows the plan fails as proposed. The numbers are not contradicted, the demand is demonstrated by the offer itself, and the alternative reaches the same upside only by accepting the same risk under worse terms. The pre-mortem — "they spent years, the film was technically impressive but the story did not land, and the studio recouped costs before the creators saw a dime" — is answered by the story choice (a toy's point of view, emotionally grounded) and by negotiating the revenue terms above.

DECISION: GO
