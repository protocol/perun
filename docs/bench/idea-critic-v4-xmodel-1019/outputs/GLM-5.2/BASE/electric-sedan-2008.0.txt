The proposal asks the executive team to commit to an exclusive battery-electric strategy, purchase a large factory from established carmakers, and mass-produce a full-size sedan, all while facing a severe capital shortfall, scarce charging infrastructure, and zero mass-production experience.

The core problem is execution risk. Buying a large factory commits the company to high fixed costs and mass-production volumes before it has proven it can manufacture at scale. The capital required for both the factory and battery packs far exceeds current resources; pursuing this path risks bankruptcy before the first car is delivered. Additionally, a full-size sedan is an expensive, high-stakes entry point for a new entrant. Established carmakers, with decades of manufacturing expertise and deep pockets, could easily crush a startup attempting to compete head-on in a capital-intensive segment.

The scarcity of charging stations in 2008 compounds the risk: even if the car is built, customers may not buy it due to range anxiety and lack of infrastructure. 

A more prudent approach would be to avoid purchasing a large factory initially. Instead, the company could contract an experienced manufacturer to build its first vehicle at lower volume, reducing capital requirements and manufacturing risk. Focusing initially on a premium, lower-volume vehicle could establish the brand and generate revenue. Furthermore, rather than committing exclusively to battery-electric vehicles from day one, a phased approach—starting with a pilot production run and building proprietary expertise in battery packs—would allow the company to validate demand and technology before taking on the massive capital burden of a full-scale factory.

Given the extreme capital risk, lack of manufacturing experience, infrastructure deficits, and competitive threats, approving the proposal as written would jeopardize the company's survival.

DECISION: NO-GO
