The proposal targets a genuine, observed shift in the customer base rather than relying on speculation. American-brand owners seeking a smaller sedan represent a concrete opportunity, and the approach of using a shared platform with added trim and features is a direct way to meet that demand at a premium price point.

However, the risks raised are not minor operational hurdles—they strike at the core of what makes a luxury brand viable. The concern that buyers may see through the shared platform is not just about perception; it threatens the very justification for the premium price. If the vehicle is recognized as a mass-market compact with added features, the price becomes difficult to defend against rivals' smaller sedans, which may offer more coherent luxury positioning.

The salespeople's reluctance to call it by the main brand name is particularly damaging. If those representing the product won't associate it with the marque, it signals an internal lack of confidence that customers will sense immediately. This isn't a marketing nuance—it's a fundamental disconnect between the product and the brand identity it's supposed to carry.

The risk to the main brand's prestige is perhaps the most serious long-term concern. Luxury brands trade on exclusivity and distinction. Introducing a product that salespeople won't fully endorse, that may be perceived as a dressed-up mass-market car, and that struggles to justify its premium price could erode the equity built over years. The short-term sales gain risks long-term brand dilution that is far more costly.

The opportunity is real, but as proposed, the execution threatens to undermine the brand's foundation. The shared platform must be differentiated more convincingly, the pricing strategy must better reflect the value proposition, and sales alignment must be secured before launch.

DECISION: NO-GO
