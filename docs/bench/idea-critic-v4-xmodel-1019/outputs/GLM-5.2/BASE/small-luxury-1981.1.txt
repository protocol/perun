The proposal misreads the market opportunity and creates unacceptable risks to the parent brand's core positioning.

First, the target customer shift is real but the conclusion is wrong. American-brand buyers seeking something smaller represent a volume opportunity, not a luxury opportunity. These customers are price-sensitive and brand-agnostic. They are not natural candidates for a premium-priced compact, even one with added trim. The proposal attempts to serve a downmarket demand with an upmarket product, which is a fundamental mismatch.

Second, the shared platform creates a transparency problem that will only worsen over time. Buyers in this segment are informed and comparative. If the underlying car is widely known as a mass-market compact, the premium price becomes indefensible against rivals offering purpose-built smaller sedans. The added features will not overcome the perception that the buyer is paying luxury money for an economy car underneath.

Third, the risks to the main brand are severe and asymmetric. The prestige equity took decades to build and depends on exclusivity and engineering distinction. A compact built on a mass-market platform dilutes that equity directly. Salespeople refusing to use the model name signals internal rejection of the positioning. If the dealer network does not believe in the product, customers will not either.

The downside scenarios all converge on the same outcome: the car is either too expensive for what it is, or it must be discounted to move, undermining both margins and brand value simultaneously.

The proposal should not proceed in its current form. A compact offering may be viable eventually, but only with a dedicated platform, clear separation from mass-market siblings, and a pricing strategy that respects the target customer's actual willingness to pay.

DECISION: NO-GO
