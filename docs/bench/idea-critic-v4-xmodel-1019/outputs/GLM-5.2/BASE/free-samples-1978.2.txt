The prior four-city test failed because the product lacked identity and a clear use case, not because the core technology lacks value. The new proposal addresses the exact failure point of the first launch: renaming the product and physically placing it in offices.

Free samples are not a sunk cost here; they are the most efficient way to generate trial and overcome the "why do I need this?" barrier. Office workers are the ideal target audience because the product's utility as a temporary, non-damaging bookmark and reminder is highest in a paper-heavy environment. The risk of recipients failing to see a need is precisely what the sampling test is designed to measure before committing national funds.

The proposal's strength lies in its staged commitment. It does not immediately commit the large national marketing budget. Instead, it creates a low-cost, high-signal checkpoint: if most recipients say they would buy it after using it, the company has de-risked the national rollout. If they do not, the company has spent only the minimal cost of samples in one mid-sized city, avoiding a far more expensive repeat of the four-city failure.

The alternative is to abandon a proprietary adhesive technology based on one poorly executed launch, which would waste the initial R&D investment. By focusing on a single mid-sized city, the company can tightly manage distribution, gather qualitative feedback on actual usage, and refine the product's positioning before any national expenditure.

The risks raised are valid but manageable under this specific plan. The primary risk—that office workers won't see a need—is a hypothesis that must be tested, not a reason to halt. The sampling strategy provides the empirical evidence needed to make a final, informed decision on the national rollout.
