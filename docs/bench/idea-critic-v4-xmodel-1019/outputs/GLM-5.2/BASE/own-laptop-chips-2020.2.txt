The core strategic question is whether vertical integration of laptop processors creates more value than the risks it introduces. The company already possesses deep expertise designing processors for phones and tablets, meaning the foundational capability exists in-house. Extending this to laptops is not a leap into the unknown but a calculated expansion of a proven competency.

The risk of software compatibility is real but manageable. The company controls its operating system and developer ecosystem. If it provides robust translation layers or native recompilation tools, developers can transition smoothly. The existing phone and tablet software ecosystem already demonstrates the company's ability to manage software across its own architectures.

Regarding unproven performance against the supplier's chips: the supplier has historically dominated laptop processors, but the landscape is shifting. Competing chipmakers are showing that alternative architectures can match or exceed traditional laptop processors in performance-per-watt. The company's phone chips already lead in efficiency for their class. Applying that design philosophy to laptops positions the company to potentially surpass the supplier, especially as laptop workloads increasingly mirror mobile ones.

The transition cost and buyer confusion are valid concerns but are tactical, not existential. A phased rollout—starting with lower-end laptops—can mitigate confusion and allow real-world performance validation before scaling. Engineering time is an investment, not merely a cost; it builds long-term capability and reduces dependency on a single supplier whose roadmap may diverge from the company's strategic needs.

The supplier relationship ending is a natural consequence of vertical integration. While it removes a partnership, it also eliminates supplier pricing power, allocation risks, and the constraint of fitting the company's vision into another company's silicon roadmap.

The long-term value of controlling the processor stack across all the company's devices outweighs the short-term transition costs and risks.

DECISION: GO
