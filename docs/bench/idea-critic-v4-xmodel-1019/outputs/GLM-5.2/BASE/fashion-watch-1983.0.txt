The proposal asks us to stake our reputation on an unproven plastic product while simultaneously committing to an aggressive sales target of one million units in year one. These two elements are in direct tension with each other.

If we launch twelve fashion models at once, we are spreading our bets across rapidly changing consumer tastes without any market feedback. Fashion is volatile; a twelve-model collection assumes we can predict what will sell across multiple segments. This is a gamble, not a strategy.

The reputation risk is real but manageable. Our movement-making heritage is an asset, not a constraint. The concern that an inexpensive plastic watch will harm our quality reputation assumes that consumers view all products through the same lens. They do not. A plastic fashion watch can stand on its own if positioned correctly. However, positioning it as a "quality" product would invite exactly the criticism we fear. We must let it be what it is: accessible, stylish, fun.

The competitive response—established brands cutting prices—is the most serious threat. If we commit to one million units and competitors slash prices, we will be caught with excess inventory and eroded margins. This is the scenario that could actually damage the company.

The core idea has merit. The market is shifting, and plastic watches represent a genuine opportunity to capture new customers who would never buy our traditional products. But the scale and speed of this proposal are wrong.

We should start smaller. Launch with three to four models in a limited test market. Use the results to validate demand, refine the collection, and understand competitive dynamics before committing to a full launch. This reduces our downside while preserving the opportunity.

The proposal as written asks us to bet the brand on an aggressive, untested plan. That is too much risk for the reward offered.

DECISION: NO-GO
