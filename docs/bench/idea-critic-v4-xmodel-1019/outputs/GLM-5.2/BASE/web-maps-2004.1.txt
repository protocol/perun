The proposal asks us to give away, for free, a product with no revenue model, in a market where entrenched competitors already have the users we want. We would be licensing costly satellite imagery and map data, then serving it through 2004-era browsers that struggle with interactive graphics. On its face, this is an expensive bet on technology that may not deliver a usable experience.

However, the strategic upside is significant. Moving from a desktop download to a browser-based application dramatically expands accessibility. The combination of satellite imagery with route planning offers a visually compelling experience that could differentiate us from existing map sites. More importantly, as a search company, our core asset is the ability to organize and surface information. Local search is a massive, largely untapped opportunity; maps are the natural interface for location-based queries. A free, high-quality mapping application could capture user attention, drive search volume, and create a platform for future advertising that does not yet exist.

The risks are real but manageable. Browser limitations are a technical hurdle, not a permanent barrier; we can invest in optimizing performance. Data licensing costs are a concern, but they can be negotiated or phased in as we prove the concept. The lack of a direct revenue model is acceptable in the context of our broader search business, where user engagement ultimately monetizes through advertising. The presence of established competitors means the market is validated, but their offerings may lack the integration and visual appeal we can provide.

This proposal aligns with our strengths and positions us for future growth in local search. The risks are substantial, but the potential to own a critical user interface for location-based information justifies proceeding.

DECISION: GO
