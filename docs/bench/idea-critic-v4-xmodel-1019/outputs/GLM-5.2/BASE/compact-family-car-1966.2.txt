The proposal asks us to enter a crowded, price-competitive segment with a structurally expensive distribution model and a factory that demands high volume to break even. That combination is fragile.

First, the dedicated dealer network is a major fixed cost with no guarantee of throughput. If rivals already offer cheap small cars, our new entrant must either undercut them—destroying margin while carrying new-plant overhead—or differentiate on features, which pushes price up and risks losing the economy buyer. The proposal does not explain how we win on cost or value against incumbents who already have scale and dealer footprints.

Second, the new plant’s economics are binary. If demand falls short of capacity, unit costs rise and the plant becomes a drag on the whole company. The risks admit buyers may prefer a cheaper or a larger car, which means our 1100 cc compact sits in a narrow middle ground. A product that is neither the cheapest nor the most desirable has weak pull in a market where rivals already cover the low end.

Third, the proposal assumes a dedicated network is necessary, but it does not justify why existing channels cannot be used or shared. Building a standalone network before proving demand reverses the proper sequence: validate the product-market fit first, then expand distribution. Committing to both a new plant and a new dealer network simultaneously multiplies downside exposure.

The core problem is not the car itself but the bundled commitments: new product, new plant at capacity, new dealer network, all aimed at a segment where competitors already have cost advantages. The proposal needs a clearer path to competitive pricing, a lower-cost or phased distribution model, and evidence that demand exists for a mid-priced compact rather than for cheaper or larger alternatives.

DECISION: NO-GO
