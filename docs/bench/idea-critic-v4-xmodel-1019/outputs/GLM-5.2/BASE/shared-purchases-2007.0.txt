The proposal's core logic is sound but its execution is dangerously exposed. The opportunity is clear: commercial intent data from purchases is the highest-value signal available to advertisers, and social distribution of that data creates a defensible inventory advantage. The business case warrants pursuit.

However, the risk profile as presented is unacceptable for three reasons.

First, the notification-and-decline mechanism places the entire burden on the user. Notification Fatigue is well-documented: users dismiss dialogs reflexively, meaning consent obtained this way is neither informed nor meaningful. If even one sensitive purchase surfaces publicly, the resulting backlash will dwarf the revenue gains. Medical products, relationship gifts, political donations—the surface area for harm is vast.

Second, the proposal underestimates reversibility risk. Once partners integrate purchase-sharing into their conversion funnels, and once users begin seeing (and clicking) friend-purchase ads, removing the feature creates revenue cliffs, partner contract disputes, and expectations mismatches. The proposal acknowledges this risk but offers no mitigation.

Third, partner embarrassment is framed as a partner problem. It is a platform problem. Partners who face public scrutiny will blame the network, and advertiser confidence will corrode.

The path forward requires three structural changes before approval. The feature must default to off for new users, with affirmative opt-in—this sacrifices scale for durability. A real-time sensitivity filter must screen purchases before surfacing, with a manual review queue for edge cases. A staged rollout must begin with a single non-sensitive partner category, with a pre-committed kill metric.

The revenue opportunity justifies continued development. The current proposal does not justify launch.

DECISION: NO-GO
