The proposal asks us to position a costly, unproven synthetic as a premium shoe-upper material while spending heavily on manufacturer marketing. Every risk raised is a direct threat to the core business case, and the 1963 context makes them more severe, not less.

Shoe factories in this era are built around leather cutting, lasting, and stitching. Asking manufacturers to retool production lines for a more expensive input is a hard sell. The premium positioning is supposed to justify the higher cost, but premium footwear buyers expect comfort, breathability, and a patina that improves with age. If wearers find the material hot, stiff, or inferior to real leather, the premium claim collapses and manufacturers who adopted it will retreat.

Heavy marketing spend aimed at manufacturers is therefore premature. We would be pushing a product whose real-world comfort and durability have not been validated at scale, into a channel that faces switching costs and uncertain consumer acceptance. If fashion also moves away from the look, we are left with expensive capacity and weak demand.

The underlying material may well have a future, but not as proposed. The right sequence is small-scale wear trials with cooperative manufacturers, targeted cost reduction, and evidence that consumers accept the comfort and appearance before committing to heavy promotional spending.

DECISION: NO-GO
