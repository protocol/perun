The proposal has strong commercial logic but unacceptable execution risk as framed.

The opportunity is real: a proven, highly popular genre, a chance to reuse existing assets, and a monetization model that fits cosmetic purchases. The market has shown explosive growth, and speed matters when a category is accelerating.

However, the plan asks the studio to do four things it has never done before simultaneously: build a new mode, launch a separate title, operate free-to-play monetization, and compete directly against an entrenched rival. A two-month timeline for all of this is not aggressive; it is brittle. Any slip threatens the central premise of capturing the wave while it is still cresting.

The cannibalization concern is manageable if the new title reaches a different, larger audience than the paid cooperative game. But the lack of free-to-play experience is the deeper issue. Cosmetic economies require live operations, pricing judgment, anti-fraud measures, content cadence, and community management. If monetization underperforms, the separate title becomes a cost center with no fallback.

That said, the core bet is sound. The studio should proceed, but not exactly as proposed. The two-month build should target a contained early access or limited regional launch, not a full global release. This lets the team validate technical performance, player retention, and cosmetic purchasing before committing to full live operations. It also creates a checkpoint: if retention or monetization signals are weak, the studio can iterate or withdraw without having built out a complete free-to-play organization at full scale.

The proposal should be approved with the condition that the release is phased: a narrow launch for validation, followed by expansion only if early metrics confirm the model. This preserves the strategic opportunity while reducing the risk of an untested team committing to a full-scale launch on an unrealistic timeline.

DECISION: NO-GO
