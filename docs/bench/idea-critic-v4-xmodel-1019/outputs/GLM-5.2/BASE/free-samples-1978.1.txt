The prior four-city test failed because the product lacked a clear use case, not because the underlying technology was flawed. Handing out free samples in a single mid-sized city will likely repeat this failure: giving away a product does not create a need for it. Without a compelling reason to use it, most office workers will politely accept the sample, perhaps stick it to a desk for novelty, and then forget it exists. "Would you buy it?" is a notoriously unreliable metric when asked of people who just received something for free.

The core problem is that the proposal treats a **positioning failure** as a **naming failure**. A rename alone will not convince office workers they need repositionable notes. The product requires a specific, repeatable workflow use case—such as bookmarking, flagging documents, or leaving non-permanent reminders—that makes it indispensable.

Furthermore, the financial risk is significant. Free samples cost money in materials, packaging, and distribution. A national rollout demands a large marketing budget. Committing to that path based on weak evidence from a single city's free-sample recipients would be reckless. If the signal is false, the company wastes capital on a product with no proven demand.

A better path exists: run a controlled trial where the product is positioned around a specific office task, priced normally, and measured by actual repeat purchases rather than stated intent. Only with that evidence should a national rollout be considered.

The proposal as written asks the executive team to gamble on a vague test after an already disappointing launch. The risks raised are valid and unmitigated by the current plan. Approving this now would mean committing to a costly national rollout based on little more than hope and free samples.

DECISION: NO-GO
