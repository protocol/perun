Doubling monthly production in this environment would accelerate the company toward insolvency.

The core problem is already visible in the numbers: 7,500 cars produced but only 3,000 sold leaves 4,500 units of unsold inventory. That stock represents locked-up capital the company cannot afford to freeze further. Doubling production monthly would swell that inventory rapidly, assuming sales remain flat or decline. Even if sales held steady, the gap between production and deliveries would widen dramatically.

The external conditions compound the danger. A recession suppresses discretionary purchases, and high interest rates make car loans more expensive for buyers and floor-plan financing more costly for dealers. The proposal acknowledges that dealers may struggle to finance additional stock. If dealers cannot absorb more cars, the manufacturer either sees inventory pile up at its own facilities or must extend financing terms it can ill afford given its limited cash position.

Cash is the binding constraint. Building cars requires paying for labor, materials, components, and overhead well before revenue arrives. With limited cash and rising financing costs, doubling production would burn through reserves faster while generating no near-term revenue. The company risks running out of money before the economy recovers, regardless of how efficiently it manufactures.

The logical path is the opposite of what is proposed: cut production to align with current demand, aggressively manage existing inventory, preserve cash, and wait for macroeconomic conditions to improve. Doubling output into a recession with high interest rates, a growing unsold stockpile, constrained dealer financing, and limited company cash would be reckless.

DECISION: NO-GO
