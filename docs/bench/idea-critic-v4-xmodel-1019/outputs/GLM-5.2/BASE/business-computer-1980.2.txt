The proposal rests on a single assumption: that business buyers will reward a more capable machine. The facts given do not support that assumption.

The only successful product is the company's sole proven revenue source. The proposal calls for phasing it out in favor of a successor whose design is complex, whose reliability is unproven, and whose delivery timeline is tight. That is three independent failure modes stacked on top of one another, with no evidence presented that any of them can be mitigated within the year.

More critically, the plan depends on both existing owners and software makers migrating to the new platform. No compatibility guarantee is mentioned, no installed-base transition plan is described, and no evidence is given that software vendors will support an unproven machine. If they do not migrate, the company will have abandoned its only successful product and replaced it with a platform that has no software ecosystem and no proven demand.

The features cited, lowercase letters and an 80-column display, are described as things business users "want," not things they have demonstrably committed to buy. Building a complex new product to satisfy stated preferences, while simultaneously phasing out the revenue-generating product before the successor is proven, creates an avoidable and severe revenue risk. The downside is concentrated and binary: if the successor is late, unreliable, or unsupported by software makers, the company loses both its current product and its replacement simultaneously.

The core problem is sequencing. The proposal asks the company to commit to phasing out its only successful product before the successor is built, tested, or adopted. That ordering puts revenue, customer relationships, and software-vendor support at risk simultaneously, with no fallback described if the timeline slips or the hardware fails.

A more prudent path would be to build and validate the successor first, prove reliability and software-vendor support, and only then commit to phasing out the existing product. As proposed, the plan asks the executive team to accept unmitigated schedule, reliability, adoption, and revenue risks all at once.

DECISION: NO-GO
