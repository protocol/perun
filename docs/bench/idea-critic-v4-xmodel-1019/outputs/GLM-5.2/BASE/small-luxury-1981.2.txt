The proposal misdiagnoses the opportunity and underestimates the risks.

The core finding is that buyers are American-brand customers seeking a smaller sedan. These buyers are value-conscious and cross-shopping mass-market compacts. The proposed response is to build on a shared mass-market platform, add trim, and charge a premium. This does not serve the identified customer. It attempts to move them up in price without giving them a genuinely differentiated product.

The risks raised are not minor operational concerns. They are fatal flaws:

- If buyers see through the shared platform, the premium price becomes indefensible. The customer is already coming from a value-oriented background. They will not pay luxury prices for a mass-market car with leather seats.

- Salespeople refusing to use the main brand name signals an internal confidence problem. If the brand's own representatives will not associate the car with the brand, the market will not either.

- Prestige dilution is a one-way door. Once the luxury brand is associated with rebadged mass-market cars, recovering premium positioning is extremely difficult.

The fundamental problem is that the proposal asks a luxury brand to compete on price-adjacent terms in a segment where it has no structural advantage. A shared platform with added trim is not a luxury car. It is a mass-market car with a luxury badge and a luxury price tag. The identified customer base will see this clearly.

The opportunity, if it exists, requires a genuinely different approach: either a purpose-built compact that earns its premium through engineering and design, or an honest positioning that does not pretend a shared-platform car is something it is not.

As proposed, this is a strategy that risks the brand's core asset to chase a customer it does not understand how to serve.

DECISION: NO-GO
