The proposal attempts to build global dominance immediately, but the structure works against itself. Launching in Europe and America simultaneously with offices in several countries creates cash burn that scales faster than revenue can develop.

The core problem is sequencing. A focused entry in one market would let the team validate the unproven online fashion buying behavior, refine logistics, and build a marketing engine before replicating it. Instead, the proposal front-loads every risk: multi-country operations, untested consumer demand, and heavy fixed costs. If online fashion buying fails to convert at projected rates, the company will discover this across multiple markets simultaneously while its capital disappears.

The identified risks are not peripheral; they are structural. Logistics and marketing costs in several countries at once will consume venture funding rapidly. A launch delay in this multi-country setup compounds across all markets. The target demographic of young, fashion-minded buyers is promising, but this group's online purchasing behavior remains unproven in 1998. Proving demand requires operational focus, not geographic dispersion.

The venture capital runway is the binding constraint. With simultaneous multi-country expansion, the burn rate accelerates before any market reaches profitability. If sales lag expectations, the company faces a funding gap with no proven market to justify the next round.

A stronger approach would concentrate capital and attention on one region first, establish product-market fit and operational reliability, then expand using a validated model. The current proposal treats expansion as the strategy rather than treating proven execution as the prerequisite for expansion.

The ambition is defensible, but the execution path asks investors to fund maximum risk before minimum validation. Approving this as proposed would commit capital to a plan that amplifies uncertainty rather than controlling it.

DECISION: NO-GO
