The proposal hinges on a low-margin, high-volume bet in a market where scale and marketing power determine winners. The licensor earns only a small royalty per unit, meaning massive sales are required to generate meaningful revenue. Meanwhile, the licensor bears significant brand risk: if the licensee undermarkets the product or delivers a poor experience, the licensor's brand is damaged without the upside of controlling the customer relationship.

The core problem is misaligned incentives. The licensee keeps the product revenue and controls marketing, while the licensor depends entirely on the licensee's motivation to push an unproven product against entrenched incumbents. If the licensee prioritizes its own higher-margin products or loses confidence after a slow launch, the console stalls and the licensor gets nothing. There is no mechanism described to guarantee aggressive marketing or adequate support.

Additionally, the brand confusion risk is serious. Consumers seeing the licensor's platform in a toy context may not form the intended association, or worse, may perceive the brand as cheapened. Given the licensor already has a small market share, diluting brand equity for a small royalty is a poor trade.

The fundamental issue is that the proposal asks the licensor to give up control of product execution, marketing, and customer experience in exchange for a tiny per-unit fee and vague brand exposure. The risks are not balanced by the rewards. A consumer product strategy could make sense, but not through this structure. The licensor would need either a larger revenue share, direct control over marketing and product quality, or a partnership where the licensee has strong incentives to prioritize the console. As proposed, the economics are too weak and the risks too high.

DECISION: NO-GO
