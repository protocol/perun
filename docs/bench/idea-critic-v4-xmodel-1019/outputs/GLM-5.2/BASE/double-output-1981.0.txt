The proposal asks us to double production when we have already built 7,500 cars but sold only 3,000. That leaves 4,500 unsold units, and the gap is widening. Doubling output under these conditions would accelerate cash burn, increase inventory carrying costs, and pile further pressure onto a dealer network that is already struggling to finance the stock it has.

Demand is contracting, not expanding. A recession means fewer buyers with the confidence or income to purchase cars, and high interest rates make both consumer auto loans and dealer floorplan financing more expensive. Pushing more product into a market that is pulling back does not create demand; it creates discounting, bloated lots, and strained relationships with dealers who cannot absorb the volume.

The company has limited cash. Production consumes cash through parts, labor, and overhead, while finished goods sit as illiquid inventory until sold. Doubling production would deepen the cash shortfall precisely when financing costs are rising and external funding is likely to become more expensive or unavailable. The risk is not merely inefficient allocation; it is potential insolvency. If cash runs out before the inventory clears, the company fails regardless of how many cars it built.

The core flaw in the proposal is that it treats production as the driver of success when the binding constraint is demand. In a recession with high rates, the rational move is to align production with realistic sales forecasts, preserve cash, and avoid burdening dealers with unfinanceable stock. Doubling output now would convert a manageable inventory problem into an existential liquidity crisis.

DECISION: NO-GO
