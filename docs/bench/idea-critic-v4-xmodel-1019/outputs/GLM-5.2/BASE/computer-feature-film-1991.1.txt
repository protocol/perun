The core creative bet is exceptionally strong. A small toy's point of view is a natural, emotionally resonant concept for computer animation. Toys have simple geometric surfaces that render faster and more convincingly than complex organic forms like hair, water, or human faces. This single insight solves multiple problems simultaneously: it justifies the medium, gives the artists a world they can actually render within 1991 constraints, and delivers a universal story hook.

The company's inexperience with feature-length work is real but manageable. The leap from short films to a feature is primarily about sustained narrative and production pipeline scaling, not about fundamental artistic capability. The team has already proven it can ship completed animated work. The studio partner's experience can help bridge the storytelling gap.

Rendering speed and cost remain the proposal's most serious unresolved threat. Slow rendering at feature length could mean budget overruns, missed deadlines, or forced quality compromises. However, the toy-centric concept directly mitigates this: simple plastic surfaces and controlled interior sets require far less compute than the sweeping organic scenes that would otherwise be necessary. This is not a generic computer animation problem; it is a problem the chosen subject matter already helps solve.

The power imbalance with the larger partner is the most dangerous structural risk. If the publisher controls distribution, marketing windows, sequel rights, merchandising, and final cut, the small company could deliver a breakthrough film and still gain little leverage from it. Accepting the deal as proposed means accepting terms that may permanently subordinate the company to its larger partner, regardless of artistic success.

The creative and technical logic is compelling enough to pursue, but the distribution and control imbalance is severe enough that approving the deal exactly as proposed would be reckless. The concept is right, the timing is right, but the partnership terms need restructuring before commitment.

DECISION: NO-GO
