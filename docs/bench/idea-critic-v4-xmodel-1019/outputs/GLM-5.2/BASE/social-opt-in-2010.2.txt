The proposal presents three compounding risks that make approval as proposed unwise.

First, the core value exchange is misaligned. Users chose a webmail service for private communication. Automatically enrolling them into a social layer shifts their personal data into a visible context without meaningful consent. Default privacy settings that expose connections to others are not a minor configuration detail; they are the foundation of the product experience. Starting from a position that requires users to restrict access rather than opt in creates immediate distrust and invites the exact regulatory scrutiny the proposal acknowledges.

Second, the stated difficulty of reversal is decisive. If connections are exposed and the product is later found to be unwanted, the company cannot unring the bell. Users whose contact graphs were revealed will have suffered a concrete privacy harm regardless of any subsequent fix. A launch that creates irreversible exposure before validating user demand inverts the normal risk sequence. The company should learn whether users want this feature at a small scale before committing to an architecture that cannot be walked back.

Third, the engagement hypothesis is untested. The proposal assumes that a social network embedded in an inbox will generate sustained use, but it offers no evidence. If the feature fails to retain users, the company will have spent its credibility on privacy for no lasting gain. The combination of high privacy risk, irreversibility, and unvalidated engagement assumptions means the downside is both severe and likely.

The path forward is not to abandon the social layer but to change the launch conditions. Enrollment should be voluntary, defaults should protect visibility of connections, and the company should pilot the feature with a willing subset of users to measure real engagement before exposing a broader population. These changes preserve the strategic goal while reducing the likelihood of an irreversible mistake.

DECISION: NO-GO
