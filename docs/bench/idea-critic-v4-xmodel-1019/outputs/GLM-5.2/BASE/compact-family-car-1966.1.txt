The proposal asks us to launch an 1100 cc compact family car through a dedicated dealer network at our new plant. The risks raised are not minor operational frictions; they are foundational challenges to the unit economics.

First, building a dedicated dealer network introduces a massive fixed cost before a single car is sold. If we pair this with a new plant that must run near capacity to pay off, we are stacking two heavy fixed-cost burdens on top of each other. Any shortfall in demand immediately threatens our break-even point.

Second, the competitive landscape is unfavorable. Rivals already sell small cars at low prices. To win share, we would either squeeze our margins to match them or differentiate enough to justify a premium—neither of which is guaranteed in this segment.

Third, the demand risk is real. Buyers may prefer a cheaper car, in which case our 1100 cc model is too expensive, or a larger car, in which case it is too small. We are aiming at a narrow middle ground in a market where preferences may be polarizing away from us.

The core problem is that every major assumption—demand stability, dealer network viability, plant capacity utilization, and competitive pricing—points to high downside risk with no clear mechanism for upside protection. The proposal does not demonstrate how the new plant’s output will be absorbed if initial demand falls short, nor does it address how the dedicated dealer network will achieve sufficient throughput to justify its cost.

Approving this as proposed would commit us to a path where multiple independent variables must all break in our favor simultaneously. The prudent course is to revisit the strategy: consider whether the existing plant and dealer channels can be leveraged instead, whether a different engine size better matches demand, and whether a phased rollout could reduce fixed-cost exposure.

DECISION: NO-GO
