The core economic bet is that the film's brand will drive sales regardless of quality, which is plausible but unverified. The proposal asks the company to bet its entire balance sheet on a single programmer finishing a rushed game in five weeks.

The fatal flaw is the sequencing of risk. The company is taking three massive risks simultaneously: extreme development crunch, quality uncertainty, and a massive inventory bet. If the programmer fails, falls ill, or the game is simply unplayable, the company is left with a high license fee, no product, and potentially millions in unsold cartridges.

The "large production run" is the trigger. Cartridge manufacturing in 1982 had long lead times, so the inventory order must be placed before the game is finished or tested. This means the company is committing to the inventory spend blind. If the game is poor, the brand may carry initial sales, but returns and markdowns will destroy margins on a huge volume of unsold stock.

The proposal as stated offers no mitigation: no milestone reviews, no option to test market, no ability to reduce the run if development slips, and no contingency if the programmer cannot deliver. Approving this as proposed means accepting a binary outcome: either a modest success or a loss that could threaten the company's survival, all for one title.

The risks are not merely high; they are correlated and unmanaged. The development timeline makes quality failure likely, and the inventory commitment makes that failure catastrophic. This is not a calculated risk; it is a gamble with no fallback.

DECISION: NO-GO
