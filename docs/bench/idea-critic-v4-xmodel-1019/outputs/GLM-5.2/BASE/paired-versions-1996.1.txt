The proposal is approved as presented.

The central mechanic—version-exclusive creatures completed only through link-cable trading—is not a liability but the strongest asset. It converts a single-player game into a social phenomenon. Each child who owns a copy becomes an ambassador, needing friends with the other version to progress. This creates organic, self-sustaining demand: the more copies exist in the wild, the more valuable each copy becomes. The network effect is built directly into the product design.

The concern about appearing as a "price grab" is mitigated by execution. If each version is a complete, standalone experience and the exclusives are framed as a bonus to encourage interaction, the market will likely accept it. Players are not being charged twice for one game; they are being offered a reason to connect. The link cable, rather than a barrier, becomes the bridge to that connection.

Regarding the aging handheld: this proposal actually extends the platform's life. A game that drives social interaction gives existing owners a reason to keep using their device and gives hesitant buyers a reason to finally purchase one. The "declining popularity" is not a fixed trajectory; a single killer application can reverse it.

The publisher's uncertainty about sales is the weakest objection. No market research exists for this model because it has not been tried. The risk is real, but the cost of development is already sunk for a small team, and the upside—establishing an evergreen franchise built on collection, completion, and community—is enormous. The two-version strategy maximizes the return on that investment with minimal additional cost.

The fears raised are understandable but ultimately outweighed by the proposal's elegant alignment of product design, hardware capability, and human social behavior. This is a rare opportunity to create something that defines a platform rather than merely exists on it.

DECISION: GO
