The proposal carries fundamental flaws that make approval unwise. The core issue is the mismatch between user expectations and the product experience. People treat email as a private, confidential space. Automatically enrolling them into a social network, with visible connections by default, violates that expectation. The risk is not merely user discomfort; it is a breach of trust that could damage the core email business itself.

The privacy concerns are well-founded. Default settings that expose connections without explicit consent are likely to provoke strong negative reactions. This is compounded by the regulatory risk. In 2010, privacy scrutiny on large technology companies is intensifying. Launching with settings that disregard user consent invites exactly the kind of regulatory attention that could hamper not just this product but the broader business.

The difficulty of reversing the launch is a critical strategic problem. Once connections are exposed, they cannot be un-exposed. A failed launch under these conditions would leave lasting damage: eroded user trust, potential regulatory action, and a contaminated product that is harder to relaunch successfully later.

The usage concern also merits weight. A social network embedded in an inbox faces an identity problem. Email addresses are not social identities; they are communication endpoints. Without a compelling reason for ongoing engagement, the product risks becoming a feature that users ignore or actively disable, which would make the privacy and regulatory risks taken completely unjustified.

A viable path would require fundamental changes: opt-in enrollment rather than automatic, privacy-preserving defaults that require explicit user action to share, and a clear value proposition that distinguishes the social experience from the email experience. The current proposal does not meet these conditions.

DECISION: NO-GO
