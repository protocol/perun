The proposal rejects the one mechanism that makes premium hardware viable in this market. Existing leaders sell consoles at a loss and recoup through game sales, which lets them reach mass-market prices. Here, licensees must profit on hardware, so the floor price stays high. The start-up then stacks its own royalty on top, pushing the final retail price even further beyond what consumers accept. Every incentive is misaligned.

The $700 launch price is fatal. Early adopters will pay premiums, but not triple the cost of proven systems with deep game libraries. At that price, installed base stays tiny. With a tiny installed base, no third-party developer writes games. With no games, no one buys the console. The royalty model depends on volume that this pricing structure makes impossible.

Licensing to multiple manufacturers is supposed to create competition and drive down prices, but licensees need hardware margins, so they have no room to cut. They also bear manufacturing and inventory risk with no software upside, making the license unattractive to exactly the partners the start-up needs.

The core bet is that superior graphics will establish a new standard. But graphics alone have never been sufficient. The market has multiple entrenched competitors with exclusive titles, developer relationships, and war chests built to absorb hardware losses for years. A start-up entering at a higher price point, with no guaranteed game supply, dependent on licensees who need margins, faces a structural trap rather than a solvable risk.

The model must be redesigned so that the entity earning software royalties also absorbs or subsidizes hardware cost, bringing retail price into a competitive range. Without that change, the unit economics guarantee failure.

DECISION: NO-GO
