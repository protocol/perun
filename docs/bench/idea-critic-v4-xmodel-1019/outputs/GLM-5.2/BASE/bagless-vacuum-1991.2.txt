The core technology is proven: thousands of prototypes validate that the cyclone concept works in practice. The manufacturing-risk concern is overstated, because the alternative—licensing the design to incumbents—has already failed; those firms have every incentive to suppress a technology that eliminates their high-margin replacement-bag revenue. Selling the business outright is unattractive for the same reason: dominant players will discount the invention to protect their existing model. If the inventor does not build this company himself, the innovation stalls.

The pricing objection is real but manageable. The machine is more expensive up front, yet it removes the ongoing cost and inconvenience of bags. Framed as total cost of ownership and sustained suction performance, the premium is defensible to a quality-focused buyer. The absence of a manufacturing record is a logistics problem, not a fatal flaw; experienced contract partners and disciplined operations governance can close the gap. Incumbents could attempt to copy the cyclone design, but their bag-dependent revenue streams create organizational inertia that slows rapid imitation. Patents and first-mover advantage provide a defensible window.

Market timing is favorable. Consumers in 1991 are accustomed to mediocre suction and recurring bag purchases. A clearly differentiated product with visible performance benefits can carve premium shelf space and command direct-to-consumer attention. The risk of remaining with the status quo is invisibility: a breakthrough invention left uncommercialized generates zero return and invites eventual imitation anyway.

The proposal faces genuine hurdles in cost, distribution, and production scaling. Yet the alternative paths are closed, the technology is validated, the pricing model is coherent, and the competitive landscape favors an agile entrant over entrenched incumbents. Approving the proposal as presented aligns capital with a proven prototype and a clear market opportunity.
