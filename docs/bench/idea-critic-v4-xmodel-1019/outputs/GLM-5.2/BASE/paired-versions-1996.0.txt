The proposal rests on a defensible insight: trading turns the two versions from a potential price grab into a social engine. Each copy becomes more valuable as more people play, which directly addresses the publisher's uncertainty about sales. The link cable requirement, raised as a risk, is actually the mechanism that makes this work—players need each other, which creates word-of-mouth and peer recruitment.

The declining popularity of the handheld is a real concern, but it cuts both ways. A breakout hit could extend the platform's life and justify continued support. The risk of appearing opportunistic is manageable through messaging: the versions are complementary, not redundant, and trading is framed as a feature rather than a tactic. The development cost of a second version is likely modest since the core engine and most content are shared.

The main vulnerability is the network effect working in reverse. If initial adoption is low, trading partners are scarce, completion feels impossible, and momentum stalls. The proposal as stated does not address how the first wave of players will be reached or how the trading loop will be seeded.

This is a high-variance bet with a clever core mechanic, but the publisher's own doubt about whether the game will sell is the decisive factor. Launching two versions of an unproven title on a declining platform, without a clear plan to guarantee the trading loop activates, is too much risk to approve now. A limited single-version release or a pilot in one region would test demand and social dynamics before committing to the dual-version strategy.

DECISION: NO-GO
