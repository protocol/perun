The proposal attempts to disrupt an entrenched standard with a clean-slate design. Historically, the dominant architecture possesses a massive advantage: a vast library of existing software. By relying entirely on emulation for legacy programs, the proposal accepts a severe performance penalty. Users and enterprises rarely tolerate sluggish performance for critical applications, creating a high barrier to adoption.

Furthermore, the design shifts an immense burden onto the compiler. Extracting instruction-level parallelism automatically is a complex challenge. If compilers cannot optimize code effectively for this new architecture, the hardware's potential remains untapped, and performance suffers additionally.

The competitive risk is equally dire. Rivals can extend the existing dominant architecture to 64 bits, maintaining full backward compatibility. This path offers customers a seamless transition with no emulation overhead. A compatible 64-bit extension would likely capture the market quickly, leaving this proposed joint venture obsolete before it gains traction.

Given the massive installed base, the difficulty of compiler development, the emulation performance penalty, and the high likelihood of a compatible competitor winning the market, approving this proposal as-is carries too much risk. The potential rewards do not justify the technical and market uncertainties.

DECISION: NO-GO
