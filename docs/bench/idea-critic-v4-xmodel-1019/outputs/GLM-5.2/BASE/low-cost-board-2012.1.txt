The proposal targets a clear unmet need: giving students hands-on, low-cost access to programming. At $35, the price point is compelling and distinct from ordinary laptops and tablets, which remain far more expensive and less open.

The concerns raised are real but manageable. The requirement for buyers to add their own display, keyboard and power supply is not a fatal flaw; it is a design choice that keeps the core device cheap and flexible. Many schools already have monitors and peripherals, and a bare-board approach is acceptable for an educational tool.

A small first batch selling out is not a risk; it is evidence of demand and a useful signal for scaling. The charity’s lack of manufacturing or support experience is the most serious issue, but the plan mitigates it by using licensed distributors, which shifts logistics and fulfilment to parties with that capability. Support can be community-led, which is appropriate for an open educational device.

The competitive landscape is not decisive. Cheap laptops and tablets serve different purposes and are not direct substitutes for a small, hackable board intended to rekindle programming interest.

Overall, the proposal is coherent, low-cost, and well-targeted. The risks are identifiable and addressable without changing the core concept.

DECISION: GO
