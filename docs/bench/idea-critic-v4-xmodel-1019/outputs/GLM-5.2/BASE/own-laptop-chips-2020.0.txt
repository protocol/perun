The proposal presents a strategically sound transition. The company's existing experience designing processors for phones and tablets establishes a credible technical foundation. While the risks raised are material, they are manageable and outweighed by the long-term benefits.

Software compatibility is the primary concern. Laptops run complex operating systems and third-party applications built for the supplier's architecture. However, the company controls the operating system for its phones and tablets, and it already maintains software stacks across multiple device categories. This positions it well to build a translation layer or natively adapt its laptop operating system, ensuring existing software continues to run.

The unproven nature of the company's laptop chips against the supplier's is a valid competitive risk. Yet the supplier's roadmap has historically constrained the company's laptop performance and release schedules. By designing its own chips, the company gains tighter hardware-software integration, improved power efficiency, and full control over its product timeline. The same design approach that succeeded in phones and tablets can plausibly scale to laptops, where thermal and power envelopes are less restrictive than mobile devices.

Engineering costs and potential buyer confusion are short-term transition challenges, not fatal flaws. The company has successfully managed platform shifts before. Clear communication and a phased rollout can mitigate buyer confusion. The ending of the supplier relationship is a natural consequence of vertical integration and eliminates dependency on a single external vendor.

The proposal aligns with the company's demonstrated strengths: vertical integration, custom silicon expertise, and ecosystem control. The risks are real but addressable through competent execution, phased migration, and strong software engineering. The strategic upside of controlling its own laptop processor destiny justifies approval.

DECISION: GO
