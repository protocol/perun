The proposal presents severe, compounding risks that threaten catastrophic financial loss.

First, the development timeline is dangerously inadequate. Five weeks for a single programmer is insufficient to produce a quality game, even by 1982 standards. Rushed development virtually guarantees a poor product, alienating consumers and damaging both the game company's reputation and the film license's value.

Second, the large production run magnifies every risk. Manufacturing costs for cartridges are substantial. If the game is poor and sales are weak, the company faces heavy unsold inventory—a direct write-off. The high license fee already creates financial pressure; layering massive production costs on top of a likely failed product risks significant losses.

Third, the Christmas deadline, while commercially important, does not justify shipping a defective product. The video game market in 1982 is increasingly competitive; consumers have growing choices. A bad game based on a hit film will disappoint fans and generate negative word-of-mouth, undermining sales momentum during the critical holiday window.

The core issue is that the proposal optimizes for schedule while ignoring quality and financial exposure. The company has already committed a high license fee—sunk cost. The decision now is whether to compound that risk. Approving a large run on a five-week development cycle is reckless.

A better path exists: either extend development to improve quality (missing Christmas but protecting the brand), or reduce the production run significantly to limit inventory exposure. The proposal as stated does neither.

Given the high probability of a poor product, the high probability of unsold inventory, and the already high license fee, the expected value of this proposal is strongly negative. The company should not proceed as proposed.

DECISION: NO-GO
