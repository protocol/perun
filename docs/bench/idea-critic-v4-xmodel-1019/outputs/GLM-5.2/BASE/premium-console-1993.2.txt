The proposal has fatal economic and structural flaws.

Existing leaders sell hardware at a loss to maximize installed base, then profit on software. This model is not arbitrary; it creates a barrier to scale and locks in developers. Our model inverts that: licensees must profit on hardware, so consoles reach market with a cost-plus premium, while we still demand a royalty on top. At $700, the unit is roughly double mainstream consoles. Early adopters alone cannot attract the developer base required for a sustainable platform, and without exclusives, the machine is a costly PC alternative with limited software.

Licensing multiplies our risk. We would depend on several manufacturers, each needing margin, each potentially racing to cut costs or differentiate, fragmenting the platform. They have no incentive to subsidize adoption because their profit is per unit, not per game. Meanwhile, incumbents can drop price and absorb losses indefinitely, using their existing software revenue as cover. We cannot match that without massive capital, and the royalty structure cannot fix it.

The competitive field is already crowded with well-funded players who own exclusive franchises and developer relationships. Entering at a premium price, with a hardware-profit licensing model, hands incumbents every advantage: lower price, larger installed base, deeper software libraries, and stronger brand. Our differentiation rests on high-end graphics, but graphics leadership is temporary and expensive, and incumbents will close the gap within a product cycle.

The core idea, setting a standard through licensing, has appeal in theory, but it fails here because the console business is a software-ecosystem business, not a hardware-spec business. The economics of this proposal are inverted relative to what the market rewards. Approving it as proposed would commit capital to a strategy that strengthens incumbents and starves our platform of adoption.

DECISION: NO-GO
