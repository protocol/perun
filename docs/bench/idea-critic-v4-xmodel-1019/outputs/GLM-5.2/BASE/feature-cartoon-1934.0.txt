The core opportunity is clear: the studio has a proven operational capability and a steady revenue stream, but thin margins in shorts offer no path to significant growth. A full-length feature represents the only viable mechanism to break out of a low-margin volume business and establish premium pricing power.

However, the financial structure as proposed is untenable. Funding a project budgeted at ten times the cost of an average short entirely from the studio's earnings from shorts places the entire enterprise at existential risk. The studio operates on thin profits. Diverting those profits to a single, unproven feature would drain the cash reserves needed to sustain ongoing operations and survive inevitable budget overruns. This is not a calculated risk; it is an all-or-nothing gamble with the studio's survival.

The objections raised by the founder's family and film-industry insiders are significant, but they are grounded in the conventional wisdom of the market as it exists today. The concern that audiences will not sit through a feature-length cartoon is a legitimate behavioral risk. Overcoming it requires a leap in narrative quality and production value that must be validated before committing the full budget. The insiders' advice reflects the industry's current demand curves, which do not account for the potential creation of a new product category.

The proposal asks the executive team to approve a massive capital outlay, funded by cannibalizing the studio's only reliable revenue source, to enter an unproven market against the explicit advice of industry experts. The potential upside is transformative, but the execution path is reckless. The studio must first identify external funding or a strategic partnership to isolate the core business from the feature's risk. It must also develop a proof-of-concept—a shorter pilot or a detailed animatic—to test audience reception and refine cost projections. Approving the proposal as it stands would be irresponsible.

DECISION: NO-GO
