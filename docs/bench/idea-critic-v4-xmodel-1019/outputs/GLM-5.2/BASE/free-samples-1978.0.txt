The prior four-city test yielded disappointing sales, but that result is likely an artifact of poor positioning rather than evidence of absent demand. The core adhesive technology is proven and functional. The problem is that the product's value proposition was never effectively communicated to consumers.

Sampling addresses this directly. The primary barrier to adoption is not price or availability; it is the fact that office workers have never experienced the product and therefore cannot see a need for it. Free samples solve this by putting the product in users' hands and letting the functionality speak for itself. The cost of sampling is modest and bounded, especially when targeted to offices in a single mid-sized city.

The proposed gate metric—rolling out nationally only if most recipients say they would buy it—is appropriately conservative. It ties major spend to demonstrated user enthusiasm rather than to optimism. If the sample fails to convert intent, the company loses only the cost of a small trial. If it succeeds, the company gains validated demand and a clear positioning angle before committing to national rollout.

The risks raised are real but manageable. Free samples cost money, but the scale is limited and the learning is valuable. Office workers may not see a need, but that is precisely what the test is designed to discover. The large marketing budget for national rollout is only triggered by a positive signal, protecting the company from a costly mistake.

The proposal is sound: test cheaply, learn from real users, and scale only on evidence.

DECISION: GO
