The core problem is that almost every major variable is fixed at its worst-case value, while the proposal relies on a single point of failure to rescue a massive financial commitment.

The timeline is the immediate disqualifier. Five weeks for a full game is not merely "short"—it is insufficient to produce a finished, tested, and manufacturable product. A single programmer is a catastrophic bottleneck; illness, burnout, or a single intractable bug collapses the entire schedule. There is no redundancy. If the programmer fails, the Christmas window is missed, and the license fee is wasted.

The quality risk is not a secondary concern; it is the primary financial threat. The entire economic model depends on the game selling through the large production run. A poor-quality game tied to a hit film will still attract some initial sales due to the license, but it will also generate rapid negative word-of-mouth. This creates the worst possible outcome: high initial shipments that become returns, leaving the company with both unsold inventory and a damaged reputation.

The large production run amplifies all other risks. It assumes the five-week development process will yield a product that merits massive manufacturing. This is a gamble where the downside is severe and the upside is capped by the short development window's inherent quality limitations.

The high license fee is a sunk cost, but it creates pressure to "go big" to recoup the investment. This is precisely the wrong instinct. The company has already overpaid for the rights; compounding that error by overproducing a rushed product would be negligent.

The proposal must be rejected. The risks are not merely elevated; they are systemic and interlocking. A five-week, single-programmer development cycle cannot reliably produce a quality game, and a massive production run cannot be justified without that certainty. The company should either negotiate a reduced production run that reflects the actual quality risk, or accept the loss on the license fee and avoid compounding it with inventory and reputational damage.

DECISION: NO-GO
