The core issue is whether this company can profitably reach consumers despite its lack of retail experience and the technical limitations of random movement. The risks raised are real but manageable.

First, the lack of guaranteed complete coverage is a technical concern that may not matter to consumers. A robot that cleans most of the floor daily with no effort from the user offers clear value. The alternative—mapping-based robots—may appear smarter, but they come with higher costs and complexity. A low-cost, simple device can capture the mass market before premium rivals establish dominance.

Second, the company’s lack of consumer-product experience is a significant hurdle. Selling directly to consumers requires marketing, distribution, and customer support capabilities that the company may not possess. However, this risk can be mitigated through partnerships with established retailers or hiring experienced consumer marketing professionals.

Third, buyer trust concerns are valid but addressable. Consumers may be skeptical of a robot in the home, but a low price point reduces the perceived risk. Early adopters will tolerate imperfections, and their feedback can drive improvements. The company can also offer warranties or return policies to build confidence.

The fact that two floor-care companies rejected the prototypes is not necessarily a negative signal. Established companies often dismiss disruptive innovations because they threaten existing product lines. Their lack of interest may indicate that this is an open market opportunity rather than a flaw in the product.

Overall, the proposal presents a disruptive opportunity with manageable risks. The low-cost, simple approach aligns with the needs of mass-market consumers, and the company can address its experience gaps through strategic hires and partnerships.
