The core mechanic of trading is a feature, not a flaw. By splitting creatures across two versions, the proposal converts a solo activity into a social phenomenon. The requirement of a link cable and a second player is an obstacle to immediate consumption, but it is a powerful driver of network effects. When a child buys one version, they are incentivized to find a friend with the other, effectively turning every single sale into a potential two-for-one conversion. This trading loop extends the game's lifespan far beyond a finite, single-player campaign.

The concern that two versions may appear as a "price grab" is valid, but it is mitigated by the fact that each version is a complete game on its own. The consumer is not forced to buy both to enjoy the core experience; the second version is an optional enhancement for completionists. This framing transforms a perceived negative into an acceptable, even exciting, proposition.

The declining popularity of the handheld is the most significant risk. However, this proposal does not rely on the console's past success; it aims to create a new reason to buy the hardware. A game that demands social interaction could reinvigorate the platform's relevance among school-aged children, who see each other daily and have the social infrastructure to support such trades. The publisher's uncertainty about sales is understandable, but the dual-version strategy is a low-cost method to double the product's surface area without doubling development costs.

The potential upside is not merely a modest profit on a single title. If successful, this model creates a self-sustaining ecosystem where the game's social mechanics drive hardware adoption and vice versa. The risks are real, but they are manageable and are outweighed by the potential to establish a durable, long-term franchise.
