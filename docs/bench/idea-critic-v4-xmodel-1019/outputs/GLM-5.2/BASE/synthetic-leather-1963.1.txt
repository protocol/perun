The proposal fails on its core economic logic. The material costs more than leather, yet the plan asks for heavy spending to convince manufacturers to adopt it. This is a direct contradiction. There is no incentive for a shoe maker to switch to a more expensive input, especially when doing so requires altering their factories. The business case asks the company to spend heavily to overcome a fundamental lack of customer value.

The identified risks are not peripheral; they are fatal to the proposal as structured. First, factory conversion is a massive barrier. If manufacturers must change their production lines, the material must offer overwhelming cost or performance advantages. By definition, it offers neither, as it is more expensive. Second, the comfort risk is a direct threat to the premium positioning. If wearers find it less comfortable, the shoes will fail in the market, and manufacturers will reject the material entirely. Third, the fashion risk makes heavy upfront spending highly speculative. If trends move away from the material, the marketing investment is lost.

The proposal attempts to enter the premium market with a product that has a higher cost basis and a potential comfort deficit. This is a high-risk, low-return strategy. The company would be paying to convince reluctant manufacturers to use an inferior economic proposition.

The proposal must be rejected as presented. The company cannot spend heavily to push a more expensive material into a market that is resistant to change and potentially uncomfortable for the end user. The strategy is fundamentally misaligned with the economic realities of the target customers.

DECISION: NO-GO
