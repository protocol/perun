The proposal turns a non-core asset into royalty revenue with no manufacturing or marketing burden. The risks are real but manageable: dominance by incumbents is a market feature we accept by choosing a low-cost licensee strategy; small per-unit royalties are acceptable if the licensee achieves high volume at mass-market price points; and weak licensee marketing is a control issue addressable through contractual minimum spend commitments and performance milestones.

The core strategic logic is sound. In 1995, the internet and gaming console market is nascent and rapidly evolving. A toy and game company brings established retail distribution, consumer marketing expertise, and brand recognition in households that a small-share computer maker lacks. The licensor's brand disconnect risk is actually a feature, not a bug: if the product fails, the computer maker's core brand is insulated. If it succeeds, royalty revenue diversifies income with minimal downside.

The alternative—building and marketing a console ourselves—is far riskier for a company with small market share and limited consumer brand presence. It would require capital investment, hiring consumer marketing talent, and competing directly against entrenched incumbents. The licensing model converts these risks into a partner's problem while preserving upside.

The one legitimate concern is ensuring the licensee has sufficient incentive and capability to execute. This should be addressed in term sheet negotiations: require minimum marketing expenditure, sales volume thresholds for license renewal, and quarterly performance reviews. The licensee's existing toy and game distribution channels provide a credible path to market that the licensor cannot replicate alone.

Given the favorable risk-reward asymmetry, minimal capital requirements, and strategic logic of leveraging a partner's consumer expertise, this proposal merits approval.

DECISION: GO
