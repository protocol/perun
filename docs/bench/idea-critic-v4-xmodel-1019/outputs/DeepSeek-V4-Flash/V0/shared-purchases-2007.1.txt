DECISION: NO-GO

The proposal fails on its own terms. The core mechanism—sharing purchase and browsing data with friends—directly conflicts with the social network's foundational promise of user trust. The opt-out notification is a weak shield: users rarely read such prompts, and the default state (sharing) is what most will experience. The "option to decline" is reactive, not proactive consent.

The revenue upside is speculative, while the downside is concrete and severe. A single embarrassing purchase shared publicly (e.g., a gift for a partner, a medical item) creates a viral PR disaster that dwarfs any ad revenue gain. Partner sites face public ridicule or backlash, making them reluctant to participate, undermining the data supply.

The stated risk is fatal: "hard to withdraw once launched." A feature that reshapes the user experience and partner relationships cannot be cleanly rolled back. The trust damage is permanent.

The proposal does not beat "do nothing" or cheaper alternatives—e.g., better-targeted ads using the network's own data, or a clearly-labeled, fully opt-in sharing feature with no default.

The kill criterion is missing: there is no measurable threshold that would trigger reversal before the damage is done. By the time user churn or outrage is measurable, the trust is gone.

The only defensible version of this idea is a fully opt-in, user-initiated sharing feature with explicit per-item consent and an easy off-switch—not a default-on feed insertion. That is a different proposal. This one, as presented, should not proceed.
