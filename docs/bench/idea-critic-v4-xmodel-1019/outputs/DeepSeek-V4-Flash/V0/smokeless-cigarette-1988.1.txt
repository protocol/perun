DECISION: NO-GO

The core claim—that a “cleaner” cigarette will be widely accepted and defended as a genuine improvement—fails on multiple fronts. First, the taste and lighting method are likely deal-breakers for the target user; smokers are habitual and convenience-driven, and a complicated ritual will not replace the familiar cigarette. Second, the regulatory risk is not a mere possibility but a near-certainty: any health-adjacent claim invites scrutiny, and the company’s credibility is already low. Third, the sunk cost is irrelevant to the go/no-go decision; it does not make a flawed product viable.

The stronger alternative is to kill or shelve the product, or to reposition it strictly as a reduced-smoke option with no health claims, launched quietly and without fanfare. That avoids the regulatory trap and the reputational damage of overpromising. The proposal as written—wide promotion as “cleaner”—is a one-way door that commits the company to a narrative it cannot defend.

The pre-mortem is clear: the product fails in test markets due to taste, regulators force withdrawal of marketing language, and the brand suffers a public backlash. There is no credible kill criterion that would stop the damage early enough. The cost of delay is low; the cost of a bad launch is high.

The evidence provided is entirely internal and optimistic, with no independent user data or regulatory opinion. The proposal does not beat “do nothing” or the cheaper repositioning option. Approving now would be reflexive assent to a large sunk investment, not a sound decision.
