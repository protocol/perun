DECISION: NO-GO

The proposal rests on an unproven assumption: that low price and simple functionality will overcome the absence of any demonstrated consumer demand. The two floor-care companies’ disinterest is a signal, not a noise—they know their market, and their rejection suggests the product does not solve a problem they believe consumers will pay for.

Randomized movement is a genuine technical weakness. It cannot guarantee coverage, and the company has no evidence that consumers will accept "good enough" cleaning. The mapping rivals, even if more expensive, offer a tangible benefit: reliability and perceived intelligence. Competing on price alone, without a clear marketing or distribution strategy, is a high-risk bet.

The company’s lack of consumer-product experience is not a minor detail. Selling to consumers involves retail channels, customer support, returns, and brand trust—none of which the company has demonstrated. The early rejections indicate they have not cracked the go-to-market problem.

The risks are not mitigated. There is no pilot plan, no target market definition, no cost structure, no break-even analysis, and no fallback. This is a leap, not a calculated step.

The correct move is to revise: find a narrow beachhead (e.g., a specific segment like small apartments), run a limited test, and gather real usage data before a full launch. That is not what is proposed. As presented, the downside is large and the evidence for upside is thin.
