DECISION: NO-GO

The proposal’s core bet—that compiler-driven parallel scheduling will beat the dominant architecture’s momentum—fails against the numbers in front of us. The installed software base is huge, and emulation will slow it down. That alone is a blocker: users will not tolerate a performance hit on their existing programs, and the new architecture offers no immediate benefit to justify the switch.

The risk that rivals extend the dominant architecture to 64 bits is not speculative; it is the obvious, cheaper, and safer move for them. They get 64-bit capability, full backward compatibility, and no compiler rewrite. The proposal’s own evidence—huge installed base, hard compilers, slow emulation—already shows the target users have no reason to adopt this. The stated facts contradict the claim that this will win.

A better-way alternative exists: extend the dominant architecture to 64 bits while maintaining compatibility. It costs less, risks less, and reaches the same upside (64-bit success) without betting on a compiler revolution. The proposal’s only advantage is theoretical performance, which is unproven and likely to be negated by emulation overhead.

Cost of delay is low: waiting a quarter loses nothing, while committing now risks billions and a strategic dead end. This is a one-way door—high irreversible spend, no clear path back.

In short: the plan fails as proposed because its own evidence (installed base, emulation penalty, compiler difficulty) already shows the route to success is blocked. The cheaper, safer alternative reaches the same goal. No kill criterion can save it, because the fundamental premise is wrong.
