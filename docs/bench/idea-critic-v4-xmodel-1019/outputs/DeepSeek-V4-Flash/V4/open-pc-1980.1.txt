First call: GO. The company’s existing build-it-all model is threatened; a fast, cheap, open entry is a reasonable hedge.

Steelman: A small, autonomous team can ship a viable PC in one year using commodity parts and external software, gaining retail presence and learning the market without betting the core business.

Attack: The proposal contradicts the company’s integrated, high-margin identity. Open design invites copycats; outside software creates dependency; lower margins and no retail channel could dilute brand and distract leadership. Doing nothing risks irrelevance, but a rushed, unproven entry might waste resources and fail competitively.

Better ways: License an existing design, partner with a retailer, or acquire a small PC maker. But these are slower or more costly; a separate team with a one-year window is the cheapest reversible test.

Kill criteria: If the team misses key milestones within six months, or if retail uptake is nil in the first quarter, stop or pivot. The cost is capped by team size and time-box.

Strongest attack survived: “This will cannibalize focus and margins.” It fails because the team is separate, the bet is small, and the downside is limited to one year—while the upside is a foothold in a growing market.

Remaining risk: Execution speed and supplier reliability, but these are manageable and not fatal to the core business.

DECISION: GO
