First call: GO. The small-computer market is growing fast, and the proposal is a low-cost, reversible way to enter it without committing the whole company.

Steelman: An independent, off-the-shelf team can ship a viable personal computer in a year, open the door to a fast-growing market, and do so without disrupting the core mainframe business or its margins.

Do nothing: The company stays out of a fast-growing segment, ceding it entirely to smaller, nimbler competitors. Cost is opportunity loss; risk is long-term irrelevance; reversibility is high (can enter later, but at a disadvantage).

Alternative 1: Build a proprietary PC in-house. Higher margins, more control, but slower to market, higher cost, and contradicts the company's core model. Riskier and less reversible.

Alternative 2: Acquire an existing small-computer maker. Fastest entry, but expensive, complex integration, and uncertain cultural fit.

The proposal beats do nothing: it captures upside with limited downside. It beats alternatives: it's cheaper, faster, and more reversible than acquisition, and less risky than a full in-house build.

Risks are real but manageable: rivals can copy, but the proposer gains first-mover advantage in its distribution channel. Supplier dependency is a risk, but outside software is the industry norm and reduces internal cost. Lower margins are acceptable for a new, growth market. Retail inexperience is a learning cost, not a fatal flaw.

No blocker exists. The plan is a capped, time-boxed first step into a growing market. The risks become kill criteria: if the team misses the one-year window or fails to secure retail distribution, the effort can be stopped or pivoted without harming the core business.

DECISION: GO
