First call: NO-GO. The proposal contradicts its own premise: licensees must profit on hardware, yet incumbents deliberately sell hardware at a loss to win the game royalty stream. A $700 price against established competitors at far lower cost is a structural mismatch.

Steelman: Licensing spreads manufacturing risk, royalty on both hardware and games creates dual revenue, and high-end graphics justify a premium price to early adopters.

Attack: The core mechanism is inverted. The proposal assumes licensees can profit at $700, but the stated market reality—incumbents lose money on hardware—means the start-up’s partners are at a competitive disadvantage. A licensee must either match price (losing money, violating the profit requirement) or stay at $700 (losing share to cheaper rivals). The royalty on games is too thin to compensate licensees, who carry all the hardware risk while the start-up takes none.

Options: Do nothing (no entry, no risk). Build and sell its own hardware (capital-heavy, but aligns incentives). License to a single dominant partner (reduces coordination, but still price problem). The proposal’s $700 price is a blocker: on stated facts, it cannot beat incumbents’ loss-leading hardware plus game profits.

Pre-mortem: Licensees defect to established platforms after low sales; start-up has no direct customer relationship; early adopters balk at price once competitors drop theirs.

Kill criteria: none proposed. Evidence: all internal, no external validation of demand at $700.

Blockers: Price and profit model contradict market facts stated in the proposal. Risks: execution, competition, licensee churn.

DECISION: NO-GO
