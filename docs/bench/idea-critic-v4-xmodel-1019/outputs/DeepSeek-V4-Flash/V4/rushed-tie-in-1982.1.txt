DECISION: NO-GO

The proposal assumes a five-week development window can produce a quality game, but this contradicts the stated industry norm of far longer development. The evidence on hand shows the schedule is the exception, not the rule, and nothing in the proposal explains how quality risk is mitigated. A poor game tied to a hit film could damage both the product and the license’s value.

The large production run amplifies downside: if the game flops, unsold inventory compounds the already high license fee. The proposal offers no cap on run size, no pilot or market test, and no kill criterion—if early sales are weak, there is no planned stop or pivot. The cost of delay is real (missing Christmas), but a rushed, over-ordered launch risks a total loss far exceeding a missed season.

A better path: build a smaller, time-boxed first run, ship what is achievable in five weeks with a quality bar, and reorder only if sell-through justifies it. This preserves the Christmas window, limits inventory risk, and lets early sales data drive the second decision. The current plan bets everything on one unverifiable assumption—that speed alone meets the market—without a fallback.

The stated facts already show the plan fails as proposed: the schedule contradicts normal development, quality is unaddressed, and the large run is a one-way door with no exit. Approving it now would commit real money to an unproven product with no safety valve.
