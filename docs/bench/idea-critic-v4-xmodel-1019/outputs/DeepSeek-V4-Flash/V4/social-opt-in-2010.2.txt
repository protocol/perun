First call: GO — the user base and distribution are real, and the risk is manageable if capped.

Steelman: Leveraging an existing, active user base with zero acquisition cost gives the social layer immediate scale that a standalone launch could not match, and the network effect is the only viable path to competing with incumbents.

Attack: The core failure is not privacy backlash — it is that the feature generates little sustained engagement, making the privacy cost a bad trade. Automatic enrollment exposes connections before users have chosen to participate, which converts a reversible product test into an irreversible trust event. Regulator attention is a risk, not a blocker, but the combination of exposed default data and no opt-in creates a concrete scenario where the product is both disliked and legally fragile.

Better way: Launch with explicit opt-in for the first cohort, or default to private connections with a one-click "make visible" prompt. This preserves the network effect while keeping the door open.

Kill criterion: If fewer than 20% of enrolled users actively use the social features within 90 days, roll back visibility to private. That is measurable and bounded.

Blockers: NONE. The proposal's own facts do not show the numbers fail; they show a risk of low engagement and privacy concern, both addressable.

Risks: Privacy backlash, regulator action, low ongoing use, irreversibility once exposed. None are proven to kill the upside.

Strongest attack survived: That the privacy cost is not worth a feature that may see little use. It fails because the kill criterion and a staged rollout cap the downside while preserving the upside.

Remaining risk: The trust damage from a bad launch is real but bounded by a rollback date and metric.

DECISION: GO
