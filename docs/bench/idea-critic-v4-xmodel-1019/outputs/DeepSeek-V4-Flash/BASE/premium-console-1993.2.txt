The proposal hinges on a fundamental misreading of the console market’s economics. Existing leaders deliberately sell hardware at a loss, subsidizing it to build an installed base, then monetize through game royalties. Your model forces licensees to profit on hardware alone, which means your launch price must be high—$700—to cover their margin. That price is double or triple the incumbent’s effective cost to consumers, so you will only attract a tiny niche of early adopters, not the mass needed to attract third-party game developers. Without a large installed base, your game royalty stream is negligible, and licensees will see declining sales, then exit.  

The proposed advantage—high-end graphics—is a temporary differentiator, not a durable standard. Competitors with deeper pockets and existing relationships with retailers and developers can quickly match or exceed that graphics curve while still selling hardware at a loss. Your licensees, needing to protect their own hardware profit, will resist price cuts, making you inflexible in any price war. The royalty-per-game model only works if you control the platform’s ecosystem, but with multiple licensees building different variants, you will have fragmentation, no unified quality control, and no direct relationship with consumers or developers.  

The start-up has no brand recognition, no exclusive content, and no network of developers locked in. The risk section already identifies the core flaws: price, loss-leader competition, and licensee profitability. These are not minor concerns; they are structural contradictions. Approving this now would commit resources to a path that cannot achieve scale. The only rational move is to reject this structure and demand a different business model—likely selling hardware at cost or loss, funded by future game royalties, and controlling the platform directly.  

DECISION: NO-GO
