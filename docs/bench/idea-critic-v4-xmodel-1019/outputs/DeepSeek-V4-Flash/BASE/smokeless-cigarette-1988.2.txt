The proposal hinges on launching a heat-not-burn cigarette as a cleaner alternative in 1988, despite known consumer friction (taste, lighting, complexity) and regulatory risk around health claims. The sunk cost is large, but that is irrelevant to future value. The core question is whether the product’s differentiation—reduced smoke—translates into a sustainable commercial advantage. Test markets can reveal taste and usability issues, but they cannot resolve the fundamental regulatory ambiguity: if regulators treat any reduced-exposure claim as a health claim, the marketing strategy collapses. The product is not a true innovation in harm reduction—it still delivers nicotine via an aerosol with a lit carbon tip, which may not satisfy smokers seeking a ritual or health-conscious non-smokers. The complexity of use compared to a regular cigarette is a major barrier to mass adoption; smokers are habitual and convenience-driven. The promotion as “cleaner” invites legal scrutiny and reputational backlash, potentially poisoning the entire category. Given no network or files, the only facts are the product’s clear drawbacks and the massive sunk cost, which biases toward approval but does not justify it. The test markets may show promise, but the regulatory and consumer risks are not resolvable by more data—they are structural. Launching now with wide promotion would expose the company to litigation and brand damage that outweigh any short-term sales. The product is not ready for prime time; it needs a different positioning, likely as a harm-reduction tool with strict scientific backing, not a mass-market “clean” cigarette. That requires time and regulatory alignment, which the proposal lacks.  
DECISION: NO-GO
