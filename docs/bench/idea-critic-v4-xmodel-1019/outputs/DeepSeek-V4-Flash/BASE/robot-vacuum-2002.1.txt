The core question is whether to proceed with a direct-to-consumer launch despite two industry rejections. The rejections are weak signals—incumbents often dismiss disruptive low-end entrants. The randomized movement is a deliberate cost trade-off, not a flaw, if the value proposition is "good enough cleaning at a fraction of the price." The company’s lack of consumer experience is a real liability, but mitigable through packaging, simple instructions, and a strong return policy. Trust is the largest hurdle, but a low entry price lowers the perceived risk of trial. The mapping rivals target a premium segment; this product targets a mass market that values price over perfection. The risk of incomplete coverage is acceptable if marketing sets honest expectations—"regular maintenance cleaning" rather than "deep clean." The timing (2002) is early for smart-home hype, but also means less competitive noise. The company can learn directly from consumer feedback, iterate quickly, and build a brand before mapping costs drop. The two rejections actually free the company from channel constraints, allowing direct online and big-box retail sales where price and novelty win. The biggest danger is over-engineering or over-promising; the proposal’s simplicity is its strength. If the unit cost is truly low and margins exist, a niche but viable market is plausible. The executive team’s role is to ensure disciplined execution, not to seek perfection. Given the low-cost, high-volume potential and the lack of a clear incumbent response, the risk-reward favors proceeding now.
