The proposal asks the studio to risk its stable, modest income on a single, costly experiment. The objections are concrete: audience tolerance for a long cartoon is unproven, cost overruns are a real threat, and the founder’s own advisors—including family—are opposed. Funding would drain from the reliable short-film pipeline.

However, the core asset is the studio’s proven creative skill in animation. Shorts build technique, humor, and audience goodwill. A feature is a natural escalation of that craft, not a leap into the unknown. The budget, while large, is still a multiple of a known cost base—not an open-ended gamble. The risk is real, but so is the potential to redefine the medium and capture a new market before competitors. The thin profits from shorts mean the studio has little to lose in absolute terms; a failure would hurt, but a success would transform its fortunes. The objections come from caution, not from evidence that the audience rejects long-form animation. No data exists to prove failure. The studio’s survival depends on growth, and this is the only path to it.

The timing is right: the craft is mature, and the market has shown appetite for novel entertainment. Approving now, with discipline on budget, is the bold but rational move. The downside is contained; the upside is historic.

DECISION: GO
