The proposal asks the studio to stake its stable, thin-margin short-film business on a single, untested product at ten times normal cost. The risks are concrete and severe: audience tolerance for a long cartoon is unknown, budget overruns are common in ambitious animation, and the founder’s own family and industry insiders—people with direct knowledge of distribution and audience behavior—are opposed. Funding must come entirely from current short-film profits, meaning any failure would not just lose the investment but also cripple the studio’s ability to keep producing the shorts that keep it alive.

There is no evidence presented that the feature will succeed. No market research, no test screenings, no comparative precedent of another studio’s successful feature-length cartoon, no plan to mitigate overruns, and no alternative financing. The only arguments in favor are creative ambition and the hope that a new format will pay off. But hope is not a strategy, especially when the downside is existential.

The timing is also poor: 1934, during the Great Depression, when discretionary entertainment spending is fragile. A failure would be catastrophic, and even a moderate success might not repay ten times the cost given thin margins. The proposal asks for a leap of faith, but the facts justify caution.

The studio should not risk its core business on an unproven, under-researched gamble. The prudent path is to continue shorts, test a longer format at a modest scale, and revisit a full feature only with evidence and a safer financial structure. As proposed, it is not viable now.

DECISION: NO-GO
