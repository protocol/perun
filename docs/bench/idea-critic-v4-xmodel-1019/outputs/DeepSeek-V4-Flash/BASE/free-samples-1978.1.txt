The proposal hinges on a single, weak signal—stated intent from free-sample recipients—to justify a costly national rollout. The 1978 test in four cities already measured actual purchase behavior, and it was disappointing. Free samples will likely generate polite, non-binding enthusiasm, not reliable demand. Office workers may accept a free notepad but see no reason to buy it, especially when the adhesive is repositionable but not clearly superior to staples or tape. The cost of sampling an entire mid-sized city’s offices is substantial, and the follow-up survey is biased toward gratitude, not true willingness to pay. Even if a majority say “yes,” that does not translate into repeat purchases at scale. The national marketing budget is a massive risk given zero evidence of pull-through. The earlier failure suggests the product itself—not just its name—has a positioning problem. Renaming without re-testing the value proposition or price point is cosmetic. There is no data on repeat purchase, competitive alternatives, or margin thresholds. The proposal skips a low-cost iterative step (e.g., small paid trials in one city with different messaging) and jumps to a high-cost gamble based on a soft metric. The risks raised by the team are valid and unresolved. The plan should not proceed as proposed.  

DECISION: NO-GO
