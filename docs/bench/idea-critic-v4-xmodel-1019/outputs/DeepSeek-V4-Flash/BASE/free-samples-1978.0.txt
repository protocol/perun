The proposal hinges on a single, non-binding survey after a free-sample drop. The first test already failed to generate real purchases. A "would buy" response in a courtesy setting is notoriously unreliable, especially when the product’s core utility was rejected once before. The cost of sampling a mid-sized city’s offices is not trivial, and the subsequent national rollout budget would be massive, based on a hypothetical intent that has zero proven correlation with actual cash transactions. The risk is asymmetric: you spend real money to confirm a likely negative, and if the survey is falsely positive, you commit even more capital to a product that already underperformed in four distinct markets. The office worker pain point was not established by the first test; it was merely assumed. There is no evidence that renaming changes the fundamental value proposition—people did not avoid the product because of its name; they avoided it because they did not need it. A free sample removes the price barrier but does not create a recurring need. The only way to validate demand is to observe repeat purchases at a real price point, not a one-time gift followed by a polite question. The proposal skips that validation step entirely. It substitutes a cheap, soft metric for the hard one that already failed. Approving this now would be gambling shareholder funds on a wish, not a strategy. The correct action is to reject this path until a paid, small-scale trial with a nominal price is run, or the product is repositioned for a different use case entirely. The current evidence is insufficient and the cost of error is too high.  

DECISION: NO-GO
