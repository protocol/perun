The proposal leverages a proven in-house design approach from phones, but laptops have different performance, thermal, and software compatibility demands. The risk that existing laptop software must run on unproven chips is critical—phone and laptop ecosystems are not interchangeable. The supplier’s chips are mature and widely validated; switching to an unproven internal design could degrade performance and driver support, alienating enterprise and power users. The engineering cost and buyer confusion are real, but manageable with marketing and staged rollout. The real issue is the software compatibility risk: if legacy applications fail, the company’s laptop reputation suffers irreparably. The supplier relationship ending is secondary, but it also removes a fallback if the internal chip underperforms. There is no evidence that the phone design approach translates to laptop-class workloads (e.g., sustained multi-core performance, discrete graphics interfaces). The proposal as written does not include a compatibility validation plan, a phased transition, or a contingency to keep the supplier option open. Approving now would force a costly, high-risk leap without proof. The company should first run a pilot on one laptop model, benchmark against the supplier’s chip, and test all top software suites. Only after that evidence could a full move be justified. As proposed, it is premature.  
DECISION: NO-GO
