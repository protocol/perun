The proposal offers a $35 single-board computer to rekindle student programming interest, sold via two distributors. The core risks are real: the buyer must supply peripherals, so the “low-cost” claim is misleading—total cost is higher. A small first batch selling out is not a failure; it signals demand, but it also means limited immediate impact. The charity’s lack of manufacturing and support experience is the sharpest concern—hardware failures, returns, or user confusion could damage both the product’s reputation and the charity’s mission. Cheap laptops and tablets already exist, but they are not optimized for tinkering or learning low-level programming; this niche appeal is genuine, yet unproven at scale. The distribution model is narrow but manageable for a test. The proposal does not specify how the charity will handle support, warranty, or replacement parts, nor how it will measure educational outcomes. Given the charity’s mission, a small, sold-out first batch that creates a waiting list could actually build excitement—provided the product works reliably. But the absence of a support plan and the peripheral cost trap undermine the “low-cost” promise. The proposal as written is too thin on operational details to approve immediately. It needs a clear support strategy, a realistic total-cost-of-ownership statement, and a contingency for batch two. Without those, the risk of a failed launch outweighs the educational benefit.  
DECISION: NO-GO
