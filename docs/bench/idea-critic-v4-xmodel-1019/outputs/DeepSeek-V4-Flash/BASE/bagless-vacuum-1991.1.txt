The proposal centers on a solo founder entering a mature appliance market against entrenched brands. The core innovation—cyclonic separation—is real, but the execution plan is weak. The inventor has prototypes, not a product. He lacks manufacturing experience, retail relationships, and a brand. Pricing above incumbents with no proven demand for bagless technology in 1991 is a high-risk bet. Large players can easily copy the mechanism once it sells, and they have the scale to undercut him. The retail channel is the true barrier: stores already allocate shelf space to trusted names; a new, pricier, unproven machine will struggle to get trial. The inventor’s strength is invention, not operations or sales. The proposal does not address how he will secure production capacity, fund inventory, or persuade retailers to take a risk. It also ignores the possibility of licensing the patent to an incumbent, which would generate revenue without the capital and distribution burden. The risks raised are not hypothetical—they are structural. Without a clear go-to-market strategy, a cost advantage, or a partnership, the company will likely burn cash before gaining traction. The timing is early: consumer awareness of bagless benefits is nil, and the price premium is unjustified. A revised approach—licensing, a strategic partner, or a lower-priced niche entry—would be wiser. As proposed, with no manufacturing record and no distribution plan, this is a recipe for failure.  
DECISION: NO-GO
