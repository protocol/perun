The proposal hinges on a low-cost entry into a market dominated by established players. The licensor’s core business is computers, not consumer gaming, and its market share is small. The royalty per unit is minimal, meaning volume must be enormous to matter. The licensee, a toy and game company, has no proven track record in internet consoles or sustained hardware marketing. The risk that they under-promote is real, as their margins will be thin and their focus likely on toy-store cycles, not long-term platform building.

Consumers will see a toy, not a serious computer or internet device. The licensor’s brand will be diluted, not strengthened, because the product will be perceived as a cheap peripheral. The established console makers have deep pockets, exclusive content, and retail dominance; a low-cost, low-margin entry without a killer application or service will be crushed. The small royalty does not justify the strategic distraction or the potential brand damage. The licensee has no incentive to invest in long-term ecosystem development, and the licensor has no control over quality, support, or updates.

The proposal fails to address how the licensor will gain strategic value beyond a trickle of cash. It is a passive bet on a partner’s execution in a hostile market. Without a clear path to differentiation, network effects, or a share of content revenue, the upside is capped and the downside is a failed product tied to the licensor’s name. The timing (1995) is early for internet consoles, but the competitive landscape is already brutal. Approving this now would waste resources and reputation for a pittance. A revised plan with stronger control, a larger revenue share, or a strategic partnership with content owners might work, but as proposed, it is not viable.

DECISION: NO-GO
