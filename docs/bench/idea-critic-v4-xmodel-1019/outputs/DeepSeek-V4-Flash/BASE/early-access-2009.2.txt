The proposal leverages scarcity and direct feedback, but the 2009 context is critical. At that time, “early access” as a formal model did not exist; buyers expected finished products. Selling an explicitly unfinished alpha risks immediate negative word-of-mouth, which a solo developer cannot counter without marketing. The lack of support infrastructure means even a modest influx of buyers could overwhelm the developer, leading to poor reviews and abandoned updates. The “keep adding features based on buyers” loop is sound in theory, but it assumes buyers will tolerate instability and missing content long enough to provide useful direction—unlikely when they paid full price for a rough state. The risks raised are not theoretical; they are near-certain outcomes given no budget and no network. A better path would be to release a free demo or a heavily discounted beta to a small community, gathering feedback without public judgment, then launch a polished version later. However, the proposal offers no plan for that pivot, no scope limit, and no contingency for failure. The solo developer’s time is the only asset, and spending it on support rather than core gameplay will stall progress. The game is “playable but unfinished”—that is not an alpha to sell; it is a prototype to test privately. The executive team should not approve a move that risks the product’s reputation before it has a chance to mature. The upside is real but speculative; the downside is immediate and irreversible. Therefore, reject as proposed.  
DECISION: NO-GO
