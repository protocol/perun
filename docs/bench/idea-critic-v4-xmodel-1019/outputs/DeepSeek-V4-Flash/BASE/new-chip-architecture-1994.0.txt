The proposal asks for a joint leap into a new 64-bit architecture, betting on compiler-driven parallelism over backward compatibility. The core risks are severe: emulation slowdowns could cripple performance for existing software, the compiler challenge is non-trivial and unproven at scale, and the massive installed base of dominant-architecture software creates a powerful inertia against switching. Meanwhile, rivals extending the dominant architecture to 64 bits would offer a seamless upgrade path, likely capturing the market before this new design matures.

However, the proposal’s strength lies in its timing and partnership. Two major makers jointly developing a clean-slate architecture could set a new standard, especially if the dominant architecture’s 64-bit extension proves clumsy or limited. The compiler-scheduling approach, while hard, could yield long-term efficiency gains that hand-tuned hardware cannot match. Emulation, though slow initially, can improve over time, and the new architecture’s native performance might outstrip the extended legacy design. The installed base is a hurdle, but not insurmountable—many platforms have survived transitions with emulation layers (e.g., early 32-bit systems running 16-bit code). The key question is whether the firms can execute the compiler and emulation well enough to reach critical mass before rivals close the gap.

Given the strategic necessity of moving to 64-bit, the joint effort spreads risk and cost. The risks are real but manageable with aggressive engineering and clear migration incentives. The potential to define the next era of computing outweighs the short-term friction of emulation and compiler complexity. Approving now, with immediate focus on compiler tooling and emulation performance, is the right call.
