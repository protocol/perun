The proposal targets a real opportunity—the living room convergence of media—but the timing and positioning are flawed. In 1991, the market is fragmented: gamers want low-cost consoles, PC users want productivity and expanding multimedia, and casual consumers are confused by new formats. A $1,000 device that tries to be all things risks satisfying none.

The small title library is fatal. Without strong third-party support, the player cannot compete with the deep catalogs of Nintendo or Sega, nor the growing CD-ROM software on PCs. Consumers will not pay a premium for a device whose purpose is unclear and whose content is scarce. The “interactive disc” category itself is unproven—no killer app exists to drive demand.

The price point is the second killer. At $1,000, it is three to four times a console and near a basic PC. The value proposition is weak: a PC already does music, photos, and (soon) multimedia, with a familiar interface and upgrade path. This device offers no unique advantage over a PC and no price advantage over a console. Early adopters might buy, but mainstream households will wait—or buy a PC instead.

The company could succeed later, with cheaper components, a stronger content pipeline, and clearer messaging. But launching now, as proposed, would burn capital and brand credibility. The risks are not manageable in the proposed form; they are structural to the market’s current state.

Therefore, do not approve as proposed. The concept has merit, but the execution is premature and mispriced. A revised strategy—lower cost, focused use-case, or partnership for content—might work, but that is not this proposal.

DECISION: NO-GO
