The proposal hinges on a fundamental market mismatch. In 1993, the console market is defined by a razor-and-blades model: sell hardware at a loss, recoup on software. This start-up proposes the opposite—licensees must profit on hardware, forcing a high retail price (~$700) that is 2-3x competitors. Early adopters alone cannot sustain a platform; mainstream gamers are price-sensitive.  

The royalty on games is a double-edged sword. It creates recurring revenue, but it also raises the effective cost for licensees to compete on software pricing, and it may deter third-party game developers who already face platform fees. Established players (Nintendo, Sega, soon Sony) have deep pockets, exclusive content, and loss-leading hardware. A licensing model with multiple hardware makers risks fragmentation—no single flagship device, inconsistent quality, and diluted brand identity.  

The risk that licensees must profit on hardware is the fatal flaw. In a price war, they will either undercut quality or exit, leaving the start-up with no platform. The royalty per console and game sounds attractive, but volume will be low at $700, and licensees will demand lower royalties to stay viable, squeezing margins.  

The proposal does not address how to build an ecosystem (exclusive games, developer support) or how to convince consumers to pay a premium for an unproven standard. The market already has strong competitors; entering with a higher price and a fragmented hardware strategy is a recipe for niche failure.  

Therefore, the proposal as written cannot succeed. It needs a revised model—likely selling hardware at cost or loss, building a single flagship device, or targeting a different market segment—before approval.  

DECISION: NO-GO
