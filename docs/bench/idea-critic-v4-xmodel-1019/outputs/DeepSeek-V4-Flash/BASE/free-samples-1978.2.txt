The proposal hinges on a single, flawed assumption: that stated purchase intent from a free-sample recipient predicts actual paid behavior. The 1978 test in four cities already provided real market data—disappointing sales—which is far more reliable than hypothetical "would you buy" answers. Free samples often create a sense of obligation or momentary delight, not sustained demand, especially for a product that office workers already rejected in a live market. The cost of samples and the large marketing budget for a national rollout are sunk risks with no evidence of a fix. The problem is not the name; it is the product's perceived value. Renaming without repositioning the adhesive's utility (e.g., for temporary labeling, repositionable signs, or craft uses) repeats the same mistake at a larger scale. A mid-sized city test also lacks the demographic diversity of a national market, so even a positive result there would be misleading. The executive team should demand a revised plan that addresses the core issue: why did the four-city test fail? Was it price, distribution, messaging, or genuine lack of need? Without that diagnosis, any sampling campaign is guesswork. The proposal as written spends money to confirm a bias, not to learn. It should be returned for a deeper, lower-cost investigation—such as post-purchase surveys or a controlled trial with a different use case—before any national commitment. Approving now would waste resources on a known weak signal.  
DECISION: NO-GO
