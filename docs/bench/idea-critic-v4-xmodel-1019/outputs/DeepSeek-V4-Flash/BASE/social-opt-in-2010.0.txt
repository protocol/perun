The proposal leverages an existing user base for rapid adoption, but its core mechanism—automatic enrollment with default visibility—creates irreversible exposure. The stated risks are not hypothetical; they are likely outcomes. Privacy backlash from users who did not explicitly choose participation would be immediate and vocal, damaging trust in the core mail product. Regulator attention, while uncertain, is plausible given the era’s growing scrutiny of data practices, and a negative finding could impose fines or forced changes post-launch.  

The product’s value proposition is also weak: an inbox is a task-focused environment, not a social discovery space. Even if users tolerate the feature, low ongoing engagement would make the risk unjustified. Reversing exposure after connections are formed is technically messy and reputationally costly—once visible, the damage to user perception is done.  

There is no evidence in the proposal of opt-in friction, granular privacy controls, or a clear engagement hook. The upside (potential network effects) does not outweigh the near-certain short-term backlash and possible long-term regulatory and trust erosion. A slower, opt-in rollout with visible privacy choices could test value without forcing exposure, but that is not what is proposed.  

The executive team should not approve a launch that trades user consent for adoption speed, especially when the feature’s stickiness is unproven. The cost of a mistake here is not just feature failure—it is core product harm.  

DECISION: NO-GO
