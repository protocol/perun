The proposal hinges on a 2012 crowdfunding campaign as both funding source and market validation. The team is small, has no marketing budget, and faces entrenched competitors. The core risk is not hardware—delays are common but manageable—but the chicken-and-egg problem: no players without games, no games without a developer base. Crowdfunding can pre-sell to enthusiasts, but it does not solve the long-term content gap. The open platform is a differentiator, but it also fragments the already small addressable market of non-casual gamers who are not already served by phones or established consoles. A one-year ship window is optimistic for a new hardware ecosystem, and any slip will erode backer trust and public momentum. Without a paid marketing push, visibility will decay quickly after the campaign spike. The team’s lack of experience in retail distribution and developer relations is not mentioned as a strength. The proposal is essentially a high-risk, low-margin bet on a community that may not scale beyond early adopters. The facts provided offer no evidence of a unique technical advantage, a compelling exclusive title, or a clear path to profitability after the initial backer wave. The window for a new low-cost console in 2012 is narrow—smartphones and tablets are already absorbing casual play, and established players are dropping prices. The team would be better served by first building a small software demo, securing two or three committed indie developers, and testing a pre-order page before committing to a full hardware build. Approving now would likely burn the team’s credibility and backer money.  
DECISION: NO-GO
