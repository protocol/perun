The proposal asks a studio to stake its thin, steady profits on a single, tenfold-cost gamble. The risks are real: audience endurance for a long cartoon is untested, budget overruns are plausible, and both family and industry insiders oppose it. Funding must come from existing short-film earnings, meaning failure would threaten the studio’s core business.

However, the very factors that make it risky also make it potentially transformative. A full-length animated feature is not merely a longer short; it is a new category with no direct competitor. If successful, it would redefine the studio’s value, moving it from marginal profitability to market leadership. The insiders’ opposition is based on precedent, not on the specific creative or technical execution proposed. Short films have already proven that audiences accept animation as a medium; the leap to feature length is a question of pacing and story, which the studio controls.

The budget overrun risk is mitigated by the studio’s existing production discipline, evidenced by steady, if thin, profits. The funding constraint forces fiscal conservatism, not recklessness. The downside is losing the studio’s current modest position; the upside is capturing an entirely new market. Given that no competitor has attempted this, the first-mover advantage is enormous. Waiting for proof from others would cede that advantage.

The proposal is bold, but the facts do not show a fatal flaw—only fear of the unknown. The studio’s survival depends on more than maintaining thin margins; it depends on creating something no one else can copy overnight. The talent and system are already in place. The risk is calculated, the opportunity is unique, and the cost of inaction is stagnation.

DECISION: GO
