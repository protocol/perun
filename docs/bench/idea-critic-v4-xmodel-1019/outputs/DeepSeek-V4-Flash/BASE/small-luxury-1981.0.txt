The proposal asks the luxury marque to repackage a mass-market compact platform as a premium-badged sedan in 1981, targeting American-brand upgraders. The core risk is authenticity: the shared bones are a visible cost-cutting tell, and the premium price invites direct comparison with established compact sedans from rivals, where the car’s mechanical ordinariness will undermine the luxury story. The internal hesitation—salespeople refusing to use the main brand name—signals a lack of conviction that will leak to customers. The upside is access to a new, price-sensitive buyer pool, but that pool is precisely the one most likely to scrutinize value, not prestige. Prestige dilution is not hypothetical: if the car is seen as a dressed-up economy car, the halo over the entire lineup dims. The timing (1981, with recessionary pressure) favors volume, but the brand’s long-term equity is worth more than a short-term unit gain from a compromised product. The parent’s platform sharing works for volume brands, not for a luxury name where provenance is the product. The proposal’s own risk list is a confession: it cannot hold the premium line, cannot get internal buy-in, and cannot protect the brand. Approving now would force a launch with known fatal flaws, inviting a reputation hit that outlasts any sales bump. The better path is to reject this specific vehicle and wait for a purpose-built compact luxury offering, even if that means losing the immediate American-brand trade-in wave. The market will still be there for a genuine compact luxury car; it will not forgive a cynical badge job.  

DECISION: NO-GO
