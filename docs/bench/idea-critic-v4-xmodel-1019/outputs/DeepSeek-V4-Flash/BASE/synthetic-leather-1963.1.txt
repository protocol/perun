The proposal asks for a heavy marketing spend in 1963 on a premium synthetic shoe-upper material that costs more than real leather, targeting manufacturers who must retool. The stated risks are severe: factory resistance, higher cost, comfort concerns, and fashion volatility. No evidence is given that any manufacturer has tested or committed to the material, nor that consumer demand exists at a premium price. The comfort risk is particularly damaging—if early wearers find it less breathable or stiff, the product’s reputation dies before scale. The fashion risk means even a successful launch could be obsolete within two seasons. The cost premium over leather, which is already a natural, established material, removes the main economic incentive for manufacturers to switch. Without pilot programs, cost-reduction pathways, or a clear plan to mitigate comfort and factory retooling, the proposal relies entirely on aggressive marketing to overcome structural industry resistance. Marketing cannot fix a product that is more expensive, less comfortable, and dependent on fleeting trends. The executive team has no data on production yields, break-even volumes, or competitor responses. Approving now would commit significant capital to a high-risk, unvalidated launch. A better path would be limited trials with select manufacturers to gather comfort and production data, then decide on a broader rollout. That is not the proposal as written. Therefore, the proposal should not be approved as proposed now.

DECISION: NO-GO
