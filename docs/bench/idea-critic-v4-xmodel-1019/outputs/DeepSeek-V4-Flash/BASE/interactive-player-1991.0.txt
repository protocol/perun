The proposal targets a real consumer desire—centralizing media in the living room—but the timing and positioning are fatally flawed. In 1991, a $1,000 device sits in a dead zone: too expensive for impulse game buyers, yet underpowered and under-supported compared to a PC that can do spreadsheets, word processing, and games. The small title library is not a temporary gap; it’s a structural weakness because third-party developers will prioritize larger install bases on existing consoles or emerging PC CD-ROMs. Consumer confusion is not a marketing fix—it’s a symptom of no clear job-to-be-done. Is it a game machine? A photo viewer? A music player? Trying to be all three means it excels at none, especially at that price. The risks are not mitigable with better ads or a few launch titles; they are inherent to the product’s category. The PC is already moving into the living room with multimedia upgrades, and game consoles are dropping below $200 with growing libraries. This device would require massive content investment and price cuts before launch, which the proposal does not include. The executive team should not commit capital to a hardware bet that depends on an ecosystem they cannot control or build quickly. The market will not wait, and the device will be obsolete before it reaches scale. A revised version with a lower price, a clear single use-case, or a partnership with existing content providers might work later, but as proposed now, it’s a money pit.  
DECISION: NO-GO
