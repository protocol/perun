The proposal targets a mass-market fashion niche, not the maker’s core precision segment. Plastic positions it as disposable, which conflicts with a reputation built on craftsmanship—but that reputation can be leveraged as ironic, high-design credibility if the branding is overtly playful. The twelve-model collection spreads risk across styles, and a one-million-unit first-year target is plausible for a low-price, high-visibility fashion item, especially in 1983 when quartz disruption has made mechanical prestige less relevant. The stated risks are real but manageable: quality concerns are mitigated by framing the watch as a temporary accessory, not a heirloom; fashion volatility is offset by rapid production cycles and low per-unit cost; price cuts from established brands would validate the category and drive trial. The aggressive target forces retail ubiquity, which is necessary for fashion momentum. The key uncertainty is whether the executive team can execute a fast-moving consumer-goods launch rather than a traditional watch launch—but the proposal explicitly asks for that shift, not a half-measure. Without internal data on margins, distribution, or marketing budget, the only rational go/no-go is based on strategic fit: the plastic watch is a deliberate, bounded experiment that protects the core line while capturing a new, younger demographic. A "revise" would waste the timing window—plastic watches are already rising in street fashion, and delay invites copycats. The downside is a write-off of development cost and minor brand dilution, which can be quarantined with a sub-brand. The upside is a new revenue stream and cultural relevance.
