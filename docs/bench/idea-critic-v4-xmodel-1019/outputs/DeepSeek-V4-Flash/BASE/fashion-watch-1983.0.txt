The proposal leverages a genuine manufacturing breakthrough—cheap plastic—but pivots it into a high-risk fashion gamble. The 1983 context is crucial: quartz had already commoditized timekeeping, margins were collapsing, and brand equity was the only durable asset. A plastic watch, even if cleverly styled, directly contradicts the maker’s core reputation for precision and durability. That reputational damage is not a minor risk; it is existential. Once consumers and distributors associate the brand with disposable trinkets, recovering premium positioning is nearly impossible, regardless of fashion success.

The sales target of one million units in year one is not merely aggressive—it is reckless for a first fashion collection. Fashion cycles are notoriously short, and a single misread season leaves massive inventory write-offs. Established brands with deep pockets and established retail relationships can and will cut prices on their own quartz lines, crushing a newcomer’s volume before scale is achieved. The proposal offers no hedging strategy, no exit clause, no secondary market plan for unsold stock, and no protection for the core watch line. It treats fashion as a stable demand curve, which it is not.

The only defensible path would be a pilot: a limited run, tested in select markets, with strict brand separation (e.g., a sub-brand) and a clear kill switch. But the proposal demands immediate full-scale launch with no such safeguards. The upside is a short-lived fad profit; the downside is permanent brand devaluation and a warehouse of unsold plastic. The executive team should not approve this as proposed.

DECISION: NO-GO
