The proposal leverages a proven in-house design approach from phones, but laptops differ in performance, thermal, and software compatibility demands. The key risk is software continuity: existing laptop apps and operating systems are built for the supplier’s architecture. Re-engineering that stack is nontrivial and could delay launches or break legacy workflows. The in-house chips are unproven against the supplier’s mature, high-performance line, especially for power users and enterprise buyers. Engineering time spent on this transition will divert resources from other product lines, and buyer confusion—especially among professionals who rely on specific software—could erode market share. Ending a long-standing supplier relationship also adds supply-chain friction with no guarantee of better cost or performance. While vertical integration worked for phones, laptops have a different ecosystem and higher performance bar. The proposal does not provide evidence that the in-house design can match the supplier’s benchmarks or that software compatibility can be achieved without major investment. Given these unresolved technical and market risks, approving now would be premature. The company should first validate chip performance against the supplier’s baseline, map software dependencies, and run a pilot with trusted enterprise customers. Only after those steps can a full transition be responsibly approved.

DECISION: NO-GO
