The proposal leverages a real market shift: American-brand buyers want a smaller, premium-feeling sedan. The shared platform cuts development cost and time, allowing a quick entry. The added trim and features can create perceived value, and the parent company’s engineering is proven. The risks are real but manageable: buyers rarely inspect platforms—they assess badge, interior, and ride quality. Rivals’ smaller sedans may be cheaper, but this car offers a distinct luxury identity. Salespeople calling it by a different name is a branding choice, not a flaw—it creates a sub-brand, protecting the main marque from dilution while capturing new customers. Prestige suffers only if the product is poor; with correct pricing and positioning, it can enhance the parent’s reach without cannibalizing top-tier models. The timing (1981) suits an economic shift toward efficiency, and the luxury segment lacks a compact option from this maker. Execution risks exist, but they are mitigated by strong marketing and dealer training. The upside—new segment share, higher margins than mass-market compacts, and a foothold with younger, affluent buyers—outweighs the downside. No evidence suggests the core brand will be damaged if the car is clearly its own model with distinct styling and features. Approve now; delay only cedes ground to competitors.  
DECISION: GO
