The proposal bets on a royalty model where hardware makers profit per unit, unlike incumbents who subsidize hardware. At $700, early adopters are the only viable segment, but high-end graphics could justify the premium if the experience is clearly superior. The risk is structural: if licensees must earn on hardware, they cannot price aggressively, ceding the mass market to loss-leading rivals. The game royalty stream is attractive, but it depends on console install base—which will be small if price stays high. Existing competitors have established developer relationships and brand trust; a new entrant must offer something transformative, not just better specs. The multi-licensee approach could fragment quality control and user experience, undermining the “standard” goal. However, if the design is truly next-generation and the licensees are strong, early adopters may seed a niche that grows as component costs fall. The lack of first-party software commitment is a gap—no killer app is mentioned. Timing is 1993, where 3D is emerging but not yet dominant; this could be a leap or a misfire. The profit-on-hardware constraint is not fatal if the royalty per console is low and the game royalty is high, but it requires volume that a $700 price won’t deliver soon. The executive team should weigh whether the start-up can survive a slow burn before prices drop. The proposal does not address manufacturing scale, marketing budget, or developer incentives. Without a clear path to undercut incumbents on total cost of ownership, the risk of stagnation is high. The standard-setting ambition is bold but the execution hinges on external partners who may not align on long-term vision.  
DECISION: NO-GO
