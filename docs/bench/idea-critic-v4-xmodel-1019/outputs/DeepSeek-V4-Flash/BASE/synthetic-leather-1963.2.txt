The proposal targets a premium niche, but the core economics are inverted. In 1963, leather is the established standard—durable, breathable, and familiar. A synthetic costing more than leather offers no immediate cost advantage to manufacturers, who must also retool processes and retrain workers. The comfort risk is real: leather molds to the foot; a stiff synthetic may not, leading to returns and brand damage. Fashion is cyclical, and a premium push with heavy marketing locks in high fixed costs before consumer acceptance is proven. The company’s strength is chemistry, not consumer branding or footwear manufacturing. Success depends on converting skeptical factory owners and fickle end-users simultaneously—a double hurdle with no clear pull from either. A better path would be a lower-cost entry for work boots or industrial uses, where durability matters more than feel, and factories already handle synthetics. That would build volume, prove performance, and lower costs before attempting a premium fashion play. As proposed, the risk-reward is poor: high capital, high marketing spend, and a product that is neither cheaper nor demonstrably better in the eyes of the buyer. The shoe makers will not switch without a compelling reason, and a premium price is not it. The timeline is wrong, the target is wrong, and the cost structure is wrong. Approving this now would likely burn cash and damage credibility with the very manufacturers needed for future launches.  
DECISION: NO-GO
