The proposal asks to launch a premium-priced, bagless vacuum company in 1991 against established giants with deep retail reach. The core risk is not the technology—thousands of prototypes suggest functional proof—but the business model. A solo founder with no manufacturing record faces steep unit costs, quality control issues, and cash-flow strain before a single sale. Incumbents can quickly copy the cyclone concept once it proves successful, leveraging their existing factories and shelf space to undercut on price and distribution. Consumers in 1991 have no reason to pay a premium for a novelty when conventional vacuums work reliably; the "bagless" benefit is not yet a recognized pain point, and education costs would be borne entirely by the new firm. The pricing above established brands directly contradicts the typical early-adopter strategy of entering lower or at parity to gain trial. Without a clear moat—patents, exclusive supply deals, or a distribution partnership—the copy risk is fatal. The founder’s strength is invention, not scale operations. A better path would be licensing the design to an existing manufacturer or partnering with a retail chain, but that is not what is proposed. As written, the plan overestimates consumer willingness to pay for a functional improvement and underestimates the capital and time needed to build brand trust and retail presence against entrenched competitors. The timing is poor: early 1990s recessionary pressure favors value over innovation. The execution risk, competitive response, and pricing strategy combine to make failure more likely than success. No evidence is given of pre-orders, cost breakdowns, or distribution commitments. Therefore, the proposal should not proceed as presented.

DECISION: NO-GO
