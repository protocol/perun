The proposal leverages a proven social mechanic—link-cable trading—to turn a hardware limitation into a driver of repeat engagement and word-of-mouth. The two-version structure creates artificial scarcity of content, but in a way that rewards cooperation rather than punishing solo players, since each version is fully playable alone. The declining handheld is a real concern, but the target audience is children, who are less device-cyclical and more peer-driven; the need for a second player and cable actually encourages playground buzz, not friction. The “price grab” risk is mitigated if both versions are priced identically and marketed as a paired experience, not a premium split. The publisher’s doubt is understandable, but the proposal’s core value is not unit sales per version—it’s the ecosystem effect: players who buy one version become evangelists for the other, doubling potential attachment per friend group. The aging console means lower development costs and less competition for shelf space, and the novelty of “version-exclusive creatures” has never been tried on this platform, creating a unique hook. The downside is limited to initial production run risk; if the first wave sells poorly, the publisher can halt the second version without sunk cost. The social loop—trade to complete—turns a single-player purchase into a community event, extending the game’s life past the console’s curve. The proposal is bold but internally consistent, with low marginal cost for the second version (asset reuse) and high potential for cult status. The risk of looking greedy is outweighed by the clarity of the value proposition: two games, one shared goal, no pay-to-win.  

DECISION: GO
