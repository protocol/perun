The core tension is monetization versus user trust. The notification step and opt-out reduce surprise, but they do not eliminate the core risk: making private consumption data visible, even with consent, changes the social contract. Users may not fully grasp the breadth of tracking across partner sites, and the "option to decline" is passive, not affirmative consent.

The upside is significant ad revenue and engagement, but the downside is asymmetric. A public backlash could erode the network’s base, and the feature’s stickiness (once friends see it, they expect it) makes withdrawal a PR disaster. Partner embarrassment is real—a user liking a discount on a dating site or a medical product could go viral in a negative way. The 2007 context matters: privacy norms were less mature, but the damage to trust would be lasting.

The proposal has merit, but the execution is flawed. It requires a more granular control system, a clear public education campaign, and a hard limit on data categories (excluding health, finance, and intimate goods). The current version, with a single notification and decline, is too blunt. It will likely pass initial tests but fail at scale, triggering regulatory and user backlash that outweighs the ad lift. The cost of a mistake here is not just lost revenue—it is an existential blow to user confidence.

The network can still pursue ad growth, but not this way. It needs a redesigned consent flow, category exclusions, and a kill-switch plan before launch. As proposed, the risk is not manageable.

DECISION: NO-GO
