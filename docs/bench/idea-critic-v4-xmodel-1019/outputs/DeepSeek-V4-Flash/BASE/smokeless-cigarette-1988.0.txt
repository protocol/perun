The proposal asks to launch a heat-not-burn cigarette in 1988, positioning it as cleaner. The core issue is that the product’s only real advantage is reduced smoke, but it still delivers nicotine and carcinogens. Test markets may show taste or usability problems, but even if they succeed, the regulatory risk is severe: health claims will invite scrutiny, and any “cleaner” messaging invites lawsuits. The sunk cost is large, but that is irrelevant to future decisions. The product is more complex for smokers, with a lit carbon tip that changes the ritual—likely alienating core users. The company’s entire business model depends on nicotine addiction, not on reducing harm; promoting a “cleaner” cigarette undermines that narrative without eliminating harm. If regulators ban or restrict it, the investment is wasted. If smokers reject it, the investment is wasted. The only scenario where this works is if it quietly replaces conventional cigarettes—but the proposal demands aggressive promotion, which guarantees backlash. There is no evidence that the taste, ignition, or user experience has been fixed. The risk of reputational damage to the parent company from “clean” claims far outweighs any potential market gain. A better path would be to continue R&D on true harm reduction or to reposition as a niche product without health claims, but that is not what is proposed. The timing (1988) also predates modern regulatory frameworks, meaning future rules could retroactively punish the company. Therefore, the proposal as written—broad promotion with clean claims—is not viable. It should not be approved now.  

DECISION: NO-GO
