The proposal bets the company’s entire future on a single product and technology transition. The core strengths are real: a clean-sheet electric sedan avoids legacy costs, and buying a large factory gives immediate scale—if the capital appears. But the financial gap is not hypothetical. The factory purchase, retooling, and battery supply chain require billions, while the company’s existing capital is a fraction. No revenue stream exists yet to bridge that gap, and debt markets in 2008 are contracting.  

Charging infrastructure is a genuine adoption barrier: early buyers will face range anxiety, and without a network, demand is uncertain. Established carmakers have cash, dealer networks, and the ability to launch competing EVs or hybrids—they can also undercut pricing. The company’s lack of mass-production experience increases the risk of costly quality issues or delays, which would be fatal given the thin margins.  

The proposal is visionary but not executable as stated. It requires a phased approach: start with a premium, lower-volume model to prove the technology and build brand credibility, secure strategic partnerships for batteries and charging, and only then commit to a giant factory and full conversion. Buying a large factory now, while abandoning all combustion revenue, leaves zero buffer. The timing (2008) amplifies every risk—credit, consumer confidence, and oil price volatility.  

The team should not approve the full commitment today. The idea is sound, but the sequencing and capital plan are not. A smaller pilot, external investment, or joint venture could de-risk. As proposed, the probability of bankruptcy is too high.  

DECISION: NO-GO
