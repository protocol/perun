The proposal hinges on a single, high-risk bet: a year-long delivery timeline for new hardware, funded by a crowdfunding campaign, with no marketing budget. The team’s core assumptions are unproven. The competitive landscape is brutal—established consoles and mobile games dominate both developer attention and consumer wallets. A new platform requires a critical mass of exclusive, compelling games to justify purchase; without a developer outreach budget or a proven track record, securing those titles is speculative.

The absence of a marketing budget is fatal for a crowdfunded launch. Campaigns succeed on visibility, community building, and pre-launch hype—all of which require either paid promotion or an existing audience. The team has neither. The risk of hardware delays is not just probable, it’s near-certain for a small, unproven team; a missed ship date would erode backer trust, trigger refund demands, and poison the public launch.

The proposal offers no mitigation for any of these three critical points. It does not state how it will secure exclusive games, how it will generate demand without spending, or how it will buffer the timeline. The open software platform is a nice technical idea, but it does not solve the business problem of adoption. The year timeline is a fantasy, not a plan.

While the passion is admirable, this is a classic “build it and they will come” fallacy. The backers are not a market; they are a test group. The proposal should be sent back for a realistic timeline, a concrete developer acquisition plan, and a minimal marketing strategy—or it should be shelved entirely.

DECISION: NO-GO
