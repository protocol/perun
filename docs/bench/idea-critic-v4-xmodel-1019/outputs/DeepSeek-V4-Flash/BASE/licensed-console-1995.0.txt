The proposal leverages an underutilized asset—the computer platform—with minimal upfront cost to the licensor. The toy company absorbs manufacturing, distribution, and marketing expenses, while the licensor gains a royalty stream with zero downside risk beyond opportunity cost. The console market is dominated by established players, but this is a low-cost, internet-focused device, targeting a niche that incumbents may overlook in 1995. The small royalty per unit is acceptable if volume is high; the toy company’s existing retail channels and family-friendly brand could drive significant sales, especially during holiday seasons. The risk of weak marketing is real, but the licensee’s core business is consumer toys—they have strong incentive to sell their own product. The brand disconnect is a minor issue; the licensor’s name appears on the device, and positive association grows over time. The alternative—building and marketing the console internally—would require massive capital, distribution expertise, and marketing spend the licensor lacks. Thus, this is a low-cost, high-optionality bet. If it fails, the licensor loses little. If it succeeds, it gains market presence, royalties, and a foothold in a new category. The proposal should proceed as-is, without revisions, because delay would cede the emerging internet-console window to competitors. The licensee’s expertise and the licensor’s technology create a complementary fit.  
DECISION: GO
