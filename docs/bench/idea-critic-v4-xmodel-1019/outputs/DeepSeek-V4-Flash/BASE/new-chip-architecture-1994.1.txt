The proposal asks for a bold, risky bet in 1994: abandon compatibility for a compiler-driven parallel 64-bit design. The core value is long-term performance leap, but the immediate costs are severe. Emulation will likely cripple legacy software, alienating the massive installed base. Compiler difficulty means early developer adoption stalls, while rivals can extend the existing dominant architecture to 64-bit with far less friction. The market rewards incremental, compatible improvements over revolutionary but fragmented shifts. The joint effort may produce a technically elegant design, but commercial success depends on ecosystem migration—which the proposal does not solve. There is no clear path to overcome the network effects of the incumbent software library. The risk of a slow, emulated start and a compiler bottleneck outweighs the potential architectural advantage, especially since competitors can capture the 64-bit transition with a simpler extension. Without a mitigation plan for performance, compiler tooling, or a migration strategy, this is premature. The proposal should not proceed as written; it needs a staged rollout, a compatibility bridge, or a fallback.  

DECISION: NO-GO
