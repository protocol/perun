The proposal leverages an existing internal design capability proven in mobile devices, but applies it to a different product category with distinct performance, thermal, and compatibility demands. The key risk is software continuity: laptop users rely on a vast ecosystem of legacy applications, and the new processor architecture may require emulation or recompilation, introducing instability and performance penalties. The supplier’s chips are mature, widely benchmarked, and trusted by enterprise and prosumer segments; the company’s own chip is unproven in this context, potentially leading to negative reviews and returns.

Engineering time spent on the transition will divert resources from other roadmap items, and buyer confusion—especially among non-technical users who see performance differences or compatibility warnings—could hurt brand reputation. Ending the supplier relationship also removes a fallback option if the internal chip underperforms or faces yield issues. The proposal does not specify a mitigation plan for software compatibility, a phased rollout, or a performance validation target against the supplier’s baseline. It also lacks a timeline for when the new chip would match or exceed supplier capabilities.

Given that the laptop line is a mature revenue stream, the risk of disrupting it without clear technical validation or a pilot program is high. The upside—vertical integration and cost savings—is plausible, but the proposal as written does not address the critical unknowns. It should be revised to include a compatibility testing plan, a comparison benchmark, and a contingency if the chip fails to meet thresholds. As presented, approving immediately would expose the company to significant operational and reputational harm.

DECISION: NO-GO
