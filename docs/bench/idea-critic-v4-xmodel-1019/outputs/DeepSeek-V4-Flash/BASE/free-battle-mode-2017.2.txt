The proposal leverages existing assets and a proven cooperative foundation, but the core assumption is flawed. The rival’s success is not in the genre alone, but in a specific, polished last-player-standing experience with established player retention and monetization loops. Reusing assets from a cooperative game will likely produce a mode that feels derivative and mechanically mismatched, failing to compete on quality or novelty.

The two-month timeline is unrealistic for balancing, netcode, and matchmaking—critical for this genre. A rushed launch risks technical instability and negative reviews, which would damage the studio’s reputation and the paid game’s goodwill. Cannibalization is a real threat: existing players may shift to the free title, reducing paid revenue without guaranteeing new paying users. The studio’s lack of free-to-play experience means monetization, live ops, and anti-cheat systems will be untested, leading to poor retention and low conversion on cosmetics.

The rival’s dominance is not just market share—it’s brand lock-in. Entering with an inferior, rushed product will likely result in low adoption and wasted engineering effort. The opportunity cost is high: the same two months could improve the paid game’s cooperative content, which is already a proven revenue stream. There is no evidence of player demand for a free battle royale from this studio, nor a plan for sustained content updates.

The proposal is a reactive gamble, not a strategic bet. It spreads the team thin, risks the core product, and enters a saturated market without a competitive edge. Approval now would likely fail technically and commercially. A revised approach—with a longer timeline, dedicated free-to-play design, and a pilot monetization test—might work later, but not as proposed.

DECISION: NO-GO
