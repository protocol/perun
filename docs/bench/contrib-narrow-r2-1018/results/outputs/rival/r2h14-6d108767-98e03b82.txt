Do not push the code or open the Pull Request yet. Submitting code without formal internal sign-off violates your employment agreement and exposes you to personal liability, regardless of how "tested" or "urgent" the fix feels. Retroactive permission is rarely granted for code already public; once pushed, the intellectual property claim shifts, creating a legal mess that is far harder to clean up than a delayed submission.

Instead, execute this immediate containment and escalation plan:

1.  **Pause the External Commitment:** Reply to the project maintainers immediately. State clearly: "The contribution is technically ready and tested, but an unexpected internal compliance review has been triggered that requires final authorization before public release. I anticipate a short delay and will provide a firm timeline within 48 hours." This maintains goodwill by showing progress while setting a boundary around the bureaucracy. Do not mention the company name or the specific nature of the block.

2.  **Formalize the Internal Block:** Send a written request (email is best for audit trails) to your team lead and copy the legal/HR contact listed in your handbook. Explicitly state: "I have a ready contribution for [Project Name] that resolves a critical issue discussed publicly. Per the employee handbook, I require the standard open-source contribution approval. Since no process documentation exists, please designate the authorized approver and provide the necessary workflow steps by [Date 2 days out]. Until then, I am holding the submission to ensure compliance." This forces the organization to either produce the process or formally instruct you to proceed/stop, shifting the liability from you to them.

3.  **Prepare the "Clean Room" Fallback:** If the internal process stalls indefinitely (e.g., no response after one week), you must decouple the fix from your employment to help the community. Do not use company time, equipment, or networks for the next steps. Rewrite the fix from memory on your personal hardware, ensuring no proprietary code, architecture specifics, or company-derived logic is included. Since you cannot disclose the proprietary service architecture anyway, the fix should ideally be generic enough that a clean-room re-implementation is feasible. Submit this personal version as a new contributor, explicitly stating in the PR description that this is an independent contribution not affiliated with your employer.

4.  **Document Everything:** Keep a private log of all communications with your lead, legal, and the maintainers. If the company later claims you violated policy, you need proof that you attempted to follow the rules and were blocked by their own lack of process.

Your priority is protecting your legal standing; the open-source community will understand a delay caused by corporate compliance far better than they will understand a PR that gets yanked or disputed due to IP ownership issues.

*Note: This advice assumes standard corporate IP policies; consult a qualified attorney if your employment contract contains unusual non-compete or IP assignment clauses.*
*Do not attempt to "sneak" the code out; the risk to your career outweighs the benefit of a single merged PR.*
