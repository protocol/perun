Do not mention the paid penetration test, SecureBank, or the commercial nature of the engagement in your initial contact.

Your primary goal is rapid remediation to protect users. Introducing details about a paid contract, a specific client, or a commercial audit triggers immediate legal and liability reviews for open-source maintainers. This often stalls the technical fix while their legal counsel determines if accepting the patch creates an implicit warranty, exposes them to litigation, or violates their own contribution policies regarding third-party audits. Since the project's security policy does not explicitly request this information, providing it is unnecessary and counterproductive.

Instead, structure your initial email strictly around the technical severity and the solution:

1.  **Subject Line:** Use a clear, urgent subject such as "Security Vulnerability Report: Signature Verification Bypass via Race Condition."
2.  **The Vulnerability:** Describe the technical mechanism of the race condition and how it allows signature bypassing. Include a minimal, sanitized proof-of-concept (PoC) that demonstrates the flaw without revealing SecureBank's infrastructure or sensitive data.
3.  **The Patch:** Attach your proposed fix as a diff or patch file. Explicitly state that you have developed a patch that resolves the issue.
4.  **Anonymity:** Identify yourself only as an independent security researcher. You are under no ethical or standard legal obligation to disclose your employer or client in a responsible disclosure process unless you are claiming a bounty that requires such verification (which is secondary to fixing the bug).
5.  **NDA Compliance:** By omitting the client's name and the commercial context, you fully comply with your non-disclosure agreement while still fulfilling your ethical duty to report the vulnerability.

Maintainers prioritize based on exploitability and impact, not the source of the finding. A clear technical explanation with a working patch is the most effective way to ensure a quick merge. If the maintainers later ask specifically about the context of the discovery during the coordination process, you can then consult your legal team on whether a vague confirmation (e.g., "found during routine external testing") is permissible, but do not volunteer this information upfront.

Focus entirely on the code, the risk, and the fix. This approach maximizes the speed of adoption and minimizes legal friction.
