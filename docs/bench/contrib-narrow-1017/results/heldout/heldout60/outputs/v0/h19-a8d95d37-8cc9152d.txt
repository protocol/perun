### Guide to Following Up on Your First Pull Request

First, take a deep breath: **you likely did nothing wrong.** A ten-day silence on a small documentation fix is extremely common in open source, even in welcoming projects. Maintainers are often volunteers juggling day jobs, and small typo fixes frequently fall to the bottom of the priority pile behind critical bugs or security patches. The fact that the automated build passed is a great sign—it means your contribution is technically valid and ready to merge; it just needs a human click.

#### Is it okay to follow up?
**Yes.** It is perfectly acceptable etiquette to send a polite nudge after 7–10 days of inactivity. Maintainers generally appreciate reminders because their inboxes can be overwhelming, and a gentle ping helps surface buried contributions. The key is to be **helpful, not demanding**.

#### How to phrase your follow-up
Comment directly on your existing Pull Request (PR) thread. This keeps the context clear for everyone. Here is a template you can adapt:

> "Hi @maintainer-handle (or just 'Hi maintainers'),
>
> I hope you're having a good week! I wanted to gently bump this PR in case it got buried in the noise. I noticed the automated checks are passing, so hopefully, it's ready to go.
>
> Please let me know if there are any changes you'd like me to make or if you need any additional information from my side. Thanks so much for your time and for maintaining this library!"

**Why this works:**
*   **Assumes positive intent:** It assumes they are busy, not ignoring you.
*   **Highlights readiness:** It mentions the build passes, saving them a check.
*   **Offers help:** It opens the door for feedback rather than demanding a merge.
*   **Low pressure:** Phrases like "gently bump" and "if you have time" reduce friction.

#### Other steps to engage with the community
If you want to increase your visibility and chances of acceptance beyond just this one PR:

1.  **Check for "Good First Issue" labels:** Browse the project's issue tracker for tickets labeled `good first issue` or `help wanted`. Commenting on an open issue to ask, "Is this still available? I'd love to take a stab at it," shows proactive engagement.
2.  **Join their communication channel:** Many projects have a Discord, Slack, or GitHub Discussions forum. Introduce yourself there ("Hi, I'm a first-time contributor working on my first PR for AccessAid..."). Sometimes a quick chat in a general channel is more effective than a formal comment.
3.  **Review other PRs:** If you feel confident, look at other open PRs. Even leaving a supportive comment like "This looks great, thanks for fixing this!" builds community goodwill.
4.  **Be patient with the process:** If they still don't respond after your nudge, wait another week before trying a different channel (like a project discussion board). Avoid opening duplicate PRs or tagging multiple people aggressively.

#### What if it still doesn't get merged?
Sometimes, despite doing everything right, a PR stalls indefinitely. This is rarely a reflection of your work's quality. If weeks pass with no response after a polite follow-up, you have still succeeded: you practiced the contribution workflow, identified a real issue, and offered a solution. That experience is valuable regardless of the merge status. You can always mention the attempt on your resume or portfolio as "Proposed documentation improvement for AccessAid."

You are taking the right steps by caring about etiquette. Your contribution matters, and the community is lucky to have thoughtful contributors like you. Good luck!

*Note: Avoid tagging too many people at once; start with one maintainer or the general team handle. Always keep the tone collaborative, never entitled.*
