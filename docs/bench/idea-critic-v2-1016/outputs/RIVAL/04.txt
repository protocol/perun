## Recommendation

**Don't take your build-vs-buy question to the board as a binary, and don't sign the deal as offered.** Propose a conditional counter-offer, with build as the default if it fails. Your build instinct is right on price but wrong on what is being compared.

## Where the leaning is weak

- **You're comparing different things.** Building gets you a feature. Buying gets you a feature plus 700 accounts. Counting only the feature makes $2.1M look absurd against roughly $300-400k of engineering.
- **Your build cost is probably low.** 20 engineer-months at an assumed $15-20k loaded per month is $300-400k. A 50% slip, which is normal, gives $450-600k. The larger cost is that 4 engineers is a big share of a 25-person company for 5-8 months, and the feature arrives that much later.
- **The price is not clearly bad.** It is 2.3x acquired ARR. At your 60% retention that is about 3.9x retained ARR ($540k). At 40% retention it is 5.8x. Net of avoided build cost, you are paying about $1.5-1.7M for the customer base.

## Where buying is weak

- **Retention is an unsourced assumption.** Everything turns on it, and migrations between survey products usually lose more than founders expect. Find out where the 60% comes from.
- **Code usability is unknown.** If you rewrite most of it, you paid $2.1M for customers and a brand, and you still build.
- **Legal diligence isn't done.** Check change-of-control and assignment clauses, and whether customers can terminate on sale.
- **Half the price is stock.** That is dilution, and you haven't said at what valuation.
- **Integration is a distraction.** A 25-person team would be migrating 700 accounts while shipping.

## What to put to the board on 3 November

1. **Counter-offer:** lower cash, and make the stock portion an earn-out tied to ARR retained at 12 months. That moves the retention risk to Pebble, where it belongs.
2. **Conditions:** a satisfactory code audit and completed legal diligence before signing.
3. **Extension:** ask Pebble to move 6 November to about 20 November. Their refusal would tell you something.
4. **Start now:** one engineer spends two weeks auditing their code, which also sharpens your build estimate.
5. **Fallback:** if Pebble won't restructure, or the code is mostly unusable, build.

## What would change my view

- **Toward buying:** the code is mostly reusable, Pebble's gross churn is under about 15% a year, and the contracts transfer cleanly.
- **Toward building:** retention evidence is under about 50%, the code needs a rewrite, or the earn-out is rejected.

## Data to get before the board meets

Pebble's churn and cohort data, customer concentration, overlap with your own customers, how many customers actually use offline collection, their cash runway, why they are selling, and your own cash position and equity valuation.

*Note: the loaded-cost figure is my assumption. Replace it with your real payroll cost before this goes to the board.*
