The readout describes a test where the team "stopped the test the first day p fell below 0.05" after checking the dashboard daily. This is a textbook case of optional stopping (peeking), which invalidates the p-value and inflates the false positive rate, regardless of the final sample size matching the power calculation. While the readout claims the sample size matched the requirement, the method used to determine *when* to stop collecting that sample violates statistical rigor. The other potential errors are explicitly ruled out in the text: arm sizes are balanced (no SRM), denominators match randomization units (no DENOM), results are consistent across segments (no SIMPSON), and lift was stable over four weeks (no NOVELTY). However, the PEEK error alone is sufficient to invalidate the statistical claim of significance. You cannot trust the "8.4% lift" figure as statistically proven because the stopping rule guaranteed a significant result eventually, even if no real effect existed. The test must be re-analyzed using proper sequential testing methods or, more likely, re-run with a fixed horizon that is not checked early.
