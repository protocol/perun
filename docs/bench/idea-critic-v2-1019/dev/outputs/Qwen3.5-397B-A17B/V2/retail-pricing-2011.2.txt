The claim is that replacing a coupon-driven model with everyday low pricing and branded shops will reverse sales declines for a mid-market department store chain. The steelman: eliminating discount complexity restores margin and modernizes the brand, leveraging the CEO's prior success in electronics retail.

However, critical blockers exist. First, the customer base is explicitly "older" and "coupon-driven," with most sales historically occurring at a discount. This demographic is price-sensitive and behaviorally trained to wait for promotions; removing coupons risks immediate revenue collapse rather than loyalty retention. Second, the proposal ignores category differences: electronics are high-consideration, specification-driven purchases where branding dominates, whereas department stores rely on promotional urgency for discretionary goods. Third, the plan lacks a pilot. Rolling out a fundamental pricing strategy change across the entire chain simultaneously removes the ability to measure elasticity or adjust before systemic failure.

The "better way" is a staged approach: test everyday pricing in a subset of stores or specific categories while maintaining the promotional model elsewhere. This validates whether the specific customer base accepts the new value proposition without betting the entire company on an unproven hypothesis. The cost of delay (waiting for pilot results) is negligible compared to the risk of a full-chain rollout that alienates the core customer base immediately.

The pre-mortem is clear: six months post-launch, sales plummet because the core demographic stops shopping, waiting for non-existent sales, while new customers do not arrive fast enough to replace them. The "one-way door" nature of brand perception and customer habit loss makes this irreversible once fully deployed.

There is no evidence provided that the department store customer behaves like the electronics store customer. Assuming they do is a fatal flaw. The plan fails the blocker test: it contradicts the stated historical sales data (discount-dependent) and skips necessary validation (pilot).

Blockers: Core demographic mismatch with strategy; no pilot to validate price elasticity; false analogy between electronics and department store consumer behavior.
Risks: Immediate revenue drop; brand alienation; inability to revert customer expectations.

DECISION: NO-GO
