Steelman: Vertical integration secures the sole remaining hardware partner for a niche OS, preventing immediate defection to the dominant rival and buying time to fix the app ecosystem.

However, the proposal fails the blocker test. The core problem is an app-ecosystem deficit driven by the market leader's dominance, not a lack of hardware control. Buying a handset division with falling sales does not solve the "chicken-and-egg" developer problem; it merely adds a loss-making hardware burden to a software company. History shows that owning the factory does not compel developers to write apps if the user base remains small and shrinking.

The €5.4 billion cost creates a massive financial drag precisely when capital is needed for developer incentives and platform innovation. Integrating a large, struggling hardware workforce introduces severe execution risk and cultural clash, distracting leadership from the primary software mission. Furthermore, this move may alienate other handset makers still using the OS, pushing them faster toward the rival platform due to fears of favoritism or competition.

The "do nothing" or "partnership-only" alternative is superior: maintain the manufacturer as a neutral partner without assuming its liabilities. If the manufacturer defects, the OS was likely already doomed by the app gap, not the ownership structure. Acquiring a declining asset to solve a network-effect problem is a strategic mismatch. The falling sales indicate the hardware is already losing the market; buying it locks the buyer into a losing position rather than reversing the trend.

Blockers:
1. The acquisition does not address the root cause (app ecosystem) while adding significant financial and operational drag.
2. Falling sales of the target asset confirm it cannot serve as a savior for the platform.
3. High risk of alienating other hardware partners, accelerating the very defection the deal aims to prevent.

Risks: Integration failure, continued developer abandonment, capital misallocation.

DECISION: NO-GO
