The claim is that launching a new mid-priced division in 1957 will capture growth and fill a portfolio gap. The steelman: a dedicated brand prevents cannibalization while targeting a proven, expanding demographic with tailored styling.

However, the "better-way" analysis reveals a fatal flaw in the "do nothing" comparison. The proposal admits a recession risk and a potentially shrinking segment, yet commits to massive fixed costs (new dealers, tooling) exactly when consumer discretion tightens. A cheaper, reversible alternative exists: badge-engineering existing platforms into the current premium or low-priced lines to test the price point without the capital expenditure of a standalone dealer network. This achieves the market test with a fraction of the risk.

The "kill-criteria" pre-mortem shows that if the recession hits in 1957-58 (a known cyclical risk), the new division becomes an immediate cash drain with no exit ramp, as dealer contracts and brand identity are one-way doors. The proposal relies on the segment *continuing* to grow, contradicting its own admission of shrinkage risk. The cost of delay (waiting for post-recession clarity) is lower than the cost of failure (sunk infrastructure in a down market).

The strongest attack is the timing mismatch: committing to high-fixed-cost expansion right before a probable economic contraction. The proposal treats "market research" as a shield against macroeconomic cycles, which it is not. The evidence provided admits the segment could shrink and a recession is possible; proceeding with a "massive" launch under these conditions fails the blocker test of financial viability. The plan does not beat "do nothing" or a "low-cost pilot" because it assumes continuous growth in a volatile environment.

Blockers: The plan requires massive fixed investment (dealers, brand) immediately prior to a high-probability recession and potential segment shrinkage, with no staged entry to mitigate loss.
Risks: Brand overlap, execution complexity, cyclical downturn.

DECISION: NO-GO
