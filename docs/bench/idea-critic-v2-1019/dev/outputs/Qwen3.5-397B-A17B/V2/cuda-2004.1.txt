**Steelman:** Embedding general-purpose compute capability in mass-market GPUs creates a latent, ubiquitous supercomputer network, positioning the company as the foundational hardware provider for future scientific and AI workloads before competitors recognize the shift.

**Attack:**
1.  **Financial Suicide:** Adding silicon area and funding toolchains raises unit costs in a hyper-competitive, price-sensitive gaming market. Without immediate revenue offset, margins collapse.
2.  **Strategic Distraction:** The core business is a "race with its main rival." Diverting top engineering talent to build compilers and teach universities risks losing the gaming crown jewel, the only current cash flow.
3.  **Market Void:** "No large paying market exists." Betting on a non-existent customer base violates basic capital allocation. Academic experimentation with "hacked APIs" does not prove commercial viability.
4.  **Better Way:** Wait for clear demand signals. Let academics continue hacking; build specialized cards only when a specific, paying industry vertical emerges. Do not subsidize R&D through every consumer unit.

**Pre-mortem:** By 2006, the gaming division loses market share due to feature delays caused by the computing diversion. The software stack remains unfinished and unused. The company burns cash on a "future" that arrives too late or on a different architecture entirely. Shareholders revolt over wasted CAPEX.

**Fit:** This contradicts the immediate survival need to win the gaming war. It assumes a future (AI/General Compute) that is currently speculative.

**Blocker:** The proposal requires raising unit costs and diverting critical engineering resources from the *only* existing revenue generator (gaming) to serve a *non-existent* market, while actively losing a fierce competitive race. The math does not work: you cannot fund a speculative moonshot by taxing your sole lifeline without a committed anchor customer.

**Risks:** Total loss of gaming dominance; cash burn with zero ROI; failure to attract developers despite tooling.

**Conclusion:** This is a classic "innovator's dilemma" trap executed poorly. You do not bet the farm on a phantom market while your house is on fire (competitive race). The lack of committed customers is a fatal flaw.

DECISION: NO-GO
