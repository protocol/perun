The claim is that acquiring a small, modern OS vendor will rescue a failing PC maker despite high cost, migration risk, and historical friction. Steelman: This is the only viable path to a competitive, object-oriented core quickly enough to reverse market share loss, leveraging the founder's unique vision where internal efforts failed.

**Skeptic:** The target has negligible revenue and few units sold; paying a premium for unproven scale is financially irrational by standard metrics. The founder's past conflict could destabilize leadership. However, internal replacement has already failed twice, indicating zero organic capability. The "small revenue" metric is irrelevant if the asset is strategic technology, not current cash flow.

**Better-way:** Licensing another vendor's system avoids integration risk but cedes control and differentiation, potentially accelerating commoditization. "Do nothing" guarantees continued decline. Building from scratch has a proven failure rate of 100% for this entity. Acquisition is the only option offering both ownership and speed.

**Kill-criteria & Pre-mortem:** If the integration takes longer than 18 months or the founder refuses effective collaboration, the bet fails. The risk of developer migration is real but inherent to any OS change; delaying it ensures death. The price is high, but the cost of delay (continued market share erosion) exceeds the acquisition premium. The "difficult history" is a manageable personnel risk compared to the existential threat of no OS.

**Decision:** The proposal beats "do nothing" and licensing because it secures the only asset capable of differentiating the product line. The risks are executional, not fatal flaws. The alternative is liquidation. The high price is justified as a survival cost. No blocker exists in the stated facts that invalidates the core logic: buy the only available modern core to stop the bleeding.

DECISION: GO
