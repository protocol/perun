**Steelman:** Leverage existing mobile OS ownership to enter the tablet market immediately, matching the leader's price while aggressively subsidizing developer growth to overcome the app gap.

**Attack:** The proposal fails the "fit" and "evidence" tests. In 2011, tablet utility is defined almost exclusively by app availability, not hardware specs. A "small app catalog" at a "market-leading price" offers negative value compared to the dominant competitor. Shoppers perceive the product as unproven precisely because it lacks apps; heavy advertising without a robust ecosystem will burn capital accelerating rejection, not adoption. The "do nothing" alternative (wait for OS maturity) or a "low-price pioneer" strategy (undercut significantly to force trial) are superior. The current plan asks users to pay a premium for an inferior experience during a leadership transition—a compounding risk.

**Pre-mortem:** The device launches, ads drive traffic, but conversion collapses at the "apps" comparison. Developers ignore outreach because the installed base is tiny and churns quickly. The project is cancelled in 18 months, having damaged the brand's premium perception.

**Blockers:**
1. **Price/App Mismatch:** Charging market-leading prices for a fraction of the utility is a factual economic failure. The value proposition is negative.
2. **Timing:** Launching during leadership transition with an unproven OS multiplies execution risk without a compensating advantage.

**Risks:** Developer apathy despite outreach; brand erosion from a failed premium launch.

**Decision Logic:** The plan requires users to ignore objective inferiority (app count) based on future promises (outreach). This contradicts observed buyer behavior in platform markets. The proposal must either drop the price significantly to reflect the app deficit or delay launch until the catalog matures. As proposed, it is a capital destruction event.

**Kill Criteria:** If app count does not reach 50% of the leader within 6 months, halt advertising spend immediately.

**Strongest Attack Survived:** The argument that "marketing can bootstrap the ecosystem" was tested against historical platform failures where marketing failed to overcome network effects without a price or utility wedge. It did not survive; marketing amplifies product-market fit, it does not create it ex nihilo.

DECISION: NO-GO
