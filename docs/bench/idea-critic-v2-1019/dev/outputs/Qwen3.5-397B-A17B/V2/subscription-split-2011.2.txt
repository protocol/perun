The claim is that splitting the DVD and streaming services into separate brands, accounts, and billing systems will allow each to pursue optimal strategies despite a recent 60% price hike for the combined plan.

**Steelman:** Separating the declining DVD asset from the growth-oriented streaming business prevents brand drag, allows distinct management focus, and isolates the financial optics of the DVD decline from the streaming valuation.

**Attack:** The proposal ignores the immediate context: a 60% price increase occurred just two months prior. Splitting the services now forces customers who previously paid one bill to manage two accounts and two bills, effectively compounding the friction of the recent price hike. This looks less like a strategic pivot and more like a obfuscation tactic to hide the magnitude of the price increase or to make canceling the DVD portion difficult. The loss of shared ratings destroys the unified value proposition that likely justified the original bundle. Without shared data, the streaming recommendation engine degrades, reducing retention exactly when churn risk is highest due to pricing. The "customer confusion" risk is understated; this is active customer hostility. The proposal treats the services as unrelated, but the customer base overlaps significantly. Forcing a split immediately after a massive price hike signals that the company is abandoning its legacy customers rather than transitioning them.

**Better Way:** Maintain a single brand and account structure. Offer tiered pricing within one bill (e.g., Streaming Only, DVD Only, Both) to let customers self-select without the friction of separate logins. Share data to improve recommendations across formats. Manage the business units internally without fracturing the user experience.

**Kill Criteria:** If the split results in a >15% churn rate in the first quarter or a significant drop in streaming engagement due to degraded recommendations, the strategy fails.

**Verdict:** The timing is catastrophic. Executing a complex, friction-inducing split immediately after a controversial price hike validates customer fears of corporate greed and confusion. It solves an internal management preference at the expense of the user experience during a volatile period.

DECISION: NO-GO
