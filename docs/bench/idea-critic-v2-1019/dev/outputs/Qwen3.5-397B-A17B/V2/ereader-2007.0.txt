The claim is that a non-hardware bookseller should launch a subsidized e-reader with free cellular connectivity to dominate the emerging digital market. The steelman: only a seamless, always-on device priced at cost can overcome prior adoption failures and publisher piracy fears by locking users into a single store.

However, critical blockers exist in the proposal's own facts. First, the company has **no experience designing consumer hardware**, yet the plan mandates building a custom device with complex cellular integration—a high-capital, low-reversibility bet where failure means total loss of R&D spend. Second, the economic model relies on "low price per title" and "free cellular," which explicitly triggers the stated risk of **squeezed margins** and **publisher antagonism**. Without an existing hardware supply chain or negotiated digital rights leverage, the unit economics likely fail immediately. Third, "earlier e-readers sold poorly" suggests the market is unproven; entering with a full-scale proprietary hardware launch rather than a software-first or partnership approach violates the principle of minimizing sunk costs when testing demand.

The "better way" is to license existing hardware or launch a software application first to validate digital demand without the burden of manufacturing and cellular subsidies. The proposal forces the company to solve three hard problems simultaneously (hardware design, carrier negotiations, publisher pricing wars) despite having zero core competencies in any of them. The risk of cannibalizing print sales is real, but doing so with a likely unprofitable hardware venture compounds the error.

The strongest attack is the mismatch between the company's core competency (retail/logistics) and the proposal's requirements (hardware engineering/telecom). The evidence provided ("never designed consumer hardware") directly contradicts the feasibility of executing this specific plan successfully. A cheaper, safer alternative exists: partner with an existing hardware vendor to host the store, proving the digital model before committing to proprietary silicon and network contracts.

Blockers: Lack of hardware capability makes execution highly probable to fail; subsidized pricing model threatens immediate margin collapse without guaranteed volume.
Risks: Publisher backlash, print cannibalization, capital drain.

DECISION: NO-GO
