The proposal assumes that integrating two incompatible network technologies will yield synergies outweighing the $36 billion cost and integration risks. The steelman is that acquiring the target's high-value business customer base justifies the massive expense and technical debt of running legacy systems during a complex migration.

However, the "better way" analysis reveals a critical flaw: the target's core value (push-to-talk) relies on technology incompatible with the buyer's data-limited legacy network. Forcing a merger requires maintaining dual networks indefinitely or undertaking a costly, risky migration that could alienate the very business customers being acquired. The "do nothing" alternative—organic growth or a technology partnership—avoids the $36 billion goodwill risk and the operational nightmare of incompatible infrastructure.

The pre-mortem shows failure via integration paralysis: the combined entity spends years and billions trying to merge incompatible systems, degrading service for both consumer and business segments, while competitors launch native IP-based push-to-talk solutions that render the target's legacy tech obsolete. The cost of delay is high, but the cost of a botched integration is existential.

The strongest attack is the technological incompatibility itself. In 2005, the industry was shifting toward IP-based standards. Acquiring a legacy, incompatible network locks the buyer into maintaining obsolete infrastructure while competitors innovate. The goodwill asset is fragile; if integration fails or service dips, the high-value business customers will churn to more reliable, modern providers. The proposal lacks a credible, low-risk path to migrate the target's users to the buyer's platform without losing the specific utility (instant push-to-talk) they pay for.

There is no clear kill criterion defined for when to abandon the integration if costs spiral, nor a leading metric to track customer retention during the transition. The risk of creating a bloated, inefficient hybrid network with no clear path to unified technology is a blocker. The cheaper, safer alternative is to license the push-to-talk technology or build a compatible solution, rather than buying the entire incompatible network and its baggage.

DECISION: NO-GO
