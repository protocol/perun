The claim is that acquiring the struggling handset manufacturer for €5.4 billion will secure the ecosystem and prevent a rival pivot. The steelman: vertical integration is the only remaining lever to stop the partner's defection and force app developer attention when market share is critically low.

However, the "better way" analysis reveals a fatal flaw. The core problem is an app-ecosystem deficit driven by the market leader's dominance, not a lack of hardware control. Buying a falling hardware division does not solve the developer incentive problem; it merely adds a bleeding cash-flow business to a software company. The cheaper, safer alternative is to license the OS widely to *all* manufacturers to maximize install base, rather than competing with them. Ownership creates a conflict of interest that may drive other handset makers to the rival platform faster, accelerating the very isolation this move tries to prevent.

The pre-mortem is clear: integration drags focus from software innovation, the acquired division continues to lose money due to lack of apps, and the market perceives the OS as a "single-vendor" niche, further depressing developer interest. The cost of delay is high, but the cost of this specific action is existential. The evidence provided—falling sales and dominant rival apps—suggests hardware ownership cannot fix the root cause (network effects). The €5.4 billion expenditure locks capital into a depreciating asset without a proven mechanism to reverse the app gap.

Blockers: The proposal assumes hardware ownership fixes an app-ecosystem problem, but the facts state the rival wins *because* of apps. Buying the hardware does not instantly create apps, and the declining sales suggest the brand is already tainted. No kill criteria or pivot plan is offered for if the division continues to bleed cash post-acquisition.

Risks: Massive integration distraction, alienation of other hardware partners, and burning cash on a declining unit.

DECISION: NO-GO
