The claim is that a late entrant can displace a dominant music ecosystem by removing copy-protection and adding hardware features. The steelman: users value frictionless sharing and superior hardware enough to abandon an entrenched network, and labels will accept the loss of DRM control to gain a new revenue channel.

Alternatives include licensing the software to existing device makers (cheaper, lower risk), focusing solely on a desktop store without hardware (reversible), or doing nothing to avoid channel conflict. The "do nothing" option preserves partner relationships but cedes the digital future. The proposed path requires massive capital for hardware, store infrastructure, and marketing, with high reversal costs if it fails.

Fit depends on whether the company's north star is services revenue or hardware integration. If the latter, this fits; if the former, the hardware drag is a mismatch. The pre-mortem reveals the most likely failure mode: record labels refuse to license content without DRM, rendering the store empty at launch. The "wireless sharing" feature directly contradicts the labels' primary requirement for copyright security in 2006. This is not a risk; it is a structural blocker based on the stated fact that the company is dropping the very standard partners demand.

The cost of delay is losing the digital transition, but the cost of launching an empty store is total brand damage and sunk R&D. The proposal assumes labels will prioritize a new partner over their existing protectionist stance without evidence. The "larger screen" and "desktop client" are commodities; the content is the product. Without a signed guarantee from major labels waiving DRM, the venture cannot function.

Blockers: Record labels are highly unlikely to license content to a store that explicitly removes the copy-protection standard they currently mandate, leaving the store with no inventory at launch.
Risks: High marketing spend failing to break network effects; hardware margins eroding profitability.

Kill criteria: If major label licensing agreements are not secured with explicit DRM waivers 90 days pre-launch, pivot to a software-only licensing model or cancel. Leading metric: Number of licensed tracks available at launch vs. competitor.

DECISION: NO-GO
