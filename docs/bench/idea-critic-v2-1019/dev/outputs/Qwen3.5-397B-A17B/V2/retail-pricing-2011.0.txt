The claim is that replacing a coupon-driven model with everyday low pricing (EDLP) and branded shops will reverse falling sales for a mid-market department store whose customers are historically trained to wait for discounts.

**Skeptic:** The core assumption is that the existing customer base values pricing simplicity over perceived savings, despite years of behavior proving the opposite. The proposal ignores that "falling sales" may be exacerbated by removing the very mechanism (coupons) that drives traffic for this specific demographic. There is no evidence presented that the new CEO's success in electronics translates to apparel/home goods where promotion elasticity differs.

**Better-way:** The "do nothing" alternative involves optimizing the current coupon model rather than discarding it. A superior alternative is a phased pilot: test EDLP in 5-10 stores to measure churn before a chain-wide rollout. This reduces catastrophic risk while gathering data. The current plan rejects the cheaper, safer option of validation.

**Kill-criteria:** The pre-mortem is clear: if loyal customers perceive "everyday prices" as higher than "sale prices," traffic will collapse immediately. The one-way door is the full rollout; once the coupon expectation is broken, retraining customers is nearly impossible if the new model fails. The cost of delay (waiting for a pilot) is negligible compared to the cost of a chain-wide failure.

**Decision:** This is a **NO-GO**. The plan fails the blocker test: it attempts a high-risk, one-way door transformation based on unverified assumptions about a different customer segment, without a pilot to validate the hypothesis. The absence of a test run for a strategy that contradicts decades of customer behavior is a fatal flaw. The correct path is a capped, time-boxed pilot to establish kill criteria (e.g., traffic retention rates) before committing the entire chain.

DECISION: NO-GO
