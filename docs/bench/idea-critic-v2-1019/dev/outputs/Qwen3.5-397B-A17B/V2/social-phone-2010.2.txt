The proposal fails the blocker test. The primary blocker is the value proposition mismatch: targeting teenagers who "live on social networks" with a device that lacks an application store at launch. In 2010, social networking on mobile is increasingly app-centric (e.g., Facebook, Twitter apps), not just browser-based. A phone without apps cannot serve the core habit of the target demographic, regardless of messaging heritage.

The "sunk cost" of two years of development is a fallacy; it is a reason to stop, not proceed. Continuing to invest in a product that does not meet user needs compounds the loss. The decision to use a new in-house operating system rather than the company's existing mobile platform fragments resources and delays ecosystem maturity, creating a secondary blocker: high friction for both users (no data plan affordability) and developers (no store, new OS).

The alternative of "do nothing" or pivoting to use the existing platform with an immediate app store strategy offers a clearer path to relevance. The current plan demands teenagers pay a premium data plan for a crippled experience. This contradicts the stated goal of capturing the social-networking youth market. The risks are not merely theoretical; they are structural flaws in the offer. The pre-mortem is obvious: the phones launch, teens realize they cannot access their preferred social tools easily, sales flatline, and the carrier partnership sours due to high churn.

There is no kill criterion that saves this specific configuration because the core utility is missing at launch. Waiting to add an app store later cedes the market to rivals with established catalogs now. The sunk costs should be written off immediately to prevent further capital destruction.

DECISION: NO-GO
