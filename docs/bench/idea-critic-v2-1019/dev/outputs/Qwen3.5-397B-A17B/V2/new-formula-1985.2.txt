The claim is that replacing the flagship formula with a sweeter alternative, validated by blind taste tests, will reverse market share losses. The steelman: sensory superiority drives purchase, and removing the old formula forces migration to the superior product, maximizing efficiency.

However, the attack reveals a fatal blocker. The evidence cited (blind taste tests, surveys, focus groups) explicitly excluded the critical variable: the permanent removal of the original option. Consumers tested a new addition, not a substitution. The proposal ignores the "emotional attachment" and "brand heritage" risks raised, treating the product as a mere commodity rather than a cultural icon. Blind tests measure immediate sensory gratification (sweeter wins), but real-world choice involves identity, nostalgia, and reaction to loss.

The "do nothing" alternative (keeping the old formula, perhaps adding the new one as a line extension) captures the sweeter preference segment without alienating the core base. The proposed plan creates a one-way door with no fallback; once the original is withdrawn, the backlash cannot be undone by simply reverting if supply chains and shelf space have shifted. The cost of delay is negligible compared to the catastrophic risk of severing the emotional contract with loyalists.

The pre-mortem is clear: launch occurs, initial curiosity fades, loyalists feel betrayed by the disappearance of their choice, media narratives shift from "taste" to "corporate arrogance," and boycotts ensue. The testing methodology is fundamentally flawed for this specific strategic move because it hid the constraint (withdrawal) that defines the user experience. A cheaper, safer path exists: launch the new formula alongside the old. This proposal fails the blocker test because its own evidence (tests hiding the withdrawal) contradicts the core action (enforcing the withdrawal). The risk is not theoretical; the method guarantees the very backlash it fears by removing the safety valve of choice.

DECISION: NO-GO
