**Steelman:** A premium, connected juicer with a subscription model creates a recurring revenue stream and ensures optimal juice quality through proprietary packaging, justifying the high upfront cost via convenience and technology.

**Attack:**
1.  **Unit Economics Failure:** At $699, the device exceeds the price of high-end traditional juicers ($300–$400) that accept any produce. The proprietary bag model likely carries a significant markup over raw grocery costs. For a commodity product like juice, consumers rarely accept a "razor-and-blades" model where the blade costs 2x the market rate and the blades are mandatory.
2.  **Unnecessary Complexity:** Requiring Wi-Fi to check "freshness" is a solution in search of a problem. Freshness is best determined by smell, sight, and date labels, not an app. This adds failure points (connectivity issues, server downtime) and privacy concerns without tangible user value.
3.  **Market Timing & Behavior:** In early 2016, the "smart kitchen" trend was nascent and fraught with failures (e.g., smart forks, connected scales). Consumers were not yet conditioned to pay hardware premiums for app integration in low-frequency tasks like juicing. The friction of buying specific bags versus grabbing fruit from the fridge creates a adoption barrier.
4.  **Sunk Cost Risk:** Custom-engineering a press for soft bags is capital intensive. If the subscription churns (likely due to price), the hardware becomes an expensive paperweight, damaging brand reputation.

**Better Way:** Launch a high-quality, non-connected juicer at $399 that works with standard produce. Test a separate, optional subscription for pre-cut bags delivered to the door without hardware lock-in. Validate the subscription demand before building custom hardware.

**Kill Criteria:** If pre-orders do not cover 50% of the tooling costs within 60 days, or if the cost-per-ounce of the proprietary bags exceeds 3x the local grocery average, the model is unsustainable.

**Verdict:** The proposal relies on unproven consumer willingness to pay a massive premium for a constrained, internet-dependent system in a commoditized category. The risks of hardware obsolescence and subscription churn are too high given the lack of clear advantage over existing cheap alternatives.

DECISION: NO-GO
