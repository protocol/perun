The claim is that a dominant DVD rental leader should immediately launch a free, limited streaming tier in 2007 despite high costs, poor quality, and imminent competition from tech giants.

**Steelman:** Offering free streaming now acts as a defensive moat, retaining subscribers who might otherwise defect to upcoming competitors by locking them into the existing ecosystem before rivals establish a foothold, even if the immediate unit economics are negative.

**Attack:**
1.  **Cannibalization without Offset:** The streaming catalog is small and old, while the DVD business is profitable with new releases. Subscribers switching to the inferior free product reduces revenue per user without adding value, accelerating the decline of the cash cow before the new model is viable.
2.  **Capital Misallocation:** Licensing costs are high and technology infrastructure requires heavy upfront investment. Competing against well-capitalized tech entrants with a "poor quality" product wastes resources on a losing battle rather than maximizing profits from the current monopoly.
3.  **Market Timing:** Broadband penetration is not universal. Launching a bandwidth-dependent service now targets a niche within a niche, failing to achieve the scale necessary to justify the licensing spend.
4.  **Strategic Confusion:** Entering a low-quality market dilutes the brand promise of reliability and selection that built the seven-million-subscriber base.

**Better Way:** Wait for broadband saturation and codec improvements to lower delivery costs. Use current profits to build a war chest for future acquisitions or aggressive licensing when the market matures, rather than subsidizing a premature, inferior product.

**Kill Criteria:** If the streaming tier does not demonstrably reduce churn among users who would otherwise leave for a competitor within 12 months, or if it reduces average revenue per user (ARPU) by more than 5% without acquiring new subscribers, the initiative must be halted.

**Verdict:** The proposal fails the blocker test. It intentionally degrades the customer experience (poor quality, small catalog) while eroding the profitable core business (cannibalization) to fight a war against better-funded enemies before the battlefield (broadband infrastructure) is ready. The risks are not merely potential; they are structural flaws in the current plan.

DECISION: NO-GO
