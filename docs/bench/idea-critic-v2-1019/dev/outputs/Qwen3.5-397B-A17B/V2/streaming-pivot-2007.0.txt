The claim is that a dominant DVD rental leader should immediately launch a free, limited streaming tier in 2007 despite high licensing costs, poor infrastructure, and imminent competition from tech giants.

Steelman: Offering free streaming now acts as a defensive moat, retaining subscribers who might otherwise defect to emerging competitors, while gathering crucial data on viewing habits before the market matures.

However, the "better way" analysis reveals a fatal flaw in the "free" and "now" approach. The proposal admits the catalog is small, quality is poor, and broadband penetration is low. Launching a subpar product for free invites immediate negative comparison against future high-quality entrants (like Netflix's actual 2007 launch or upcoming Hulu/Amazon services) without generating revenue to offset the massive licensing burn. Cannibalization of the highly profitable DVD core is guaranteed, but the replacement revenue is zero.

The strongest attack is the unit economics mismatch: paying premium licensing fees for old titles to give them away for free to users who largely cannot watch them (due to broadband limits) destroys value. The strategic risk isn't just competing with larger entrants; it's training your own loyal base to expect free digital content before you have the scale or rights library to sustain it. A "do nothing" stance regarding a free tier, or a "go-if" stance requiring a separate paid pilot with strict caps, is superior. The proposal as stated lacks a kill criterion for licensing spend relative to DVD margin erosion. It assumes being first with a bad product is better than being second with a good one, which history suggests is false in media transitions.

The evidence provided (high costs, low quality, limited reach) directly contradicts the viability of a mass-market free launch. This is not a blocker that can be fixed with a metric; the fundamental offer (free, poor quality) is the blocker.

DECISION: NO-GO
