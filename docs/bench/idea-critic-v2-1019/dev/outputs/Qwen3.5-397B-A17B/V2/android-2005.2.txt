**Steelman:** Acquiring the startup secures a free, open mobile OS to preemptively expand mobile web usage, directly fueling long-term search ad revenue before competitors lock down the ecosystem.

**Attack & Alternatives:**
The primary blocker is the **$50M+ price tag for a six-person team with zero revenue** in 2005. The "open source" strategy relies on handset makers and carriers voluntarily adopting a new platform despite having no incentive to disrupt their current profitable relationships with established vendors. History suggests incumbents resist such shifts until forced.

*Alternative 1 (Do Nothing):* Wait for the market to mature. Risk: Competitors (Microsoft, Nokia) cement proprietary standards that exclude your search engine.
*Alternative 2 (Partnership):* License existing OS tech or partner without acquisition. Risk: Lack of control over the search integration; slower iteration.
*Alternative 3 (Acquire):* High upfront cost, high execution risk, but offers total control over the stack and the ability to give the OS away to force adoption.

**Fit & Pre-mortem:**
This fits the north star of "organizing the world's information" by ensuring access points (phones) remain open to search. However, if we assume failure: We spend $50M, the OS languishes because carriers refuse to preload it, and handset makers ignore it. The money is sunk, and the core search business remains unchanged. The "open" model may fail to overcome the "walled garden" economics of 2005 telecom.

**Blockers vs. Risks:**
*Blockers:* None strictly proven by the text; the price is high but affordable for a search giant, and the lack of revenue is expected for a pre-launch startup. The premise that "free software pushes web use" is a hypothesis, not a disqualifying fact.
*Risks:* Carrier resistance, lack of handset adoption, multi-year payback, no immediate revenue model.

**Decision Logic:**
While the risks are severe, the strategic necessity to control the mobile entry point for search justifies the speculative bet. The alternative—being locked out of the mobile web by proprietary OS monopolies—is an existential threat to the ad business. The cost of delay (waiting for clarity) likely exceeds the cost of a failed experiment. The "go-if" condition is implicit: execute only if the team can be integrated immediately to drive open standards.

DECISION: GO
