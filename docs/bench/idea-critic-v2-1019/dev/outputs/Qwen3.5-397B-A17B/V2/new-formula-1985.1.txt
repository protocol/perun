The claim is that replacing the flagship formula with a sweeter variant, validated by blind taste tests, will reverse market share loss. The steelman: objective sensory preference dictates purchase behavior, and removing the inferior old product forces migration to the superior new one, maximizing efficiency.

However, the attack reveals a fatal blocker in the evidence methodology. The taste tests were blind, isolating flavor from brand identity, yet the proposal executes a change deeply embedded in brand heritage and emotional attachment. The data explicitly fails to measure the reaction to the *removal* of the original, which the proposal admits was not disclosed to participants. This creates a contradiction: the decision relies on data that intentionally excluded the primary risk factor (loss of the original).

Alternatives were not sufficiently weighed. "Do nothing" maintains the status quo. A better way would be introducing the sweeter formula as a *new* line extension while retaining the original, allowing market behavior to validate the preference without alienating the core base. The proposed "all-in" replacement is a one-way door with catastrophic reversibility costs if the emotional backlash occurs.

The pre-mortem is clear: loyal consumers perceive the removal not as an upgrade, but as a theft of a cultural touchstone. The blind test data is irrelevant to this emotional reality. The cost of delay is low compared to the cost of a botched launch that triggers a boycott. The plan fails because it solves a sensory problem (taste) while ignoring the actual product definition, which includes heritage and availability. The absence of a fallback product amplifies the risk from manageable to existential.

Blockers: The core evidence (blind taste test) explicitly excludes the variable that defines the risk (removal of the original), rendering the data insufficient to support the specific action of total replacement.
Risks: Mass consumer backlash, bottler revolt, permanent brand damage.

DECISION: NO-GO
