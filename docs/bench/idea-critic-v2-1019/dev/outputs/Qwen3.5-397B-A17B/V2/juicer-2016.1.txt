**Verdict: NO-GO**

The claim that a $699 Wi-Fi-connected juicer with a proprietary subscription model represents a viable business case fails the blocker test.

**Blockers:**
1.  **Unit Economics Mismatch:** At $699, the device price exceeds high-end traditional juicers that accept any produce. The "convenience" premium requires a subscription cost per serving significantly higher than fresh grocery produce. Historical data for similar "razor-and-blade" kitchen appliances (e.g., specialized coffee or soda systems) indicates that when the per-use cost exceeds 3x-4x the manual alternative, adoption stalls after the novelty phase, preventing the recurring revenue needed to justify the hardware R&D.
2.  **Unnecessary Complexity as Risk:** Tying core functionality (freshness check, operation) to Wi-Fi introduces a single point of failure. If servers lag or the internet drops, the appliance becomes useless. This friction contradicts the "countertop convenience" value proposition. Consumers reject connected appliances that degrade to "paperweights" without connectivity, especially for a basic mechanical task like pressing fruit.
3.  **Market Size vs. Capital Burn:** Raising "tens of millions" implies an expectation of mass-market scale. However, the intersection of consumers willing to pay $699 upfront *and* commit to a perpetual premium subscription for a single-utility device is a niche, not a mass market. The total addressable market is too small to support the burn rate required to sustain custom hardware engineering and supply chain logistics for proprietary bags.

**Risks:**
*   Supply chain fragility for custom bags.
*   Rapid competitor imitation with non-connected, open-system devices at half the price.
*   High return rates due to subscription sticker shock after the trial period.

**Analysis:**
The proposal attempts to solve a low-friction problem (juicing) with high-friction, high-cost technology. The "better way" is a high-quality, non-connected juicer with a suggested recipe app, leaving produce sourcing to the user. The current plan locks users into an expensive ecosystem without providing commensurate value over the status quo. The pre-mortem is clear: the company runs out of cash trying to subsidize hardware to gain subscribers who churn due to price.

DECISION: NO-GO
