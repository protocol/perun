The proposal attempts to mitigate backlash from a recent 60% price hike by operationally severing the two services. However, this strategy fundamentally misreads the customer sentiment. The primary friction point is not brand confusion or technical limitations regarding separate queues; it is the perceived greed of charging significantly more for a previously unified value proposition.

Splitting the brand creates a "double billing" nightmare that visually and psychologically reinforces the price increase rather than softening it. Customers forced to manage two accounts, two passwords, and two distinct charges will interpret this as a hostile move to extract more revenue, not a strategic refinement. The loss of shared ratings and recommendations degrades the user experience for the remaining hybrid users, removing a key differentiator against emerging competitors.

Furthermore, launching a separate brand for the declining DVD business signals abandonment. It tells the legacy customer base that their preferred format is being walled off and deprioritized, likely accelerating churn rather than managing a graceful decline. The rationale that streaming needs freedom to pursue its own strategy does not require a consumer-facing brand split; internal management structures can achieve operational separation without alienating the user base.

The strongest attack on this plan is that it solves an internal management preference at the expense of external customer perception. In the wake of a massive price hike, the market demands simplification and value retention. This proposal delivers complexity and perceived fragmentation. The risks of customer confusion and backlash are not merely potential side effects; they are the guaranteed primary outcome. The "declining" nature of the DVD business suggests the focus should be on migration incentives or bundled simplicity, not administrative segregation that highlights the cost increase.

Without a mechanism to offset the perceived loss of value or a clear benefit to the consumer that outweighs the hassle of dual accounts, this move will likely compound the reputational damage from the pricing decision. The proposal fails to address the root cause of the friction: the price itself.

DECISION: NO-GO
