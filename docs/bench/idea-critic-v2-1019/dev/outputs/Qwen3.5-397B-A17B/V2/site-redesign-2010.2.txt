The claim is that a full rewrite with altered voting mechanics and a forced migration will reverse slowing traffic. The steelman: a modernized interface favoring publishers will attract new volume outweighing community churn.

However, concrete blockers exist. First, the proposal contradicts verified user values: the core community explicitly prizes current voting mechanics. Changing this removes the product's primary differentiator, likely accelerating exodus to the growing rival rather than stopping it. Second, the "all-at-once" migration eliminates the ability to rollback. If the new mechanics fail (a high probability given community attachment), the site suffers total outage or mass abandonment with no recovery path. Third, a full rewrite introduces massive engineering risk without addressing the root cause of slowing traffic, which may be market saturation or the rival's growth, not the codebase.

The "better way" analysis shows a cheaper, safer alternative exists: A/B test the new surfacing logic on a small segment while keeping the core voting mechanics intact. This validates publisher appeal without alienating the base. The proposed plan fails to beat "do nothing" because the downside (total community loss) vastly exceeds the upside (uncertain traffic gain), especially with a zero-reversibility deployment strategy.

The pre-mortem is clear: Launch day arrives, long-time users revolt over changed voting rules, traffic plummets as they migrate to the rival, and the team cannot revert because the old system was decommissioned. The cost of delay is low compared to the cost of a failed irreversible launch.

This plan lacks a kill criterion for community retention and ignores the specific risk of altering the core mechanic users value. It proposes a one-way door based on unproven assumptions about publisher content driving more value than community voting.

Blockers: 
1. Altering core mechanics contradicts stated user values, risking immediate mass churn.
2. Zero-reversibility deployment prevents recovery from failure.
3. No evidence that publisher-favored content outweighs community voting for this specific user base.

Risks: Engineering delays, rival gaining more ground during rewrite, total community collapse.

DECISION: NO-GO
