The claim is that a differentiated display and shopping feature justify launching a flagship-priced smartphone on a forked OS with a limited app store and single-carrier distribution in a saturated two-platform market.

**Skeptic:** The proposal assumes users will tolerate the absence of mainstream apps for a 3D display and shopping shortcut. In a mature market dominated by two ecosystems, the app gap is a primary driver of churn, not a secondary concern. The "unsubsidised-equivalent" flagship price removes the only historical incentive for buying into a niche platform (cost), while the carrier exclusivity caps the addressable market to one network's subscribers. The evidence provided admits the market is saturated; entering late with a handicap (no apps) and a barrier (one carrier) contradicts the requirement to overcome incumbent inertia.

**Better-way:** The "do nothing" option preserves capital. A cheaper alternative is to license the shopping feature as an app for existing dominant platforms, reaching 100% of the market instantly without hardware risk. Another alternative is a low-cost device to drive ecosystem consumption, rather than a flagship competitor. The current plan chooses the most expensive, highest-friction path to the smallest potential user base.

**Kill-criteria:** The pre-mortem is clear: the device fails because users cannot access the specific mainstream apps they use daily, rendering the hardware features irrelevant. The one-way door is the sunk cost of hardware manufacturing and carrier contract. The cost of delay is minimal compared to the cost of a failed launch. A blocker exists: the proposal offers no solution for the "app gap" other than hope, while explicitly admitting the lack of leading apps. In a two-platform duopoly, lacking both is fatal for a flagship-priced device. The risks are not theoretical; they are structural market realities that the proposal's features do not neutralize.

DECISION: NO-GO
