**Steelman:** Acquiring the dominant code-hosting platform secures the industry's central nervous system, neutralizing a potential rival and capturing the world's largest developer graph before a free competitor scales. The $7.5B price buys a monopoly on social coding that cannot be built organically.

**Attack & Alternatives:**
The primary blocker is **cultural incompatibility**. The buyer has a documented history of hostility toward open source, while the target's community is defined by distrust of corporate enclosure. Unlike technical debt, trust deficits cause immediate network effects in reverse: developer defection. If the community migrates to the free competitor upon announcement, the asset's core value (network size) evaporates, leaving the buyer with a $7.5B loss-making shell and no revenue growth to justify the multiple.

The "run independently" pledge is insufficient without a permanent CEO to champion it; leadership vacuums signal instability, accelerating exodus. A "do nothing" or "partner" approach avoids the capital risk and allows the free competitor to weaken the target naturally, potentially allowing a cheaper acquisition later or rendering the threat moot.

**Pre-mortem:**
Assume failure in 12 months: The acquisition triggers a mass migration to the free alternative. The buyer's historical reputation validates community fears despite promises of independence. Regulatory bodies, sensitive to antitrust issues in tech consolidation, launch a prolonged inquiry, freezing integration and spooking enterprise customers. The target remains leaderless, innovation stalls, and the stock price collapses as the "dominant" status dissolves.

**Blockers vs. Risks:**
*   **Blocker:** The combination of a leadership vacuum, active community hostility, and a viable free alternative creates a high-probability scenario where the asset's value (the network) degrades immediately post-announcement. The price assumes stability that the current facts explicitly contradict.
*   **Risk:** Regulatory scrutiny and integration friction are manageable; the loss of the user base is fatal.

The proposal fails the "must beat do nothing" test because the specific conditions (no CEO, hostile history, active distrust) make the "independent operation" strategy non-credible without first stabilizing leadership and community sentiment. Buying now maximizes the chance of destroying the very asset being purchased.

DECISION: NO-GO
