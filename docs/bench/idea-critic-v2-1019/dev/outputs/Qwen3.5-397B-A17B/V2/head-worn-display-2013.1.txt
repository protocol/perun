The claim is that a $1,500 head-worn display with a camera, launched via an invitation-only program in 2013, will succeed as an everyday consumer product. The steelman posits that exclusivity and early-adopter enthusiasm will overcome privacy concerns, high costs, and utility gaps, creating a new market category before competitors.

However, the proposal fails the blocker test. The stated facts explicitly list "no clear everyday use for most people" as a known risk. For a product positioned as an "everyday consumer product," the absence of a defined everyday utility is not a risk to be managed; it is a fundamental contradiction that prevents the core value proposition from existing. Without a specific, recurring problem this device solves for the general public, the high price ($1,500) cannot be justified, and social acceptance will likely trend negative due to the "privacy objections" regarding the always-on camera.

The "better way" analysis suggests that targeting early adopters without a solved utility case leads to a niche novelty that fails to cross the chasm to the mass market. The pre-mortem is clear: the device launches, early adopters exhaust the novelty, privacy backlash intensifies due to the lack of social norms, and the product is recalled or discontinued because the "everyday" positioning was unsupported by actual everyday behavior. The cost of delay is negligible compared to the reputational damage and financial loss of launching a product with no defined use case.

The evidence provided within the proposal itself contradicts the goal. You cannot sell an "everyday" product where the primary known fact is that there is no everyday use. This is a blocker, not a risk. The plan requires a defined use case before any go-to-market strategy can be valid.

Blockers: The proposal admits "no clear everyday use for most people" while positioning the item as an "everyday consumer product."
Risks: Privacy backlash, social rejection, battery limitations, high price sensitivity.

DECISION: NO-GO
