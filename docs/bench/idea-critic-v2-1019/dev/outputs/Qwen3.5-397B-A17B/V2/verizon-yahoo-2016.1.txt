The claim asserts that combining two declining internet media assets will create a scaled competitor to dominant ad platforms. The steelman is that only by merging these specific distressed assets can the carrier achieve the minimum scale required to survive in mobile advertising before the window closes.

However, the "better-way" analysis reveals a fatal flaw: the proposal ignores the "do nothing" or "divest" alternative in favor of doubling down on a failing strategy. The target suffers from falling traffic, falling revenue, and management instability. The acquirer has no relevant experience. The plan relies on the assumption that $4.8 billion can fix structural decline and cultural mismatch instantly. History shows that merging two weakening positions rarely creates strength; it usually compounds weakness. The dominant platforms win on technology and data network effects, not just raw traffic volume bought via M&A.

The pre-mortem is clear: integration drags on, key talent flees due to culture clash, ad revenue continues its secular decline faster than synergies can be realized, and the carrier is left with a $4.8 billion impairment charge and a distracted core business. The cost of delay is negligible compared to the cost of a failed integration; waiting allows the market to clarify whether a pivot to infrastructure or content licensing is viable without this massive cash outlay.

The strongest attack is the lack of product-market fit evidence. There is no proof that users or advertisers want this combined entity, only a hope that scale alone solves the problem. The "blocker" is the acquirer's total lack of capability in consumer media combined with the target's proven inability to stabilize its own business despite repeated management changes. This is not a execution risk; it is a fundamental strategy error. Buying decline does not create growth.

DECISION: NO-GO
