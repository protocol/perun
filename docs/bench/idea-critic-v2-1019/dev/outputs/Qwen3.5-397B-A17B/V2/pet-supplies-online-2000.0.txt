The proposal describes a "blitzscaling" strategy reliant on perpetual capital availability and negative unit economics to purchase market share. The steelman is that speed creates an unassailable monopoly before competitors can react, allowing future price hikes to recoup losses.

However, the attack reveals fatal structural flaws. First, selling products below cost (negative gross margin) while incurring high shipping costs on heavy items means every additional sale increases total losses. This is not an investment; it is value destruction scaled by volume. Second, the plan requires simultaneous execution of four capital-intensive events: an IPO, a national TV buy, a major acquisition, and warehouse construction. If capital markets close—a stated risk—the company cannot fund the operating losses required to sustain the below-cost pricing, leading to immediate insolvency. Third, buying a competitor while burning cash offers no synergy fast enough to offset the bleed.

The "better way" analysis shows that "doing nothing" (remaining private and optimizing unit economics) is superior because it preserves capital. A cheaper alternative is targeted digital marketing with positive contribution margins, rather than a national TV blitz. The pre-mortem is clear: the company runs out of cash six months post-IPO when the market turns, unable to raise follow-on funding due to worsening margins, forcing a fire sale or bankruptcy.

The evidence provided confirms the core claim contradicts itself: you cannot build a sustainable business by intentionally losing money on every transaction while taking on massive fixed costs and debt. The risks are not merely "possible"; they are mathematical certainties given the current trajectory. There is no kill criterion that saves this specific combination of actions because the foundation (negative margins + high fixed costs) is the failure mode.

This plan bets the entire company on a market condition (open capital markets) that is explicitly flagged as fragile, to pursue a strategy (selling below cost) that guarantees failure if that condition changes. It fails the blocker test: the numbers do not work, and the stated facts show the target model is insolvent without infinite cheap capital.

DECISION: NO-GO
