**Blockers:** NONE. The proposal directly addresses a verified demographic shift (young buyers entering the market) with a solution that neutralizes the primary cost barrier (sharing mechanicals) while differentiating on the specific desired attribute (sporty styling). The "cheap" perception risk is mitigated by the "long hood/short deck" proportions and the customizable options list, which allows buyers to define the car's value rather than inheriting the base model's stigma.

**Risks:** 
1. Unproven volume demand for sporty compacts.
2. Brand dilution via parts sharing.
3. High upfront marketing spend before revenue validation.

These are valid risks but do not constitute blockers because the proposal includes a specific mechanism to test and capture value: the "long options list." This shifts the financial risk to the consumer (who pays for customization) while the low base price ensures market entry. The heavy advertising is a necessary condition to create a new category, not a flaw; without it, the "dull" reputation of the donor car would dominate. The "do nothing" alternative guarantees losing the emerging youth segment to competitors who will inevitably fill this void. The cost of delay is the loss of first-mover advantage in defining the "pony car" segment.

The pre-mortem scenario where this fails is not that the car is too cheap, but that the styling fails to convince buyers it is distinct from the economy donor. However, the specific design constraints (long hood, short deck) are historically proven cues for sportiness that override mechanical origins. The kill criterion is clear: if initial sales do not show a higher attach rate for options compared to the donor car, the "customizable" thesis fails, and the project should pivot or stop. Until that data exists, the hypothesis is sound.

DECISION: GO
