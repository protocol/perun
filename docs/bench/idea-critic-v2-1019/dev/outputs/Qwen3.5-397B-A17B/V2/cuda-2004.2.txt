The claim is that investing heavily in general-purpose programmability for mass-market graphics chips (2004–2006) will create a new, dominant revenue stream despite zero current market demand and increased unit costs.

Steelman: Embedding general-purpose compute capabilities now creates an irreversible installed base advantage, allowing the company to own the emerging parallel computing market before rivals or CPU vendors can react, turning a commodity graphics race into a platform monopoly.

However, the proposal fails the blocker test. The plan requires raising unit costs on every consumer chip to serve a non-existent paying market. In 2004, the core business is a fierce, volume-driven duel with a main rival; adding cost without immediate performance benefit in gaming risks ceding market share in the only segment that currently generates revenue. The "niche" academic demand is being met via hacked APIs, proving users will adapt software to existing hardware rather than wait for expensive specialized silicon. There is no evidence that scientific users will pay a premium embedded in consumer cards, nor that the engineering distraction won't cause a fatal stumble in the core graphics race.

Alternatives:
1. **Do nothing:** Maintain cost parity and focus on winning the graphics race; let CPU vendors or software layers handle general compute.
2. **Wait for signal:** Add programmability only after a committed enterprise customer emerges or hacking becomes impossible, avoiding upfront R&D bleed.
3. **Proposed path:** Subsidize unknown future demand with current gaming profits.

The cost of delay is low because the market does not yet exist; the cost of failure is existential if the core graphics business bleeds share due to higher costs or divided engineering focus. The proposal bets the company's current cash cow on a hypothetical future without a bridge to monetization. A "go-if" condition cannot be met now because no customers exist to commit. The risk is not just execution difficulty; it is funding a revolution that may enrich CPU makers or remain a niche academic curiosity while the company loses its primary war.

Blockers: Raising unit costs in a price-sensitive volume war without committed customers or proven demand contradicts survival logic for the core business.
Risks: Engineering distraction, permanent cost disadvantage, market never materializing.

DECISION: NO-GO
