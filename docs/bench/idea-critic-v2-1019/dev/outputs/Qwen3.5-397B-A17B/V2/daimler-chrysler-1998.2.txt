Steelman: Combining German engineering prestige with US mass-market profitability creates a diversified global powerhouse capable of sharing platforms to reduce costs while capturing all consumer segments.

However, the "merger of equals" structure is a fatal flaw disguised as diplomacy. In practice, it creates a governance vacuum where neither party can enforce decisive integration, leading to paralysis when cultures collide. The German premium model relies on strict quality control and long-term R&D cycles, while the US partner is riding a cyclical truck boom that incentivizes short-term volume and cost-cutting. These operational rhythms are incompatible without a single, clear commanding authority.

The proposal fails the "do nothing" test. The US firm is currently highly profitable; diluting this success by merging with a lower-volume premium maker introduces massive execution risk without a guaranteed upside. Platform sharing between luxury and mass-market segments often results in brand dilution for the premium line and insufficient cost savings for the mass line due to differing component standards.

Furthermore, the labor culture gap is not merely a "concern" but a structural blocker. German co-determination laws and strong works councils clash fundamentally with US at-will employment and flexible production models. Without a designated acquirer to impose a unified operating system, these friction points will stall integration for years, burning cash while the truck cycle inevitably turns downward.

The strongest attack is the governance model itself: a true merger of equals rarely works because it prevents the hard decisions necessary to realize synergies. If this is a takeover, it should be structured as one to ensure accountability. If it is a partnership, it should remain an alliance, not a full merger. As proposed, the structure guarantees conflict without providing the mechanism to resolve it. The reliance on a cyclical boom as a primary asset valuation point also exposes the combined entity to a severe downturn just as integration costs peak.

This plan bets on synergy while architecting failure in its governance and cultural integration. The risks are not manageable under the current "equals" framework.

DECISION: NO-GO
