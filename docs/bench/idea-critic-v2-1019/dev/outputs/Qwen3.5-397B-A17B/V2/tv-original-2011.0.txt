The claim is that a data-driven, all-at-once release of two seasons of a prestige drama, without a pilot, will succeed where traditional models require testing. The steelman: existing viewership data is so granular and predictive that it renders the pilot obsolete, and the "binge" model creates a unique market differentiator that justifies the massive upfront cost.

However, critical blockers exist. First, the data is historical (licensed content); it cannot prove demand for *original* prestige drama or validate a specific creative team's execution. Second, the financial risk is catastrophic: committing two full seasons (26 episodes) without a pilot removes the industry's primary kill-switch for failure. If the first few episodes flop, the company is locked into funding the rest. Third, the "all-at-once" strategy is unproven for long-form narrative; it may cannibalize subscription longevity by allowing users to binge and cancel immediately, destroying the recurring revenue model.

The "better way" is a staged approach: commission a pilot or a single season first. This retains the data advantage while capping the downside. The proposal's reliance on data to replace creative intuition and financial prudence is a fatal overreach. The cost is too high, the evidence (historical data) does not support the specific leap to original production, and the structural risk (two-season commitment) is unacceptable without a proof-of-concept. The pre-mortem is clear: the show launches, fails to retain subscribers long-term due to the binge-drop, and the sunk cost of the second season cripples the content budget for years.

This is a one-way door with insufficient evidence. The lack of a pilot and the two-season commitment are blockers that cannot be ignored.

Blockers: Historical data does not validate original content success; two-season commitment removes financial safety valves; unproven release model risks subscription churn.
Risks: Creative failure, budget exhaustion, inability to pivot after launch.

DECISION: NO-GO
