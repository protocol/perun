The claim is that committing to a massive, unproven wide-body jet will secure market dominance and meet a specific customer's cost-reduction needs. The steelman is that this move creates an insurmountable barrier to entry for competitors while future-proofing the asset as a freighter, capturing value even if passenger dynamics shift.

However, the skeptic identifies a fatal blocker: the proposal relies on a single customer order to justify an outlay "far larger than any the company has made." The premise that supersonic jets will replace subsonic ones directly contradicts the long-term viability of a massive subsonic investment; if the market shifts to speed as predicted, this aircraft becomes obsolete before paying back its development costs. The "unproven engine" and "new factory" compound this into a binary survival risk: failure means bankruptcy, not just a missed quarter.

The better-way analysis shows "do nothing" or a incremental stretch of existing models preserves capital while waiting for the supersonic question to resolve. A smaller, lower-risk derivative could test the wide-body concept without betting the entire firm. The cost of delay is low compared to the cost of failure; waiting for multiple airline commitments or engine validation reduces the downside significantly.

The pre-mortem reveals the most likely failure mode: the supersonic transition occurs, the single large customer cancels or reduces orders due to market shifts, and the company is left with a specialized, expensive factory and no broader market. The raised cockpit feature, while clever for freight conversion, does not mitigate the primary demand risk.

This is a one-way door with insufficient evidence of broad market demand beyond one client's immediate desire. The risk of supersonic displacement is not a mere uncertainty; it is a known strategic threat that invalidates the core assumption of long-term subsonic growth. Without multiple committed customers or a resolved supersonic question, the financial exposure is disproportionate to the verified opportunity.

Blockers: Single customer order cannot support existential financial risk; supersonic threat invalidates long-term subsonic assumption.
Risks: Engine failure, cost overruns, market shift to speed.

DECISION: NO-GO
