The proposal rests on the assumption that merging two declining entities creates growth, a claim that contradicts basic arithmetic unless significant, unproven synergies exist. The steelman argues that combining operations reduces overhead quickly enough to offset falling revenue in both legacy businesses before cash runs out.

However, the "better-way" analysis reveals a fatal flaw: the "do nothing" or "organic turnaround" option for the discount chain alone carries less risk than absorbing a larger, failing department store. The department store's liabilities, aging infrastructure, and distinct customer base introduce massive integration complexity. Historical data from similar eras suggests that merging cultures during post-restructuring fragility often leads to management distraction and accelerated decline in the healthier entity.

The pre-mortem is clear: the merger fails because cost savings are slower to realize than projected, while the distraction of converting stores and merging functions causes the discount chain's core sales to drop further. Big-box rivals, unburdened by this integration, capture the remaining market share. The "one-way door" nature of this acquisition means once capital is spent on the purchase and closure costs, it cannot be recovered if the synergy thesis proves false.

The evidence provided shows both chains have declining sales and underinvested stores. There is no verified plan to stop the bleeding in either brand before merging them. The uncertainty around cost savings and customer mismatch constitutes a blocker, not merely a risk, because the proposal offers no concrete mechanism to reverse the downward trajectory of the target. Acquiring a larger, declining business to save a smaller, recovering one is a classic "diworsification" move that typically bankrupts the acquirer.

Blockers: No proven path to reverse declining sales in either chain; high risk of management distraction destroying the discount chain's fragile recovery; uncertain cost savings cannot guarantee survival against growing big-box rivals.
Risks: Culture clash, brand dilution, integration costs exceeding estimates.

DECISION: NO-GO
