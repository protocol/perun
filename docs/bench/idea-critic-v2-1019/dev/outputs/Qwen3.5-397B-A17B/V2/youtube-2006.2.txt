**Steelman:** Acquiring the dominant video platform eliminates the buyer's inability to catch up organically, instantly securing the category leader despite current losses.

**Blockers:** NONE. No factual contradiction exists between the $1.65B price and the strategic necessity of owning the primary destination for online video traffic. The lack of revenue is a feature of the 2006 web ad model, not a flaw in the asset's position.

**Risks:** Massive copyright liability could bankrupt the entity; bandwidth costs may outpace ad yield; the "unproven" ad model might never monetize short-form video effectively; integrating a tiny, chaotic startup into a large engineering culture could destroy the product's velocity.

**Alternatives:** 
1. *Do nothing:* Cede the video category permanently to a competitor who buys the target or builds a rival.
2. *Build internal:* The buyer's existing smaller service proves organic growth is too slow against the network effects already achieved by the target.
3. *Wait for litigation:* By the time copyright clarity arrives, the market position will be unassailable by anyone but the current leader.

**Pre-mortem:** The acquisition fails if media lawsuits force a shutdown before ad technology matures, or if the cost per stream remains structurally higher than revenue per stream. However, these are execution risks, not blockers to the strategic logic.

**Kill Criteria:** If, within 18 months, copyright settlements exceed $500M or daily active users drop by >20% post-integration, the thesis is broken.

**Decision Logic:** The "blocker" test requires a fact showing the plan fails *as proposed*. The risks are severe but speculative; the opportunity cost of *not* acting (ceding the category) is immediate and certain based on current traffic dominance. In 2006, video is the next computing paradigm; owning the distribution layer is worth the premium even with zero current revenue. The alternative is irrelevance.

**Leading Metric:** Daily video streams and ad fill rate by Q4 2007.

DECISION: GO
