**Steelman:** A premium, connected juicer creates a recurring revenue stream by solving convenience and freshness verification for affluent health-conscious consumers, justifying a high upfront hardware cost through a locked-in subscription model.

**Attack:**
1.  **Unit Economics Failure:** At $699, the device targets a niche luxury segment, yet the value proposition relies on volume subscriptions. Proprietary pre-packed bags inherently cost significantly more per ounce than fresh produce due to processing, packaging, and logistics. Consumers willing to pay $699 for hardware are often the same demographic capable of juicing at home or visiting high-end juice bars, making the marginal cost per cup uncompetitive.
2.  **Unnecessary Friction:** Mandating Wi-Fi connectivity for a basic mechanical function (checking freshness) introduces a critical failure point. If servers drop or Wi-Fi fluctuates, the device becomes useless "dumb" hardware, creating immediate user resentment for a $699 purchase. Freshness can be verified via simple QR codes or expiration dates without constant connectivity.
3.  **Market Contradiction:** The "well-funded" status suggests pressure for hyper-growth. However, the combination of high hardware friction ($699 impulse buy is difficult) and ongoing subscription fatigue limits the total addressable market to a tiny sliver of early adopters. The "razor-and-blade" model fails if the razor is too expensive to distribute widely enough to sell blades.
4.  **Pre-mortem:** The most likely failure mode is high churn after the first month. Users realize the cost per juice is 3-4x higher than store-bought alternatives, the machine takes up counter space, and the app connectivity feels intrusive rather than helpful. The hardware becomes an expensive paperweight.

**Better Way:** Lower the hardware entry price to subsidy levels to drive installation base, remove mandatory always-on connectivity in favor of offline verification, and allow open-source bag compatibility to reduce consumer risk perception.

**Verdict:** The current proposal bundles high friction (price, connectivity) with a weak economic incentive (expensive bags). The math does not support mass adoption, and the niche market cannot sustain the burn rate implied by "tens of millions" raised.

DECISION: NO-GO
