The claim is that a $1B+ mobile-only, premium short-form subscription service will succeed despite market saturation and fatigue. The steelman: high-production, orientation-agnostic video solves a specific quality gap for affluent users willing to pay, leveraging studio backing to outclass free alternatives.

However, the "better-way" analysis reveals fatal flaws. The proposal attempts to monetize a behavior (short-form viewing) that the market has decisively settled as ad-supported or freemium. Competitors already dominate attention with infinite, free inventory. Introducing a paywall for content that is functionally similar to free options ignores the "zero-price effect," where users disproportionately prefer free goods even if paid ones are superior. The "orientation-agnostic" feature is a minor UX improvement, not a value proposition strong enough to overcome subscription fatigue or justify a new billing relationship.

The pre-mortem is clear: the service launches, acquires few subscribers due to price resistance, burns cash on A-list talent who cannot drive retention without a library habit, and collapses as studios realize their content performs better on existing ad-revenue platforms. The cost of delay is negligible; waiting allows the market to further validate that short-form monetization works via ads, not subscriptions.

The blocker is the lack of evidence for demand. The proposal admits "no proven demand for premium short-form" while asking for over $1 billion. This contradicts the core requirement of product-market fit. A cheaper, safer alternative exists: license content to existing platforms or launch a low-cost ad-supported tier to test willingness-to-pay before raising capital for a standalone walled garden. The plan fails the "beat do nothing" test because doing nothing avoids sinking $1B into an unproven category with headwinds (fatigue) and superior free substitutes.

Blockers: No evidence of demand for paid short-form; contradiction between "subscription fatigue" and a premium price point; feature differentiation too weak to justify cost.
Risks: Talent costs, attention competition, studio conflict.

DECISION: NO-GO
