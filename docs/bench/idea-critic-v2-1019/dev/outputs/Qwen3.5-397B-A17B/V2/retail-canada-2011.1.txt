The claim is that rapid, large-scale entry via lease acquisition and new systems will succeed despite inexperience and entrenched competition. The steelman: leveraging existing leases accelerates market presence to capitalize on price differentials before competitors react.

However, the proposal fails the blocker test. The plan relies on a "new inventory-management system" and a "new supply chain" that are explicitly "not yet proven." Simultaneously launching ~140 stores, a cross-border logistics network, and unproven core technology creates a single point of catastrophic failure. The retailer admits it must "learn" regulation and logistics while executing a high-velocity rollout. This violates the principle of separating known risks from unknown execution variables.

The "do nothing" or "pilot" alternative is superior. Opening a handful of stores to validate the supply chain and inventory system before committing to 130+ leases reduces capital exposure and allows for system debugging in a live but contained environment. The current plan bets the entire international strategy on the assumption that unproven systems will function perfectly at scale immediately. The entrenched competitor can easily exploit early logistical stumbles to lock in customer loyalty.

The pace (two years) combined with unproven infrastructure is a factual blocker. The numbers do not work because the capacity to execute (learned logistics, proven systems) does not match the proposed scale. The risk is not merely "execution difficulty"; it is the near-certainty of systemic collapse when unproven technology meets complex cross-border reality at speed. A cheaper, safer alternative exists: a phased rollout starting with a pilot to prove the supply chain and software. Since the proposal rejects staging in favor of an all-at-once approach with unproven tools, it cannot proceed as written.

DECISION: NO-GO
