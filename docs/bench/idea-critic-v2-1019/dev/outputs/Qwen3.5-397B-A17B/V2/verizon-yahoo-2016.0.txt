The claim asserts that combining two declining internet media assets will create a scaled competitor to dominant ad platforms. The steelman is that only by merging these specific distressed assets can the carrier quickly achieve the scale necessary to survive in mobile advertising, leveraging the carrier's distribution to reverse the target's traffic decline.

However, the "better-way" analysis reveals a fatal flaw in the logic of scale. The proposal attempts to fight a network-effect monopoly (the two dominant platforms) by aggregating two shrinking, non-complementary legacy portfolios. History shows that combining two failing businesses rarely creates a winner; it usually compounds the weaknesses. The target has falling traffic, falling revenue, and unstable management. The acquirer has no relevant operational history. The "scale" being bought is an illusion of size without growth momentum or technological advantage.

The pre-mortem is clear: integration drags down the carrier's focus, the combined ad tech stack remains inferior to the dominants, and the revenue decline accelerates post-acquisition due to cultural clash and talent flight. The cost of delay is low because the asset is declining; waiting likely lowers the price or reveals the business is unsalvageable.

The strongest attack is the lack of a plausible mechanism for reversal. The proposal offers no evidence that the carrier can manage consumer media or that the target's decline can be arrested by this specific merger. The "synergy" is purely financial engineering, not product or market fit. A cheaper, safer alternative is to invest the $4.8 billion in building a focused mobile-first ad product from scratch or partnering with an emerging innovator, rather than buying a sinking ship to bolt onto another weary vessel.

Blockers: The core assumption that "scale via aggregation of declining assets" works against network-effect leaders is contradicted by market dynamics where quality and engagement trump raw legacy reach. The acquirer lacks the core competency to execute the turnaround.

Risks: Massive integration failure, continued revenue erosion, and distraction from the carrier's core telecom business.

DECISION: NO-GO
