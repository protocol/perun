The claim is that a $699 Wi-Fi juicer with proprietary subscriptions will succeed in early 2016. Steelman: High-margin hardware subsidizes a sticky, recurring revenue stream by solving convenience and freshness better than manual juicing.

**Skeptic:** The value proposition fractures under scrutiny. At $699, the device costs more than high-end blenders and competitors like NutriBullet, yet locks users into expensive proprietary bags, likely costing significantly more per serving than fresh produce. Internet connectivity adds failure points (bricking if servers drop) without clear user benefit for a mechanical pressing action. In 2016, "smart kitchen" fatigue was setting in; single-function appliances with high price tags and mandatory subscriptions historically fail (e.g., smart forks, connected scales). The market for people willing to pay a premium for pre-chopped fruit delivered regularly is unproven and likely tiny compared to the addressable market for general juicers.

**Better Way:** Do nothing. Wait for the subscription model to prove viability with a cheaper, non-connected device first. Alternatively, launch a standard mechanical press at $299 without Wi-Fi or proprietary locks, allowing users to supply their own produce, validating the pressing mechanism before betting on a logistics-heavy subscription model. The "do nothing" option preserves capital; the "dumb device" option tests core utility without the friction of mandatory connectivity and supply chain complexity.

**Kill Criteria:** This is a one-way door with high sunk costs in custom engineering and packaging infrastructure. If the attach rate for subscriptions drops below 80% after three months, the unit economics collapse. The cost of delay is low; being second to market with a cheaper, flexible solution is preferable to being first with an over-engineered, expensive failure. The pre-mortem reveals obsolescence: if the company pivots or shuts down, the $699 brick becomes useless trash, destroying brand trust instantly.

The proposal fails the "cheaper alternative" test and relies on unproven consumer behavior (paying a premium for pre-chopped fruit via a locked ecosystem). The risks of high price, forced connectivity, and supply chain dependency outweigh the potential upside in a crowded, price-sensitive kitchen appliance market.

DECISION: NO-GO
