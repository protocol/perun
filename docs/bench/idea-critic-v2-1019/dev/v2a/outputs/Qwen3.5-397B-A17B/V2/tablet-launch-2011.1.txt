The claim is that a computer manufacturer should launch a tablet in 2011 using a recently acquired mobile operating system, priced at parity with the market leader, despite a tiny app catalog and negligible OS market share.

The steelman: Leveraging the acquired OS creates a unique ecosystem differentiator; premium pricing signals quality, while heavy marketing and developer outreach can rapidly close the app gap before competitors react.

However, the skeptic hat identifies a fatal blocker. The proposal relies on "heavy investment" to overcome network effects, yet the product lacks the core utility (apps) that drives tablet adoption. In a two-sided market, users will not pay a premium price for a device with a "small fraction" of the leader's apps, regardless of advertising spend. The "unproven" perception combined with a known leadership transition creates a trust deficit that marketing cannot easily bridge.

The better-way analysis suggests that entering a dominated market with an inferior ecosystem at a premium price point violates basic adoption curves. The "do nothing" or "wait" option preserves capital until the OS matures or a niche (e.g., enterprise, low-cost) is identified. A cheaper alternative would be to price significantly below the leader to incentivize trial despite the app gap, but the proposal explicitly mandates pricing "in line with the market-leading tablet."

The pre-mortem reveals that the most likely failure mode is inventory glut and brand damage: consumers buy the leader due to app availability, leaving this device unsold. The cost of delay is low compared to the cost of a failed flagship launch. The evidence provided (tiny catalog, no market share, leadership transition) directly contradicts the requirement for a successful premium launch. There is no mechanism described to instantly solve the chicken-and-egg app problem before launch.

This is a classic case of overestimating the power of marketing to overcome a structural product deficit. The plan fails the blocker test: the stated facts (small app catalog, premium price) guarantee poor initial sales, which will starve developer interest, creating a death spiral.

DECISION: NO-GO
