The claim is that a computer and music player maker can successfully enter the phone market by launching a premium, keyboard-less touchscreen device with exclusive carrier distribution, overturning industry norms regarding carrier control and user interface preferences.

**Steelman:** By controlling the full stack (hardware, software, services) and securing revenue sharing instead of standard wholesale margins, the company creates a superior user experience that justifies a premium price, turning the lack of a physical keyboard into a differentiator rather than a deficit.

**Skeptic:** The premise that consumers will abandon physical keyboards for touchscreens in 2007 is unproven; business users, the primary smartphone demographic, rely on tactile typing. Furthermore, exclusivity with a single carrier severely limits the addressable market during the critical launch window, potentially allowing competitors with broader distribution to dominate. The lack of third-party apps at launch reduces long-term stickiness.

**Better-way:** The safer alternative is to license the software to multiple handset manufacturers (like the PC model) or partner with multiple carriers immediately to maximize reach. Another option is to wait until the enterprise market explicitly demands touch interfaces before entering. However, these approaches cede control of the user experience to partners, failing to solve the core threat of music player erosion.

**Kill Criteria:** If the device fails to achieve a specific activation rate or customer satisfaction score within the first six months that exceeds the carrier's average smartphone metrics, the project should be pivoted to a software-only strategy or discontinued. The risk of brand damage from a high-profile failure is significant.

**Decision:** The proposal challenges the "Chesterton's fence" of industry standards (carrier control, physical keyboards) with a coherent argument that those standards are actually barriers to quality. While the risks of inexperience and exclusivity are real, the potential to redefine the category and protect the core music business outweighs the safety of incrementalism. The revenue-sharing model specifically mitigates the financial risk of exclusivity. The lack of apps is a temporary deficit solvable post-launch, whereas compromising the interface now would be fatal.

DECISION: GO
