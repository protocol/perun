The claim is that a retailer with thin margins, no developer platform experience, and heavy existing capital scrutiny should launch a pay-per-use cloud infrastructure business in 2006.

Steelman: The company's massive, spiky internal infrastructure represents stranded capacity that, if productized, could create a high-margin revenue stream, diversify the business beyond retail, and leverage existing scale to undercut incumbent hardware vendors.

However, the skeptic hat reveals fatal flaws in the current proposal. First, the "stranded capacity" argument fails because internal spiky demand and external developer needs require fundamentally different reliability architectures; retail downtime is annoying, cloud downtime is business-ending for clients. Second, the proposal ignores the "competency gap": running a developer platform requires a completely different sales, support, and engineering culture than retail, which the company explicitly lacks. Third, the financial timing is dangerous; investors are already questioning heavy capital spending, and this move requires *more* upfront investment with unproven ROI, likely triggering a capital crisis.

The "better way" analysis shows that doing nothing or waiting is superior. The market is unproven in 2006. A cheaper alternative would be to license the internal tools as software rather than operating them as a service, reducing operational risk. Alternatively, a joint venture with an established hardware vendor could mitigate the lack of experience.

The pre-mortem is clear: The initiative distracts leadership during a critical retail period, the new division suffers outages that damage the brand, margins dilute due to unforeseen support costs, and investors pull funding due to increased capex without immediate returns. The "one-way door" nature of building dedicated data centers for external users makes reversal costly.

The strongest attack survived is the potential for high margins, but this is purely theoretical without proven demand or operational capability. The risks of distraction, margin dilution, and direct competition from established vendors, combined with the lack of internal expertise and investor skepticism, constitute a concrete blocker. The proposal commits real money to an unproven market with a competency mismatch.

DECISION: NO-GO
