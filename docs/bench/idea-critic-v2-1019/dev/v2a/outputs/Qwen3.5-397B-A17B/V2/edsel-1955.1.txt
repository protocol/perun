**Steelman:** Capturing the growing mid-priced segment with a dedicated brand prevents customer defection to competitors and maximizes lifetime value across the corporate portfolio.

**Attack:** The proposal relies on linear extrapolation of a cyclical market. The "growing" mid-priced segment is highly sensitive to disposable income, making it the first to collapse in a recession. Launching a capital-intensive division requiring a new dealer network immediately before a potential economic contraction creates catastrophic fixed-cost exposure. The "extensive research" reflects consumer sentiment during an expansion peak, not a downturn. Furthermore, internal brand overlap risks cannibalizing the premium line without securing sufficient volume from competitors if the economy turns.

**Better Way:** Delay the physical dealer rollout and brand launch until economic indicators stabilize. Utilize existing dealer networks to test mid-priced variants of current brands, achieving segment coverage with marginal capital expenditure rather than building redundant infrastructure.

**Pre-mortem:** Assume it is 1958 and the division is failing. Why? A recession hit in late 1957; consumers traded down to low-priced cars or delayed purchases entirely. The new division, burdened by high fixed costs of standalone dealerships and tooling, burned cash rapidly with no sales volume. The parent company's liquidity was drained saving the new division, weakening the core brands.

**Fit & Evidence:** The proposal admits a "recession risk" and "cost of dealer infrastructure" but proceeds with a massive 1957 debut anyway. This contradicts basic counter-cyclical strategy. The "perceived gap" may vanish when credit tightens. The cost of delay is minimal compared to the cost of launching into a shrinking market with high overhead.

**Kill Criteria:** Do not approve the creation of a new dealer network or fixed infrastructure until leading economic indicators confirm six consecutive months of stability in the mid-priced segment. Current evidence suggests high vulnerability.

**Verdict:** The plan commits to high fixed costs at the peak of a cycle with acknowledged downside risk. It fails the "beat do nothing" test because a staged approach captures upside with significantly less downside.

DECISION: NO-GO
