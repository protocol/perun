The claim is that a sporty, customizable coupe built on economy underpinnings will capture young buyers despite unproven demand. The steelman: leveraging existing mechanicals minimizes capital risk while styling and marketing create a new category, turning "cheap" into "affordable cool."

However, three critical blockers exist in the proposal as stated. First, the "unproven demand" risk is treated as a manageable uncertainty rather than a fatal flaw if volume targets are missed; there is no kill criterion defined for the advertising spend if initial traction fails. Second, sharing parts with a "dull" economy car directly threatens the core value proposition of "sporty styling," creating a brand contradiction that marketing alone may not overcome. Third, the "heavy launch spending before sales are known" violates basic capital preservation principles without a staged rollout or pilot test to validate the price elasticity of the options list.

A better way exists: launch a limited production run to test the options mix and price sensitivity before committing to a "very large" campaign. Alternatively, license the design to a third party to isolate financial risk. The proposal demands an irreversible, high-cost commitment based on assumptions about buyer psychology that have not been stress-tested against the reality of an economy-car foundation.

The pre-mortem is clear: the car launches, the economy car stigma repels the target demographic, the options list confuses buyers, and the massive ad spend yields low conversion, resulting in significant losses with no exit strategy. The cost of delay is low compared to the cost of a failed national launch. Without a defined metric to halt the campaign or a mechanism to decouple the brand perception from the donor car, the plan fails the blocker test. The risks are not merely executional; they are foundational to the product definition.

DECISION: NO-GO
