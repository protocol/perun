The claim is that replacing the flagship formula with a sweeter alternative, validated by blind taste tests, will reverse market share losses. The steelman: sensory superiority drives purchase, and removing the old formula forces migration to the superior product, maximizing efficiency.

However, the attack reveals a fatal flaw in the evidence. The taste tests isolated flavor from brand identity, creating a false positive. Consumers prefer the rival's taste in a vacuum but may reject the change when tied to the heritage brand. The proposal explicitly notes testing did not inform participants of the original's removal, rendering the data invalid for a replacement strategy. This ignores the "emotional attachment" and "brand heritage" risks cited, treating a symbolic asset as a commodity.

The "do nothing" option (launching the new formula as an extension while keeping the original) captures the taste-preference segment without alienating the core base. The proposed "all-in" swap is a one-way door with catastrophic reversibility costs; once the old formula is withdrawn, trust is broken instantly. The cost of delay is negligible compared to the risk of a mass boycott.

The strongest attack survived is that blind taste preference does not equate to market preference when brand equity is the primary driver. The failure scenario is specific: loyal consumers, feeling betrayed by the removal of their heritage product, boycott the brand entirely, driving share down further despite the "better" taste. The current kill criteria (taste tests) do not cover brand sentiment.

There is no fallback product, violating basic risk management for a flagship change. The proposal fails the blocker test: the evidence cited (blind tests) contradicts the real-world condition (brand loyalty) because the test parameters excluded the key variable (removal of the original). A cheaper, safer alternative exists (line extension) that achieves the upside without the existential risk.

DECISION: NO-GO
