The proposal relies on the assumption that dial-up remains a viable distribution channel and that the internet company's volatile stock valuation represents permanent value rather than a bubble peak. The "synergy" of delivering media over the internet presumes broadband adoption will be slow enough for dial-up to monetize the transition, yet fast enough for the cable network to support high-volume video traffic immediately—a contradictory timeline.

The primary failure mode is value destruction via currency mismatch: exchanging stable, cash-generating media assets for overvalued, depreciating internet equity just before a market correction. If the internet subscriber base flees to broadband providers who do not owe their infrastructure to the media conglomerate, the "distribution" advantage evaporates while the debt load from the merger remains. Furthermore, cultural friction between a rapid-move tech entity and a traditional media bureaucracy often stalls the very cross-promotion strategies cited as the deal's rationale. Regulatory hurdles regarding network access could legally sever the proposed technical integration, leaving two disjointed companies with inflated balance sheets.

The "do nothing" alternative preserves the media conglomerate's cash reserves and allows it to license content to *all* broadband providers as the market shifts, rather than betting the entire enterprise on one failing delivery mechanism (dial-up) and one specific infrastructure partner. A cheaper, safer path involves forming content partnerships without a full merger, avoiding the risk of transferring majority control to shareholders of a potentially collapsing asset class.

The pre-mortem reveals a scenario where the tech stock crashes within 18 months, triggering massive write-downs, executive exodus due to culture clashes, and an inability to pivot because the combined entity is too bloated to adapt. The cost of delay is negligible compared to the risk of immediate, irreversible capital misallocation. The evidence of valuation volatility and the nascent state of cable broadband infrastructure act as immediate blockers to the "guaranteed" synergy claims.

DECISION: NO-GO
