**Steelman:** A third-place console maker can win by abandoning the graphics arms race to target non-gamers with low-cost, motion-controlled hardware and family software, creating a new market segment rather than fighting for existing hardcore users.

**Attack & Options:**
The primary blocker hypothesis is that excluding high-definition (HD) and alienating third-party publishers ensures failure, as historical data suggests software libraries drive hardware sales. However, the "do nothing" option (continuing the graphics race) guarantees remaining a distant third behind established leaders with superior developer relationships and brand loyalty. The alternative of a hybrid high-power console fails the cost constraint required to reach the casual demographic.

**Fit & Pre-mortem:**
This strategy fits a "blue ocean" approach: making competition irrelevant by changing the value proposition. If we assume the pre-mortem where the device is perceived merely as a "toy," the counter-evidence is the massive, underserved demographic of families and seniors who find traditional controllers inaccessible. The risk of thin hardware margins is mitigated by the strategy of high volume and software attachment rates, provided the price point triggers impulse adoption.

**Evidence & Verification:**
The proposal relies on the assumption that "casuals" exist as a monetizable block distinct from "gamers." While untested, the low entry price reduces the barrier to trial. The skepticism from hardcore gamers is irrelevant if they are not the target customer; indeed, their dismissal may sharpen the product's focus on the new audience. The lack of HD is a feature, not a bug, enabling the low cost essential for the strategy.

**Decision:**
There is no factual blocker proving the casual market cannot be monetized or that low-cost hardware cannot sustain the business model via volume. The risks (perception, third-party support) are manageable through first-party software hits and marketing, whereas the status quo offers no path to market leadership. The potential upside of defining a new category outweighs the risk of failing to compete in the old one.

DECISION: GO
