The claim assumes open-source software alone can displace entrenched mobile OS vendors and overcome carrier control without a hardware business or revenue model. The steelman: acquiring this team is the cheapest option to seed a mobile ecosystem that drives search ad volume, betting that handset makers will adopt a free, superior OS to compete with incumbents, ultimately expanding the addressable market for search ads.

Alternatives include building an internal OS (slower, higher cost), licensing an existing OS (less control, likely costly), or doing nothing (risking irrelevance as mobile web grows). Doing nothing fails if mobile search becomes dominant; building internally likely misses the window. Licensing lacks the open-source leverage to force adoption.

However, critical blockers exist. First, handset makers have no incentive to adopt a new OS from a search company; they are locked into existing vendor relationships and see no immediate value in switching to an unproven platform. Second, carriers control distribution and have no reason to support a free OS that disrupts their current revenue models (e.g., walled gardens, paid services). Third, the $50M price tag for a six-person team with no product, revenue, or clear path to adoption is unjustifiable without evidence of unique technology or talent that cannot be hired cheaper. The "free" model does not solve the adoption chicken-and-egg problem: no users means no developer interest, and no handsets means no users.

The pre-mortem reveals failure: three years post-acquisition, no major handset maker has adopted the OS, carriers block its installation, and the $50M is sunk with zero impact on search ad revenue. The cost of delay is low; the mobile web is nascent, and waiting for clearer signals (e.g., iPhone launch, Android's actual traction) reduces risk. Without a concrete plan to force handset adoption or carrier support, the bet relies on hope, not strategy.

DECISION: NO-GO
