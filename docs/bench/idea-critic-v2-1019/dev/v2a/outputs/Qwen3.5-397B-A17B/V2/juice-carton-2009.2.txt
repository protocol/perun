The claim is that a radical visual overhaul of a legacy brand's packaging, removing its primary shelf cue (the orange with a straw), will succeed without eroding recognition among an older, loyal base. The steelman: the current design feels dated, and a modern minimalist aesthetic with a functional cap cue will attract new buyers while the ad campaign bridges the recognition gap for existing customers.

However, the skeptic identifies a fatal blocker: the proposal explicitly admits the old image is the "main shelf cue" for a "loyal and older" demographic. Removing this specific cue violates the core principle of brand recognition for this segment. The "better-way" analysis shows that "do nothing" or a gradual evolution preserves the cue while testing modern elements. A nationwide, simultaneous launch removes the ability to measure confusion before total commitment. The cost risk is compounded by the high probability of immediate sales collapse if the target demographic cannot find the product.

The pre-mortem reveals a clear failure mode: shoppers scan the aisle for the orange-with-straw, see only a glass and a cap, assume the brand is out of stock or discontinued, and switch to a competitor. The advertising campaign cannot compensate for the split-second visual mismatch at the point of purchase. The "vertically rotated brand name" further reduces readability for older eyes. This is a one-way door with irreversible market share loss if the hypothesis fails.

The evidence provided in the risks section (loyal/older base, main shelf cue dependency) directly contradicts the proposed action of removing that cue. No kill criteria or staged rollout is offered to mitigate this. The plan relies on an unproven assumption that a cap shape and ad spend can instantly retrain deep-seated visual habits of an older demographic nationwide. This fails the blocker test: the stated facts show the target users rely on the very element being deleted.

DECISION: NO-GO
