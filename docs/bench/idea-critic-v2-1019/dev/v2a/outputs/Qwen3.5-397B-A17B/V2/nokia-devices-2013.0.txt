The claim is that acquiring a struggling handset manufacturer for €5.4 billion will secure the ecosystem against a dominant rival by aligning hardware and software incentives. The steelman posits that without vertical integration, the manufacturer will defect to the market leader, causing the operating system to lose its last significant hardware partner and collapse.

However, the "better way" analysis reveals a fatal flaw in the logic. The core problem identified is a lack of apps and developers, driven by the market leader's dominance. Buying a hardware division with falling sales does not solve the app gap; it merely adds a declining hardware business to the software company's balance sheet. History suggests that owning the factory does not compel developers to write code if the user base remains small. The €5.4 billion cost diverts capital from potentially more effective ecosystem subsidies or developer incentives.

The pre-mortem is clear: In three years, the acquired division continues to lose market share due to the persistent app gap. The integration distracts leadership, other manufacturers accelerate their exit to the rival platform to avoid competing with the owner, and the company is left with a massive write-down and no improved ecosystem standing. The "door" is one-way; selling a integrated hardware division later is far harder than exiting a partnership.

The strongest attack is that this proposal confuses *control* of a sinking ship with *saving* the fleet. If the manufacturer switches platforms, the OS dies anyway; if they stay but sales continue to fall because of the app gap, the OS still dies, only now with added operational drag. The evidence of falling sales and the developer preference for the rival platform suggests the hardware asset is a liability, not a savior. The cheaper, safer alternative is to treat the manufacturer as a partner until the very last moment, investing the €5.4 billion directly into solving the developer incentive problem, which is the true bottleneck.

Since the proposal attempts to solve a software ecosystem problem with a hardware acquisition that carries high integration risk and fails to address the root cause of declining sales (lack of apps), it fails the blocker test.

DECISION: NO-GO
