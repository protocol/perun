Steelman: Transform an invisible component into a consumer demand driver by subsidizing partner ads, forcing retailers to stock computers with this specific chip.

**Skeptic Analysis**: The premise that end-users care about internal components is unproven and historically unsupported for commodity hardware. The proposal assumes computer makers will accept branding constraints despite potential resentment. The financial risk is extreme: diverting a "large share" of the budget to an unprecedented channel leaves no safety net if the campaign fails to move unit sales. The "unprecedented" nature of the risk suggests a lack of data to model ROI accurately.

**Better-Way Alternatives**: 
1. **Do Nothing**: Maintain current B2B relationships; avoid alienating partners or burning cash on unproven consumer psychology.
2. **Targeted Trade Program**: Incentivize retailers directly with margins for shelf placement rather than confusing end-consumers with component branding.
3. **Pilot Test**: Run a limited regional campaign with one willing partner before committing a large budget share globally.

**Fit & Pre-mortem**: If the campaign launches and consumers remain indifferent to the logo, the company loses its primary marketing capital while partners resent the interference. This is a one-way door; once the budget is spent and partners alienated, trust is hard to rebuild. The cost of delay is low compared to the cost of a failed global launch.

**Kill Criteria & Verdict**: The proposal lacks a defined pilot phase, specific success metrics beyond vague "sales," and a contingency for partner pushback. It commits significant resources based on an unverified assumption that consumers drive component selection. The strongest attack—that this gambles the entire marketing budget on an unproven behavioral shift—survives because the proposal offers no staged entry or fallback. A "go" decision requires a capped pilot with clear exit criteria, which is absent here.

DECISION: NO-GO
