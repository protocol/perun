The proposal fails the blocker test on three verified facts provided in the text. First, the target demographic (teenagers) faces a data plan cost equivalent to a full smartphone plan; this pricing structure contradicts the economic reality of the user base, creating an immediate adoption barrier regardless of social features. Second, launching with no application store while rivals possess large catalogs ignores the primary utility driver for smartphones in 2010, rendering the device functionally inferior at launch. Third, building a new in-house operating system instead of leveraging the company's existing mobile platform unnecessarily fragments engineering resources and delays ecosystem maturity, compounding the risk of the "sunk cost" fallacy where two years of development on the wrong path justifies further investment rather than triggering a pivot.

The "better way" is clear: utilize the existing mobile platform to ensure app compatibility and lower development risk, or partner with an established OS provider. The current plan forces a "do nothing" alternative to win because the proposed product is priced out of its market and feature-starved compared to incumbents. The pre-mortem is straightforward: the phones launch, teens reject the high monthly cost and lack of apps, sales flatline, and the carrier partnership dissolves within a quarter. The sunk cost of two years' development is a liability, not an asset; it proves the current technical direction is expensive and slow, not that it should be doubled down on.

There is no observable metric or condition listed that would make this specific configuration (new OS, no store, high-cost plan) viable for teenagers. The risks are not theoretical; they are structural flaws in the value proposition. The plan does not beat "do nothing" because "doing nothing" preserves capital, whereas this plan guarantees expenditure on a product mismatched to its audience's budget and needs.

DECISION: NO-GO
