The claim is that investing heavily in general-purpose programmability for mass-market graphics chips (2004–2006), despite no existing paying market and increased unit costs, will create a dominant new revenue stream.

**Steelman:** By embedding general-purpose compute features and a robust toolchain into every consumer chip now, the company creates an installed base that locks in developers before competitors, turning a niche academic curiosity into a ubiquitous platform for scientific and data processing, eventually dwarfing gaming revenue.

**Attack & Alternatives:**
1.  **Cost vs. Value:** Adding silicon area and funding toolchains raises unit costs immediately for a feature with zero current ROI. If the "niche" never broadens, the company loses the core gaming race due to resource distraction and higher prices.
2.  **Do Nothing:** Wait for a verified market signal. Let academics continue hacking APIs; build dedicated hardware only when enterprise customers commit to paying. This preserves cash and focus.
3.  **Pre-mortem:** The program fails if gaming performance stagnates while rivals surge, causing market share collapse before compute revenue materializes. The "network effect" of developers never triggers because professional users refuse to rely on consumer-grade hardware without SLAs.
4.  **Evidence Gap:** The proposal admits "no large paying market exists." Betting the core business on a hypothetical future market violates the principle of funding known value first. The risk of engineering distraction is existential given the fierce rivalry.

**Kill Criteria:**
This is a one-way door with massive irreversible capex. Without a committed anchor customer or a guaranteed performance win in the core gaming segment, the downside (bankruptcy or irrelevance in gaming) vastly outweighs the upside (a maybe-market). The proposal relies entirely on speculation ("will create") rather than traction. A cheaper alternative is to offer a single, high-end specialized card for researchers, not bloat every mass-market unit.

The failure scenario is clear: The company burns cash, loses the gaming war to a focused rival, and the compute market fails to emerge in time to save them. The lack of a "paying market" is a blocker, not a risk to be managed.

DECISION: NO-GO
