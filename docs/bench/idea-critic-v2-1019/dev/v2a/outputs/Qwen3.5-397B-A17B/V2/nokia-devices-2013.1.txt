The claim is that acquiring the struggling handset manufacturer for €5.4 billion will secure the operating system's future by aligning hardware and software incentives. The steelman: vertical integration is the only remaining lever to prevent the partner's defection to the dominant rival and force ecosystem relevance when organic growth has failed.

However, the attack reveals fatal flaws in the premise. First, the core problem is an app-ecosystem deficit driven by the market leader's network effects; owning a declining hardware division does not magically attract developers who follow users, not corporate ownership structures. Second, the target's sales are already falling, suggesting the brand is toxic or the product non-competitive; buying a sinking ship adds massive integration drag (workforce, culture, supply chain) precisely when agility is required. Third, the proposal admits other handset makers may react negatively, potentially fracturing the very coalition needed to challenge the duopoly.

The "better way" analysis favors doing nothing or divesting rather than doubling down. If the OS cannot succeed on multiple hardware partners, it likely cannot succeed even with one owned partner if the underlying product-market fit is missing. The cost of delay is high, but the cost of this specific action is likely total capital loss plus distraction. The pre-mortem shows the division dragged down by legacy costs while the OS remains niche, resulting in a write-down.

This fails the blocker test. The stated facts confirm the handset maker is failing and the ecosystem problem is unsolved by hardware ownership. The proposal assumes control fixes appeal, but evidence suggests the market has already voted against this specific hardware/software combination. The €5.4 billion price tag for a declining asset with high integration risk and no clear path to solving the app gap constitutes a factual blocker: the plan does not reach the stated upside (stopping defection/saving the OS) based on the provided trends. A cheaper alternative is to let the partner fail or switch, freeing resources to pivot the OS strategy entirely.

DECISION: NO-GO
