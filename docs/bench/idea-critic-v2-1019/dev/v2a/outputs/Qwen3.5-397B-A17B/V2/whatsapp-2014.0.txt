**Steelman:** Paying $19B secures the dominant mobile messaging layer, neutralizing an existential threat to the buyer's core social graph before a competitor acquires it, effectively buying user retention at scale.

**Skeptic:** The price implies a 12,600% year-over-year value increase without revenue validation. The target's explicit anti-ad stance and $1 fee model clash fundamentally with the buyer's ad-driven monopoly, creating a high probability of cultural implosion or mass user exodus post-integration. Regulatory scrutiny on privacy and monopoly power is inevitable given the scale.

**Better-Way:** "Do nothing" risks displacement but preserves $19B capital. A minority stake or partnership could monitor the threat at 1% of the cost. Building a native competitor leverages existing infrastructure, though speed is the constraint.

**Kill Criteria:** This is a one-way door; reversal means admitting a $19B write-off and signaling strategic weakness. If user growth stalls by >10% within 12 months of acquisition or if the founders depart within 24 months, the strategic thesis fails immediately. The cost of delay is potentially catastrophic loss of the mobile generation, but the cost of error is bankruptcy-level waste.

**Decision Logic:** The "blocker" test hinges on whether the threat is real and unbuyable elsewhere. Given the target's hundreds of millions of users and refusal to monetize via ads, organic displacement of the buyer's core product is a verified trajectory, not a risk. No cheaper alternative captures this specific network effect instantly. While the financial metrics are absurd, the strategic necessity of owning the communication protocol for the next decade outweighs the immediate financial irrationality. The lack of a monetization plan is actually a feature: it proves the utility is strong enough to survive without extraction, making the user base more sticky. The cultural clash must be managed by total autonomy, not integration.

The single sharpest objection is the valuation multiple relative to revenue. It survives only because the asset being purchased is not current cash flow, but the *prevention* of the buyer's own obsolescence. In a winner-take-all network market, the second-place player pays any price to remain in the game.

DECISION: GO
