The claim is that acquiring a small, modern OS vendor will rescue a failing PC maker despite high costs, migration risks, and historical friction. The steelman: this is the only viable path to a modern, object-oriented core quickly enough to stop market share bleeding, leveraging the founder's unique vision where internal attempts failed.

Alternatives were considered. "Do nothing" guarantees continued decline given two prior internal failures. Licensing another vendor's system sacrifices product differentiation and control, potentially accelerating commoditization. Building from scratch again has already failed twice; the time-to-market risk is fatal. The acquisition, while expensive relative to current revenue, buys immediate technology and talent that cannot be replicated internally in the required timeframe.

Risks are real but manageable. Low sales of the target OS reflect marketing and distribution failures of the small vendor, not necessarily technical inferiority; the acquirer provides the missing scale. Migration risk exists for any OS change, but sticking with the dated system is a slower death. The founder's difficult history is mitigated by the "advisor" role rather than immediate operational control, aligning incentives without reigniting past conflicts immediately. The price is high, but the cost of delay—continued market share erosion—is higher.

The strongest attack is that the acquisition integrates two struggling entities, compounding failure modes. However, the target's asset (modern OO architecture) is exactly what the acquirer lacks and has failed to build. The alternative of licensing yields no strategic advantage. The "do nothing" option is a guaranteed loss. The acquisition offers a binary but necessary chance at renewal. The kill criterion is clear: if the integrated team cannot produce a viable developer beta within 18 months, the project must be pivoted or halted to prevent further capital drain. Given the existential threat, the potential upside of a unified, modern platform outweighs the financial and execution risks of the alternatives.

DECISION: GO
