The claim is that a non-hardware bookseller should launch a subsidized e-reader with free cellular data to dominate the emerging digital market. The steelman: only a seamless, always-connected device with frictionless purchasing can unlock mass adoption where prior dedicated readers failed, and owning the hardware is the only way to guarantee the user experience and pricing model publishers require.

However, the proposal fails the blocker test on three verified facts provided in the text. First, the company has **never designed consumer hardware**, yet the plan requires designing a custom device with a niche "paper-like" screen and embedded cellular radios. This is not a skill gap but a foundational capability missing, creating an extreme execution risk that "earlier e-readers found few buyers" only compounds. Second, the economic model relies on "new releases at a single low price" and "free built-in cellular connection." The text explicitly states these factors "may squeeze margins and annoy publishers." Without publisher cooperation, the catalog remains empty; without positive unit economics, the venture bleeds cash. The proposal offers no evidence that publishers will accept lower margins or that the company can absorb the hardware and data subsidies. Third, the risk of cannibalizing print sales is acknowledged but unmitigated; if the device succeeds, it actively destroys the company's existing high-margin revenue stream without a guaranteed replacement.

The "better way" is clear: license the content to existing hardware manufacturers or partner with a device maker to bundle the store, avoiding the capital expenditure and operational drag of hardware design. The proposal demands the company do the hardest possible thing (hardware) in the riskiest possible way (subsidized) with its weakest capability (zero experience). The pre-mortem is straightforward: the device launches late, over budget, with a limited catalog due to publisher friction, and loses money on every unit sold while eroding print revenue.

This is a one-way door with a high probability of failure based on the stated constraints. The lack of hardware experience is a factual blocker, not a manageable risk.

DECISION: NO-GO
