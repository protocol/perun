**Steelman:** Acquiring the startup secures a free, open mobile OS to commoditize handset software, forcing widespread adoption of mobile web browsing and locking in future search-ad dominance before competitors establish walled gardens.

**Attack & Alternatives:**
The primary blocker is the **$50M price tag** for a six-person team with zero revenue, unproven technology, and no carrier relationships in a market controlled by hostile incumbents (Nokia, Microsoft, carriers). The "open source" strategy assumes handset makers will willingly adopt a Google-controlled stack despite having no incentive to cede control or differentiate. History suggests carriers and manufacturers resist external OS dominance.

*   **Do Nothing:** Wait for the market to mature. Risk: Missing the mobile shift entirely if a competitor (Apple, Microsoft) locks down the ecosystem first.
*   **Partner/License:** Attempt to license the tech without acquisition. Risk: The startup may refuse, or competitors outbid; lacks the control needed to ensure search integration.
*   **Build Internally:** Google builds its own OS. Risk: Too slow; misses the 2005-2007 window; lacks the startup's specific momentum.

**Pre-mortem:** In three years, the OS is finished but no major carrier ships it because manufacturers fear Google's data ambitions. The $50M is written off, and the team disperses. The "open" model fails to overcome carrier gatekeeping.

**Kill Criteria & Verdict:**
This is a **one-way door** with massive capital risk and extreme execution uncertainty. The proposal relies on the unproven hypothesis that "free" overrides carrier/manufacturer politics and that mobile search will monetize fast enough to justify the upfront burn. The risks (no revenue, adoption friction, long payback) are not just listed; they are fundamental structural barriers that the proposal admits it cannot solve, only hope to bypass via openness.

While the strategic logic (mobile web = search ads) is sound, the **tactical vehicle** (buying a tiny, unproven startup at a premium to fight entrenched giants via open source) is fatally flawed based *only* on the facts provided. The lack of a path to adoption and the sheer cost relative to traction constitute a blocker. The safer bet is to wait for clearer signals or pursue a lower-cost partnership, not a full acquisition at this stage.

DECISION: NO-GO
