The claim is that a mandatory online client will solve update/cheating issues and create a new store platform. Steelman: A proprietary client centralizes delivery, secures integrity, and captures downstream distribution value before competitors do.

However, the proposal fails the blocker test on current infrastructure and market reality (2003). The premise assumes universal high-speed broadband to handle "large downloads" for updates and the client itself. In 2003, dial-up remains dominant globally; forcing a large download creates an immediate adoption wall that boxed retail copies currently bypass. This contradicts the goal of reaching the existing popular user base.

Furthermore, the "do nothing" alternative (patching via website/retail) preserves retail partnerships, whereas this plan explicitly risks alienating retailers who may drop boxed games if the software requires an online gateway they cannot control. The risk of competitors copying is lower than the risk of executing a complex networked service with "early bugs" that could brick legitimate copies, driving users to pirated versions that lack these restrictions.

The pre-mortem reveals a likely failure mode: the client launches with stability issues, alienates dial-up users who cannot download it, triggers retail channel retaliation, and fails to gain traction as a third-party store due to a small initial install base. The cost of delay (waiting for broadband penetration to mature) is lower than the cost of a botched infrastructure rollout that damages the core game brand.

While the strategic direction toward digital distribution is sound long-term, the specific proposal to make it *mandatory* for *new* games *now* (2003) ignores the hard constraint of limited bandwidth and the dependency on retail shelf space. A "go-if" approach (optional client, hybrid retail/digital) would be viable, but the proposal as written demands a binary switch that the market cannot support yet. The blocker is the mismatch between the mandatory large-download requirement and the 2003 broadband reality.

DECISION: NO-GO
