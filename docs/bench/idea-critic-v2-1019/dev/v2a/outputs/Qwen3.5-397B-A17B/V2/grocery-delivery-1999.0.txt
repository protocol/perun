**Steelman:** Aggressively securing infrastructure now creates an insurmountable moat, locking out competitors before they can organize capital, betting that scale will eventually force unit economics to work.

**Attack:** The proposal commits irreversible capital ($1B+) based on zero validated learning. The "first metropolitan area" launched only in June 1999; there is no data on repeat purchase rates, actual delivery costs per order, or spoilage. Groceries have notoriously thin margins; a slight miscalculation in density or waste destroys equity instantly. Building "highly automated" warehouses in a dozen unproven markets assumes the operating model works perfectly at scale immediately. History shows that automation built for unproven processes often becomes a liability, not an asset. The risk is not just failure, but total insolvency before the first warehouse reaches efficiency.

**Alternatives:**
1.  **Do Nothing/Slow Roll:** Validate unit economics in the first city for 12–18 months. Expand only when contribution margin is positive.
2.  **Lease/Partner:** Use existing retail space or third-party logistics to test density without fixed asset burden.
3.  **Proposed Plan:** Burn cash on fixed assets before proving the variable model works.

**Fit:** This violates the basic startup principle of finding product-market fit before scaling operations. It assumes the "north star" is speed of expansion, but the actual constraint is economic viability.

**Pre-mortem:** By Q4 2000, the company runs out of cash. The warehouses are half-empty, automation creates bottlenecks because order volume is lower than projected, and the fleet is underutilized. Investors refuse a down-round because the burn rate is unsustainable with no path to profitability. The company enters bankruptcy, assets sold for cents on the dollar.

**Door Type:** One-way door. Once $1B is spent on specialized construction, it cannot be recovered.

**Blocker:** The core claim—that rapid expansion is necessary before learning unit economics—is contradicted by the low-margin nature of the business. Without proven positive unit economics in the first city, scaling guarantees magnified losses. The lack of an operating track record makes the $1B bet speculative gambling, not strategy.

DECISION: NO-GO
