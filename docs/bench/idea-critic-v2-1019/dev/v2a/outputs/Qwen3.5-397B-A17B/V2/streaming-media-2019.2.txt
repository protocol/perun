The claim is that withdrawing licensed content to launch a subsidized, proprietary streaming service will generate sufficient long-term value to offset immediate billions in lost licensing revenue and massive operating losses. The steelman: owning the direct customer relationship and data is the only viable defense against distributor commoditization, and the company's deep library provides a unique moat that rivals cannot replicate, justifying short-term pain for long-term dominance.

However, the "better way" analysis reveals a fatal flaw in the "fund with multi-year operating loss" strategy. The market leader has a first-mover advantage with over 100 million subscribers and established technology. Entering as a low-price competitor requires unsustainable cash burn to acquire users who have high switching costs. Furthermore, the proposal ignores the "Chesterton's Fence" of existing licensing deals: these are guaranteed, high-margin cash flows funding current operations. Replacing them with speculative, low-margin subscription revenue while simultaneously building an unproven technology platform introduces extreme execution risk. The company has no history of running a global streaming infrastructure, suggesting technical failure is a high-probability blocker.

The pre-mortem shows the most likely failure mode: the company burns through cash reserves trying to buy subscribers, fails to reach the tens of millions target due to market saturation, and collapses under the dual weight of lost licensing income and platform costs. The "do nothing" or "hybrid" alternative (licensing while launching a smaller, premium-priced niche service) offers a safer path to test the waters without betting the entire conglomerate on a single, unproven distribution model.

The strongest attack survived is the fear of losing direct customer contact; however, the proposed method (massive loss-leading) is not the only way to achieve this. A slower, profitability-focused rollout or a joint venture would mitigate the catastrophic financial risk. The current plan relies on the assumption that "low price" equals "market share," ignoring that content depth and user experience often drive retention more than price. Without a verified path to profitability before cash runs out, the risk of total capital destruction is too high.

DECISION: NO-GO
