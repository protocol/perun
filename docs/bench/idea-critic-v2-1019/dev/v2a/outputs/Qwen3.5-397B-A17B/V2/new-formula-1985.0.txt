The claim is that replacing the flagship formula with a sweeter alternative, validated by blind taste tests, will reverse market share loss. The steelman: sensory superiority drives purchase, and removing the old formula forces migration to the superior product, maximizing efficiency.

However, the attack reveals a fatal flaw in the evidence. The taste tests isolated flavor from brand identity, creating a false positive. Consumers prefer the rival's taste in a vacuum but may reject the change when framed as a loss of heritage. The proposal explicitly notes testing did not inform participants of the original's removal, rendering the data irrelevant to the actual decision. This ignores the "endowment effect," where ownership increases value, and the non-linear risk of alienating loyalists who define the brand's core volume.

Better ways exist: launch the sweeter formula as a new line extension alongside the original to capture the diet-crossover segment without triggering a heritage backlash. Alternatively, conduct a real-world market test in a single city with full disclosure of the swap to measure actual churn before a global rollout. The current plan bets the entire century-old equity on a metric (blind taste) that explicitly excludes the primary driver of loyalty (emotional attachment).

The pre-mortem is clear: launch day brings immediate outrage, not from taste, but from perceived corporate arrogance and loss of identity. Bottlers, facing consumer revolts, will lose confidence. There is no fallback; the original is withdrawn entirely, leaving no bridge for angry customers. The cost of delay is negligible compared to the cost of a brand crisis. The "blocker" is the mismatch between the test conditions (anonymous sips) and the real-world decision (identity purchase). The evidence contradicts the core claim that taste alone dictates market share for this specific asset.

This is a one-way door with a high probability of irreversible brand damage based on incomplete data. The risks are not theoretical; they are inherent in the methodology's omission of the brand variable.

DECISION: NO-GO
