**Steelman:** Differentiate via novel interaction and price to expand the market rather than competing on raw graphical power, leveraging legacy hardware to mitigate risk.

**Skeptic:** The core failure mode is developer abandonment. If the dual-screen architecture forces expensive, non-portable code for a nascent touch market, studios will ignore it. Without software, the hardware is a gimmick. The "lower price" claim is fragile; adding a second screen and touch digitizer typically increases bill-of-materials costs compared to a single standard display. If the price parity fails, the value proposition collapses.

**Better-Way:** The "do nothing" option (competing directly on graphics) is a likely death spiral against a well-funded rival. However, a single-screen touch device would reduce hardware complexity and cost while still testing the interaction model. Yet, this loses the unique "bridge" capability of two screens. The proposed path is the only one that creates a defensible, unique category, provided the cost target is met.

**Kill-Criteria & Pre-mortem:** Assume failure: The device launches at a price point too close to the rival, offering inferior graphics and confusing software. The kill condition is clear: if the manufacturing cost of the dual-screen assembly prevents a retail price at least 20% below the rival, the plan fails. The "gimmick" risk is neutralized only if launch titles demonstrate mandatory use of both screens, not just optional touch features.

**Decision:** The proposal correctly identifies that asymmetrical competition is the only survival path. Matching the rival's specs is a losing battle. While the hardware risk is real, the potential to unlock a non-core audience justifies the gamble. The strategy of keeping the older model on sale provides a crucial safety net for core gamers while the new device cultivates a broader base. The risks are manageable if the price target is strictly enforced.

DECISION: GO
