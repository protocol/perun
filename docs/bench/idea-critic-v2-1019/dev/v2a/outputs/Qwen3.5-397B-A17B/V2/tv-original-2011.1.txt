The claim is that a data-backed, all-at-once, two-season commitment for a prestige drama will succeed where traditional pilot testing fails. The steelman: existing data uniquely de-risks the specific creative team and format, making the pilot obsolete and the binge model a defensible differentiator that justifies the massive budget concentration.

However, critical blockers exist. First, the "data" cited is from licensed content consumption; it does not prove users will pay for or watch an *original* narrative from this specific service, creating a category error in the evidence. Second, committing to two full seasons (26 episodes) before a single frame is shot removes all iterative feedback loops, turning the project into a binary bet rather than a calculated risk. Third, the "all-at-once" release has no precedent for long-form drama; the assumption that this drives retention over weekly anticipation is unproven and could cannibalize subscription duration without extending it.

The proposal fails the "cheaper alternative" test. A single-season order or a traditional pilot would preserve the unique talent attachment while drastically reducing the downside exposure. The current plan concentrates a "large share" of the content budget on a single unproven variable (original production) with no off-ramp. The pre-mortem is clear: if the first few episodes fail to resonate, the company is contractually obligated to fund and release 13+ more episodes of a failing asset, draining resources from other content pillars.

The strongest attack is the lack of a kill criterion within the production itself. By skipping the pilot and ordering two seasons, the proposal eliminates the industry-standard gate that prevents exactly this type of catastrophic budget misallocation. The viewing data supports licensing decisions, not the greenlighting of a bespoke, high-cost original engine. Without a proven track record in original production, the leap to a double-season commitment is not a strategic pivot but a gamble that violates basic capital preservation principles. The risk of total loss outweighs the potential upside of being first.

DECISION: NO-GO
