The claim is that acquiring a six-person open-source mobile OS startup for $50M will drive mobile web usage and search ad revenue. The steelman: owning the OS layer prevents search exclusion on mobile devices, securing long-term ad inventory despite no immediate hardware or revenue model.

However, the failure hypothesis is strong: Carriers and handset makers in 2005 tightly control distribution and have little incentive to adopt an unproven, ad-centric OS over established vendors. Without a committed hardware partner or carrier support pre-acquisition, the platform risks becoming a "ghost town"—available but unused. The $50M price tag for six people implies paying for potential, not assets, while the multi-year payback exposes the firm to massive opportunity cost if adoption stalls.

Alternatives exist: partnering with existing OS vendors for search placement is cheaper and faster than building an entire ecosystem from scratch. The "do nothing" option preserves capital while the mobile market matures. The proposal lacks evidence that handset makers will voluntarily install this OS or that carriers will allow it to bypass their portals.

The pre-mortem reveals the most likely failure: the company spends years and hundreds of millions subsidizing the OS, only to achieve negligible market share because the ecosystem incentives (carriers, manufacturers) remain misaligned. The door is one-way; once acquired and integrated, exiting is costly and reputationally damaging. The cost of delay is low, as the mobile ad market is not yet mature enough to justify immediate, massive infrastructure bets.

No concrete kill criteria are defined to measure early traction before further investment. The risks (adoption, carrier resistance) are treated as hurdles rather than potential blockers. Given the lack of verified demand, the absence of a distribution strategy beyond "open source," and the availability of cheaper partnership models, the proposal fails the blocker test. The assumption that "free software" alone breaks carrier/handset monopolies is unsupported by 2005 market dynamics.

DECISION: NO-GO
