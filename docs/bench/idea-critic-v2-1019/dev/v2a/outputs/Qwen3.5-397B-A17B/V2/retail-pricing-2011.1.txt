The claim is that replacing a deep-rooted coupon culture with everyday low pricing (EDLP) and branded shops will reverse sales declines for an older demographic. The steelman: this eliminates margin erosion from discounts and modernizes the brand to attract higher-value shoppers, mirroring success in electronics.

However, the skeptic identifies a fatal mismatch. Electronics buyers value transparency and speed; this department store's core revenue comes from customers psychologically trained to perceive value *only* through discounts. Removing coupons is not a pricing change but a removal of the product's primary value proposition for the existing base. The "better-way" analysis shows a glaring omission: a pilot. Testing in 10% of stores would validate if the older demographic adapts or flees before committing the entire chain. The proposal ignores Chesterton's Fence: the coupon system exists because it drives the specific traffic this chain relies on.

The pre-mortem is clear: implementation causes an immediate, sharp drop in transaction frequency as loyalists wait for sales that never come, while new customers fail to arrive quickly enough to offset the loss. The "one-way door" nature of alienating the core base without a safety net is catastrophic. The evidence provided—that most sales historically come from discounted items—directly contradicts the assumption that these customers will accept flat pricing. There is no bridge strategy for the transition.

The strongest attack survived is the leadership's past success, but that success was in a different category (electronics) with different consumer psychology. Applying it universally here is an unverified assumption. The lack of a pilot turns a strategic pivot into a gamble with the entire company's solvency. The cost of delay (waiting for pilot results) is negligible compared to the cost of failure (total chain collapse).

This plan fails the blocker test: the stated fact that customers are "coupon-driven" contradicts the core mechanism of "everyday pricing" without a proven migration path.

DECISION: NO-GO
