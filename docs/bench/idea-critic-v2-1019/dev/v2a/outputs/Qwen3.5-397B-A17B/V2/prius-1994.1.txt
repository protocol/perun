**Steelman:** Committing now secures a decade-long lead in hybrid technology, defining the category before regulators force the issue.

**Attack:** The proposal fails the "fit" and "evidence" tests. With cheap fuel and no regulatory mandate, the market signal is absent; customers will not pay a premium for unproven durability. The "novel" transmission and battery chemistry introduce massive warranty risks that could bankrupt the program before scale. Competitors are absent because the unit economics do not work yet. This is a one-way door with high sunk costs and zero near-term ROI.

**Better Way:** The "do nothing" option preserves capital while monitoring battery tech. A superior alternative is a limited R&D pilot or fleet-only deployment to validate durability without public schedule risk. This captures learning without exposing the brand to catastrophic failure.

**Pre-mortem:** By 1998, the cars are recalled due to battery degradation, the transmission proves too expensive to repair, and the program is canceled as a financial disaster, delaying genuine electrification by years.

**Kill Criteria:** The proposal lacks a verified demand signal or a proven durability metric. It relies on assumed future regulations and hoped-for tech maturity. A blocker exists: the core value proposition (fuel savings) is negated by cheap petrol, and the risk (warranty) is unquantified.

**Verdict:** This is a high-stakes gamble based on assumptions rather than data. The lack of a near-term demand signal and unproven key components constitutes a fatal blocker. The company should not commit to mass production or a bold public schedule now. It must first prove durability and wait for either fuel prices to rise or regulations to mandate efficiency.

DECISION: NO-GO
