The claim is that a closed, curated native app store will succeed despite the CEO's prior stance that web apps suffice and despite open rival ecosystems. The steelman: a single trusted store balances developer reach with the device's signature polish and stability, creating a revenue share model that incentivizes quality while mitigating security and carrier risks through mandatory review.

However, critical blockers exist in the stated facts. First, the CEO has publicly argued web apps are sufficient; launching native apps contradicts the stated strategic vision without evidence that this view has changed, creating a leadership alignment failure. Second, the proposal admits "rival platforms already have app ecosystems with open distribution." Entering a market late with a more restrictive model (closed distribution vs. open) requires a demonstrable advantage in user experience or developer yield that the proposal does not quantify. The "polish" argument is a risk, not a guarantee; review bottlenecks could stifle the ecosystem before it reaches critical mass, especially given the admitted "review workload" concern.

The "better way" analysis favors the status quo (web apps) or a hybrid approach. Web apps require no review, pose no stability risk to the OS, and align with the CEO's public position. Moving to native apps incurs massive operational overhead (review teams) and carrier friction for a 30% cut of an unproven market on this specific device. The cost of delay is low if web apps are already deemed "sufficient," whereas the cost of a failed store launch (fragmented user experience, angry developers rejected by review) is high and potentially irreversible to the brand's "polish" reputation.

The strongest attack is the strategic contradiction: you cannot build a developer ecosystem on a platform where the chief executive explicitly devalues the core mechanism (native code) required to participate. Without a clear pivot in leadership philosophy or data showing web apps failing to meet user needs, this initiative lacks a coherent foundation. The risks of carrier objection and internal review bottlenecks are secondary to the primary failure of strategic alignment.

DECISION: NO-GO
