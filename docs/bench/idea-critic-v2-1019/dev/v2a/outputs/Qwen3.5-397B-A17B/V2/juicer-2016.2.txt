The claim is that a $699 Wi-Fi juicer with proprietary subscription bags will succeed in early 2016. The steelman: high-margin recurring revenue justifies hardware subsidy, and convenience overrides cost for busy affluent consumers.

However, the failure hypothesis is immediate: the unit economics and market friction create a "razor-and-blade" trap where the blade is too expensive and the razor too costly to try.

**Skeptic:** The $699 price point is prohibitive without a proven brand, while the subscription model forces users to pay a premium for chopped produce they can buy fresh locally. Internet connectivity adds a failure point (device bricking if servers drop) and privacy concern for a simple mechanical task. In 2016, the "smart kitchen" graveyard is already filling with single-purpose connected appliances that offered marginal utility at massive premiums.

**Better-way:** The "do nothing" alternative is buying a $150 standard juicer and fresh produce, costing significantly less over time. A cheaper path exists: sell the press mechanism as a standalone non-connected device or partner with existing grocery delivery services rather than building a proprietary supply chain and hardware ecosystem from scratch. The proposed model requires mastering hardware, logistics, and software simultaneously—a capital incinerator even with tens of millions raised.

**Kill-criteria:** If the customer acquisition cost exceeds the lifetime value of three months of subscriptions, the model collapses. If the device requires firmware updates to function, returns will spike due to connectivity issues.

**Pre-mortem:** By late 2017, the company is pivoting to B2B or shutting down because the attach rate on subscriptions is too low to cover hardware COGS, and consumers reject the "tethered" experience. The market for $700 countertop appliances is vanishingly small, and the value proposition (freshness checking via app) is a solution in search of a problem.

The proposal fails the "cheaper alternative" test and relies on unproven consumer behavior shifts regarding produce freshness and connectivity. The risks are one-way doors (custom tooling, inventory) with no clear path to profitability given the price disparity.

DECISION: NO-GO
