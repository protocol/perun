The claim is that committing now to a 1997 hybrid launch creates insurmountable first-mover advantage despite unproven technology and weak market signals. The steelman: seizing the definition of "future car" forces competitors into a reactive posture, securing regulatory goodwill and brand prestige that outweighs early losses.

However, the skeptic finds fatal blockers. First, the "bold public schedule" combined with "unproven" battery durability and a "novel" transmission creates a near-certain reliability catastrophe. In the automotive sector, a flawed first generation destroys brand trust for a decade; warranty exposure on a novel powertrain could bankrupt the division. Second, the market context negates the upside: with cheap fuel and no mandates, consumers will not pay the premium for a complex, unproven system. The "no near-term demand signal" is not a risk to manage but a fact that invalidates the business case. Third, the "better way" is to develop the technology quietly (as a skunkworks project) and launch only when battery cycle life is proven and fuel prices or regulations shift, avoiding the reputational suicide of a public failure.

The pre-mortem is clear: The car launches in 1997, suffers massive transmission failures and rapid battery degradation within 18 months. The resulting recalls and negative press cement the hybrid as a "lemon," causing competitors to avoid the technology entirely for years, delaying the industry transition rather than accelerating it. The cost of delay is low because the market isn't ready; the cost of failure is existential.

This is a one-way door with high irreversible reputational damage. The proposal relies on hope for demand and technology maturity rather than evidence. A capped, time-boxed R&D phase without a public production commitment is the only rational path. As proposed, with a fixed public deadline and unproven core components, the plan fails the blocker test on reliability and market fit.

DECISION: NO-GO
