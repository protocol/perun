The claim is that acquiring the animation studio for $7.4B in stock is the optimal path to secure future hits and revitalize the buyer's struggling hand-drawn unit.

**Steelman:** The partnership breakdown creates an existential threat; the rival could capture the studio's output. Hand-drawn animation is demonstrably failing, while the target's CGI hits prove market dominance. Buying the studio secures the IP pipeline, installs proven creative leadership, and aligns incentives by making the founder a major shareholder of the combined entity. The premium price reflects the scarcity of consistent hit-makers.

**Attack:**
1.  **Valuation Risk:** At ~$7.4B for one film/year, the price implies massive future growth. If the creative leaders leave or the next film flops, the goodwill impairment could be catastrophic. Stock payment dilutes existing shareholders heavily if the synergy fails.
2.  **Key Person Dependence:** The value resides almost entirely in a few creative leaders. If culture clash drives them out post-acquisition (a common failure mode in media M&A), the buyer pays billions for a hollow shell.
3.  **Governance Hazard:** Making the target's largest shareholder a major owner of the buyer creates a powerful internal bloc that could dictate strategy or block future moves, effectively letting the tail wag the dog.
4.  **Alternative:** A revised distribution-only deal or a minority stake with first-look rights could secure content without the massive capital outlay, dilution, and integration risk.

**Kill Criteria:** The deal fails if the creative team departs within 24 months or if the first three post-acquisition films underperform the historical average. The "culture clash" risk is not theoretical; it is the primary destroyer of value in creative acquisitions.

**Verdict:** While the strategic logic is sound (buying success to replace failure), the specific terms (all-stock, total control, price) create unacceptable concentration risk. The dependence on specific individuals combined with the governance shift makes the "as proposed" structure too fragile. The buyer is betting the entire company's animation future on a culture fit that cannot be guaranteed by a contract.

DECISION: NO-GO
