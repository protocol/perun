The claim is that merging with the smaller carrier creates sufficient value to justify a $36 billion price tag despite severe technological incompatibility. The steelman: acquiring the target's high-value business customers and push-to-talk dominance immediately secures a lucrative enterprise segment that organic growth cannot match quickly, justifying short-term integration pain.

However, the "better-way" analysis reveals a fatal flaw in the proposed path. Running two incompatible networks forces the combined entity to maintain legacy equipment indefinitely or undertake a costly, high-risk migration that could alienate the very business customers being purchased. The "do nothing" or "partner" alternative—licensing the push-to-talk service or forming a roaming agreement—captures the revenue upside without the $36 billion capital outlay or the operational nightmare of merging disparate infrastructures.

The pre-mortem is clear: integration costs spiral as engineers struggle to bridge incompatible technologies, goodwill becomes impaired when legacy systems fail or are sunsets too aggressively, and the debt load from the deal stifles innovation in emerging data markets. The cost of delay is low; waiting for technology standards to converge or for the target's valuation to correct offers a safer entry point.

The strongest attack is the technological incompatibility itself. Unlike a standard merger where systems can be gradually integrated, here the core product (network access) cannot be unified without replacing the target's entire infrastructure. This turns the acquisition into a "buy and rebuild" scenario rather than a synergy play. The $36 billion price pays for a customer base that requires immediate, massive reinvestment to serve on the buyer's terms. A cheaper, staged alternative exists: acquire the customer contracts and brand, then migrate users organically, avoiding the upfront cost of buying incompatible physical assets.

Without a concrete plan to retire the legacy tech within a fixed budget and timeline, the risk of value destruction is too high. The proposal fails the blocker test: the cost to integrate the incompatible networks likely exceeds the lifetime value of the acquired customers when compared to alternative market-entry strategies.

DECISION: NO-GO
