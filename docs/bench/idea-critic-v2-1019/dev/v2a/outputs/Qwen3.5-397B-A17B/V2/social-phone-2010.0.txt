The proposal fails the blocker test on three verified facts provided in the text. First, the target demographic (teenagers) faces a mandatory data plan costing "as much as a full smartphone plan." This pricing structure directly contradicts the financial constraints of the primary user base, creating an immediate adoption barrier that the proposal does not address. Second, the device launches with "no application store" while rivals already possess "large app catalogs." For a product explicitly aimed at users who "live on social networks," the absence of third-party applications renders the core value proposition non-functional compared to existing alternatives. Third, the decision to use a "new in-house operating system" instead of the company's "own mobile platform" ignores available internal assets, unnecessarily increasing execution risk and fragmenting the ecosystem without stated justification.

The "two years of sunk development" is a fallacy; past costs cannot justify a future product that lacks market fit. The steelman argument—that exclusive hardware controls the social experience—collapses because the platform offers fewer capabilities (no apps) at a higher relative cost than competitors. There is no "cheaper, simpler" path here because the fundamental architecture (closed OS, no store, high-cost plan) is antithetical to the stated goal of capturing social-network-dependent teens.

A pre-mortem reveals the likely outcome: the phones remain unsold as teens choose devices with app ecosystems and affordable data. The cost of delay is negligible compared to the cost of launching a product that cannot compete on features or price. The risks are not merely theoretical; they are structural flaws confirmed by the proposal's own admission of the competitive landscape and pricing model. Since the plan fails to beat "do nothing" (which preserves capital) and offers no viable path to the north star of teen adoption, it cannot proceed as proposed.

DECISION: NO-GO
