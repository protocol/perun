The proposal attempts to enter a saturated, two-platform duopoly with a high-priced, carrier-exclusive device lacking mainstream apps. The core differentiator—a 3D-like display via head tracking—is a niche novelty that historically fails to sustain premium pricing or daily utility compared to battery life, camera quality, or ecosystem depth. The "one-touch" shopping feature is a single function, insufficient to drive a whole-device switch, especially when the device cannot run the dominant apps users rely on daily.

The "better way" analysis reveals a fatal mismatch: the retailer's strength is low-price hardware (tablet/e-reader) and content consumption, yet this proposal targets the high-end flagship segment without the software ecosystem to support it. A cheaper, simpler alternative would be to integrate the shopping features deeply into existing dominant platforms via an app, capturing the commerce value without the massive capital risk of hardware failure.

The pre-mortem is clear: six months post-launch, sales are negligible because consumers refuse to pay flagship prices for a device that lacks key apps and offers a gimmicky display. The carrier exclusivity caps the addressable market just as the app gap repels early adopters. The cost of delay is minimal; waiting allows the retailer to license its shopping tech to others rather than betting the brand on a likely hardware flop.

The strongest attack is the **app gap combined with premium pricing**. In a two-platform market, users will not pay flagship prices for a fragmented OS experience unless the hardware advantage is overwhelming. A 3D screen is not overwhelming; it is a secondary feature. Without the leading apps, the device is non-viable at this price point. The evidence from the market structure (two dominant platforms) directly contradicts the feasibility of a third, forked OS succeeding with a premium, exclusive device.

This plan commits real money to a low-probability outcome with high reversibility costs (inventory write-downs, brand damage). The kill criterion is immediate: if the device cannot run top-50 mainstream apps, it cannot command a flagship price. Since the proposal admits the app store is smaller and lacks these apps, the core value proposition collapses.

DECISION: NO-GO
