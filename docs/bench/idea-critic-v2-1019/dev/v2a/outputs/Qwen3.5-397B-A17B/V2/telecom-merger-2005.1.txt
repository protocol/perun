The claim is that merging with a smaller carrier to acquire its push-to-talk business customers justifies a $36 billion expenditure despite severe technological incompatibility. The steelman: acquiring a sticky, high-value enterprise segment via merger is faster than building it, and the push-to-talk feature creates a defensible moat against competitors.

However, the skeptic hat identifies a fatal blocker. The proposal explicitly states the networks use "different, incompatible technologies" and the target is "limited for data." In 2005, the industry trajectory is unequivocally moving toward data-centric 3G standards. Acquiring a legacy, incompatible network forces the buyer to run parallel infrastructures, drastically increasing operational costs while inheriting "limited" data capacity exactly when the market demands more. The $36 billion valuation likely prices in the customer base but discounts the massive technical debt and the eventual cost of migrating those users to a modern standard or maintaining a dying network.

The "better way" analysis suggests organic development of push-to-talk over the buyer's existing IP-based network or a smaller, technology-compatible acquisition. Running a legacy network as a "separate but equal" division rarely succeeds; it usually becomes a cash-draining distraction that slows the primary network's evolution. The pre-mortem reveals a likely failure mode: integration costs balloon, the legacy network degrades, business customers churn to more modern data-capable competitors, and the buyer is left with $36 billion in goodwill and no synergistic revenue.

The strongest attack survived is the value of the specific customer base. Yet, even if those customers are valuable today, the incompatibility means they cannot be migrated to the buyer's future data roadmap without significant friction or churn. The risk is not just integration cost; it is strategic obsolescence. The proposal fails the "fit" test because it anchors the combined company to a legacy technology stack that contradicts the industry's data-first north star. The goodwill risk is not a side note; it is the primary financial exposure if the integration fails to retain users during the inevitable, difficult migration.

Without a credible, costed plan to migrate these users to a compatible technology immediately, the deal buys a shrinking asset at a peak price. The incompatibility is a structural blocker, not a solvable engineering hurdle within the proposed value frame.

DECISION: NO-GO
