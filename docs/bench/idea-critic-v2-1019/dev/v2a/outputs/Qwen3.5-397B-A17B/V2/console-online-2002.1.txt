The claim is that a paid, mandatory online service will succeed despite market norms of free play and low broadband adoption in 2002. The steelman: a unified, high-quality network with voice chat and friends lists creates a defensible moat and recurring revenue that fragmented, free alternatives cannot match, justifying the friction of a subscription.

However, the "better way" analysis reveals a fatal mismatch with current reality. In 2002, broadband penetration is critically low; mandating it excludes the vast majority of the installed base, artificially capping the network effect required for matchmaking to function. Competitors offering free, publisher-led solutions lower the barrier to entry, allowing the ecosystem to grow organically. A mandatory fee on top of a new console purchase creates a double-friction point that historically stifles adoption in nascent markets.

The pre-mortem is clear: the service launches to a tiny user base because few have broadband and fewer will pay an annual fee for unproven utility. Servers sit underutilized while fixed operational costs for hosting and support drain capital. The "shared identity" value proposition collapses without critical mass. The company lacks operational experience in running persistent online services, introducing high execution risk alongside market risk.

While a unified service is a strong long-term vision, launching it as a *mandatory*, *paid*, *broadband-only* requirement in 2002 is a blocker. The evidence suggests the market is not ready for this specific combination of constraints. The plan fails the "cheaper alternative" test: a free or freemium tier, or delaying the mandate until broadband adoption crosses a tipping point, reaches the same upside with significantly less risk of total adoption failure. The current proposal bets the farm on infrastructure and consumer behavior that do not yet exist at the necessary scale.

DECISION: NO-GO
