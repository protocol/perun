The claim is that an asymmetric hardware strategy (dual-screen, touch, lower price) will outperform a symmetric "specs war" against a wealthier rival. The steelman: this approach creates a unique value proposition that expands the total addressable market to non-core users while leveraging existing software libraries via the legacy device, making direct graphical comparison irrelevant.

**Skeptic:** The premise relies on developers accepting fragmented screen real estate and unproven touch inputs. If third-party support fails, the device becomes a gimmick with no software library. However, maintaining the older handheld mitigates this by ensuring a baseline library exists day one, reducing developer risk. The cost risk of two screens is explicitly traded for the strategic advantage of a lower final retail price; if the BOM allows the target price, the math holds.

**Better-way:** Matching the rival's graphics requires a R&D budget this company may not have and cedes the "innovation" narrative. Doing nothing guarantees slow erosion as the rival captures the media-centric user. The proposed path is the only option that changes the competitive axis from "power" (where the rival wins) to "interaction" (where the field is open).

**Kill-criteria & Pre-mortem:** Failure occurs if the price parity isn't met or if the touch interface is treated as a secondary novelty rather than a core mechanic. The "gimmick" risk is real but is the necessary cost of differentiation. The proposal already acknowledges the risks; the mitigation (keeping the old system) is built-in. There is no cheaper alternative that offers similar defensive moats. The cost of delay is losing the holiday season window to the rival's established momentum.

No factual blocker exists in the text. The risks are execution-based, not strategic flaws. The plan forces the rival to compete on a new front where their graphical advantage is neutralized.
