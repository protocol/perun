The proposal describes entering a saturated market against a dominant incumbent with a loyal ecosystem, while actively dismantling the company's existing industry alliances by dropping the copy-protection standard partners rely on. The "better way" analysis reveals a fatal flaw: the plan attempts to compete on features (screen size, wireless sharing) that the incumbent can easily replicate, while simultaneously alienating the record labels required to stock the store. Without a verified licensing strategy or a mechanism to overcome the "late to market" disadvantage, the large marketing budget becomes a sunk cost burning cash to acquire users who have no reason to switch.

The pre-mortem is clear: six months post-launch, the device has niche appeal, the store lacks key catalog due to label friction over the removed copy protection, and the company is trapped in a price war it cannot win. The "do nothing" option preserves current partner relationships; this plan destroys them for uncertain gain. The claim that a larger screen and wireless sharing constitute a defensible moat is unsupported; these are table stakes, not differentiators capable of breaking an ecosystem lock-in.

The strongest attack is the contradiction between needing label support for the store and unilaterally removing the copy-protection standard those labels demanded. This is not a risk; it is a structural blocker. No kill criteria can save a strategy built on antagonizing the supply chain while offering no unique value to the consumer beyond what the leader already provides or can quickly copy. The proposal fails the "must beat do nothing" test because the downside includes permanent reputational damage with content partners.

To proceed would be to gamble significant capital on a me-too product with a hostile supply chain strategy. The evidence provided suggests the plan relies on hope rather than a verified path to displace the incumbent.

DECISION: NO-GO
