The claim is that a pre-revenue startup should commit $1B to fixed infrastructure before validating unit economics. The steelman: rapid infrastructure deployment creates an unassailable moat by locking in density and speed before competitors can react.

However, this fails the "do nothing" and "cheaper alternative" tests. The cheapest path is operating out of existing retail space or small manual hubs to prove customers actually want 30-minute grocery delivery at a sustainable margin. The proposal ignores the "low-margin" reality: groceries tolerate almost no waste. Highly automated warehouses are inflexible; if demand patterns shift or the 30-minute promise proves too costly to fulfill, the $1B becomes stranded assets.

The pre-mortem is clear: the company runs out of cash because the fixed costs of a dozen automated warehouses crush the thin margins before customer density in any single city reaches profitability. This is a one-way door; once built, these warehouses cannot be easily repurposed or scaled down. The cost of delay is negligible compared to the risk of building the wrong system for an unproven market.

The strongest attack is the lack of operating data. Building for "over a dozen" areas assumes the model works in the first, which is explicitly unproven. A blocker exists: the plan commits irreversible capital based on assumptions rather than verified unit economics. The alternative—scaling manually in one city until margins are positive—reaches the same upside (market dominance) with significantly less risk and capital exposure.

This violates the principle of staging investment. A capped, time-boxed pilot in the first city is the only logical first step. Committing $1B now is not strategy; it is gambling with venture capital on unverified hypotheses. The kill criterion is simple: do not scale infrastructure until the first city proves profitable unit economics. Since that data does not exist, the proposal must be rejected.

DECISION: NO-GO
