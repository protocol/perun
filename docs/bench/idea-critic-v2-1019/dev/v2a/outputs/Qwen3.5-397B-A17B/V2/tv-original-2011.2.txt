Steelman: Leveraging proprietary viewing data to bypass the pilot model and commission a high-profile original series outright could secure exclusive content, differentiate the brand from licensors, and lock in talent before competitors, transforming the service from a distributor to a studio.

However, the claim fails the blocker test. The proposal commits to **two full seasons (26 episodes)** before a single frame is shot. While skipping a pilot is a calculated risk supported by data, ordering 26 episodes of an unproven format (straight-to-series prestige drama) from an online-only service is financially reckless. No online service has ever produced prestige scripted content; the audience behavior for binge-watching long-form drama is unverified. The "viewing data" likely reflects preferences for licensed content, not necessarily a willingness to engage with original, exclusive narratives from this specific platform.

The cost represents a "large share" of the content budget. Committing that capital to 26 episodes removes all flexibility. If the first few episodes fail to resonate, the company is contractually bound to fund the remainder of the series, potentially sinking the entire content strategy on one unproven gamble. The standard industry approach (pilot, then season order) or a reduced commitment (one season) exists specifically to mitigate this exact "uncertain audience" risk. Ordering two seasons upfront offers no marginal upside in talent acquisition compared to a single-season guarantee but introduces catastrophic downside risk.

The "all-at-once" release model adds further uncertainty regarding cultural impact and subscription retention curves, which are vital for a subscription service. Without a pilot or a phased rollout, there is no off-ramp. The data supports original content, but it does not support a 26-episode blind commitment. The cheaper, safer alternative is a one-season order with a pilot or a tight script review gate. As proposed, the plan ignores the fundamental principle of staged investment in high-uncertainty ventures.

DECISION: NO-GO
