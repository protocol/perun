The claim is that a non-hardware bookseller should launch a subsidized e-reader with free cellular data to dominate the emerging digital market. The steelman: only a seamless, always-on device with frictionless purchasing can unlock mass adoption where prior dedicated readers failed, and owning the hardware is the only way to secure publisher cooperation against piracy while protecting print margins through controlled pricing.

Alternatives include licensing software to existing hardware makers (low cost, low control, high piracy risk) or waiting for the market to mature (ceding first-mover advantage). Doing nothing guarantees irrelevance if digital shifts rapidly.

However, critical blockers exist. First, the company has zero experience in consumer hardware design or supply chain management; failure here risks brand reputation and massive capital loss. Second, the business model relies on subsidizing both the device and the cellular connectivity, betting entirely on long-term book sales volume to recoup costs. If adoption lags or publishers refuse the low-price tier due to margin fears, the company bleeds cash with no exit ramp. Third, earlier e-readers failed not just due to lack of connectivity, but due to limited content and poor UX; solving connectivity alone may not overcome the "nobody buys these" inertia.

The pre-mortem: The device launches late and over budget. Publishers, unconvinced by the low-price promise, withhold key titles. Users find the catalog thin compared to pirated alternatives available on emerging multifunction devices. The cellular subsidy becomes a fixed cost drain as hardware sales miss targets. Print sales cannibalization occurs without sufficient digital replacement revenue.

While the strategic fit for a bookseller is logical, the execution risk is extreme. The proposal lacks a staged approach, such as a limited pilot or a partnership to mitigate hardware inexperience. It commits to a full-scale launch with unproven assumptions about publisher buy-in and user willingness to pay for convenience over price. The "free cellular" model creates a high fixed-cost structure before demand is proven. Without a verified path to hardware competence or publisher commitments, this is a high-stakes gamble that violates the principle of testing core assumptions before full commitment.

DECISION: NO-GO
