The claim is that committing to a massive, unproven wide-body jet will secure market dominance and lower costs despite existential financial risks. The steelman: this specific design (raised cockpit, high-bypass engines) creates a dual-use asset (passenger/freight) that locks in the largest customer and preempts competitors, justifying the unprecedented capital outlay.

However, the skeptic identifies fatal blockers in the current facts. First, the financial exposure ("far larger than any... made") combined with "only one airline has ordered" creates a single-point failure; if that customer cancels or the market shifts, the company faces insolvency. Second, the technological risk is compounded: an unproven engine paired with a speculative airframe size lacks a safety margin. Third, the strategic timing is suspect; if "supersonic airliners are expected to replace subsonic jets," investing heavily in a larger subsonic vessel may be building obsolescence into the product before launch. The "do nothing" or "wait" alternative preserves capital until supersonic viability is settled or more orders are secured.

The pre-mortem is clear: the company exhausts its liquidity building the factory and developing the engine, only for the supersonic transition to accelerate or the sole customer to defect, leaving the firm with a useless asset and no cash. The cost of delay is lower than the cost of total failure. A "go" decision requires multiple binding orders to share the risk and proof that supersonic replacement is not imminent. Neither exists in the proposal. The risk profile is not merely high; it is asymmetric with unlimited downside and uncertain upside based on a single data point.

This fails the blocker test: the financial scale relative to confirmed demand (1 vs. massive outlay) and the looming technological discontinuity (supersonic) are facts already in hand that contradict the safety of the proposal.

DECISION: NO-GO
