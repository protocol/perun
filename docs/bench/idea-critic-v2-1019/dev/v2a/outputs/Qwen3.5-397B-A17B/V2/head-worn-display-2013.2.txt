The claim is that a 2013 head-worn display with a camera, priced at $1,500 and marketed as an everyday consumer product, will succeed via an invitation-only early adopter program. The steelman posits that exclusivity and technological novelty will overcome privacy concerns, high cost, and lack of clear utility to establish a new mass-market category.

However, the proposal fails the blocker test against its own stated facts. The "everyday consumer" positioning directly contradicts the identified risks: no clear everyday use for most people, active privacy objections to a wearable camera, and social acceptance issues. A product lacking a defined daily utility cannot function as an "everyday" device, regardless of early adopter enthusiasm. The $1,500 price point restricts the market to enthusiasts, yet the strategy aims for general public scalability immediately following this narrow window. This creates a distribution cliff where the product cannot transition from a niche toy to a mass-market tool because the core value proposition (everyday utility) remains unproven and actively contested by social norms.

The "better way" analysis suggests a focused enterprise or vertical-specific launch (e.g., medical, manufacturing) where the camera and hands-free operation solve explicit, high-value problems, justifying the cost and bypassing public privacy backlash. Alternatively, a "do nothing" approach regarding the general public launch avoids brand damage from a failed mass-market rollout. The pre-mortem is clear: the device becomes a social pariah ("Glassholes"), privacy regulations tighten in response to public outcry, and the lack of recurring utility leads to high churn after the novelty fades.

The proposal attempts to use an invitation-only program to mask these fundamental product-market fit gaps, but exclusivity does not create utility where none exists. The risks are not merely execution hurdles; they are fundamental contradictions to the "everyday consumer" thesis. Without a pivot to a specific, high-utility vertical or a resolution to the privacy/social acceptance deadlock, launching as proposed guarantees a high-visibility failure that could poison the well for future iterations. The evidence provided (no clear use, privacy objections, social friction) constitutes a named blocker that disproves the core claim of mass-market viability.

DECISION: NO-GO
