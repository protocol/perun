The proposal fails the "beat do nothing" test. The claim is that a carrier-locked, app-less smartphone on an unproven OS will succeed with teenagers in 2010. The steelman argues this leverages the acquired brand's messaging popularity to bypass app dependency. However, the attack reveals fatal contradictions: teenagers, the target demographic, are defined by social network engagement which, in 2010, increasingly relies on third-party applications (games, media, emerging platforms) unavailable without an app store. 

Comparing alternatives, "do nothing" preserves capital. A cheaper alternative exists: port the acquired messaging software to existing platforms (iOS, Android, BlackBerry) rather than building a new OS and hardware from scratch. This captures the user base without the massive overhead of a carrier-exclusive hardware play. The current plan incurs high fixed costs (data plan, hardware R&D) while removing the primary value driver for teens (apps).

The pre-mortem is clear: six months post-launch, sales stall because parents refuse the high monthly data cost for a device that cannot run the games or tools their children use on friends' phones. The "sunk cost" of two years development is a trap; continuing guarantees further loss. The lack of an app store is not a minor feature gap; it is a category error for a "social-networking phone" in this era. The evidence provided (rival catalogs, high cost, new OS) directly contradicts the core value proposition. There is no path to the stated goal (capturing the teen social market) with these constraints. The plan commits real money to a strategy that ignores the ecosystem reality of the target user.

DECISION: NO-GO
