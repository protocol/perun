The proposal attempts to dislodge a dominant incumbent by entering a mature market with a late, higher-cost hardware device while simultaneously alienating existing industry partners. The core strategy relies on dropping the copy-protection standard previously championed by the company. This creates an immediate, fatal contradiction: record labels, who control the inventory required for the store to function, are explicitly noted as likely to restrict sharing. Without label cooperation, the "music store" and "subscription" components cannot launch with sufficient content to compete against the leader's entrenched ecosystem.

The "better way" analysis reveals that "doing nothing" or licensing the existing dominant platform yields higher immediate value with zero capital risk. The proposed path requires massive upfront investment in hardware manufacturing, retail distribution, and marketing to overcome network effects, despite having no price advantage and a smaller initial catalog. The risk of labels restricting sharing is not a hypothetical "might"; it is a direct consequence of the proposed architectural shift away from the industry-standard protection they currently rely on.

The pre-mortem is clear: the device launches with a limited library due to label boycotts, the marketing budget burns without converting users from the loyal competitor ecosystem, and the company is left with unsold inventory and broken partner relationships. The "wireless sharing" feature, intended as a differentiator, becomes the primary blocker for content acquisition. There is no evidence presented that labels will abandon the incumbent's protection scheme for a new entrant with zero market share. The plan fails the basic viability test of securing supply before demanding demand.

This is a one-way door with high capital expenditure and a high probability of total loss. The assumption that content providers will follow the platform into a new, less secure environment without a massive existing user base is unsupported by the stated facts. The proposal does not beat "do nothing" on cost, risk, or reversibility.

DECISION: NO-GO
