The proposal describes a "blitzscaling" strategy reliant on perpetual capital availability and negative unit economics to purchase market share. The steelman is that speed creates an unassailable monopoly before competitors can react, allowing future price hikes to recoup losses.

However, the attack reveals fatal flaws in the current context (early 2000). First, the plan requires selling products below cost while simultaneously incurring massive fixed costs (warehouses) and variable costs (heavy shipping, national TV ads). This creates a cash-burn rate that likely exceeds any plausible IPO proceeds, especially given the warning that capital markets could close. If the IPO window shuts due to market volatility or poor performance of similar tech firms, the company faces immediate insolvency because its operations are fundamentally unprofitable on a per-unit basis.

Second, the strategy assumes competitors will not match price cuts or that customers will remain loyal once prices rise. In online retail, switching costs are near zero; a "race to the bottom" on price without a differentiated moat simply destroys value for all players without guaranteeing dominance. Buying the largest competitor adds integration risk and debt without solving the core issue: the business model loses money on every shipment.

The "do nothing" or "slow growth" alternative focuses on fixing unit economics before scaling. A cheaper path is to target niche segments with positive margins, optimize logistics for lighter goods, and use targeted digital marketing rather than expensive national TV spots. The proposal fails the blocker test: the combination of negative gross margins, high fixed-cost expansion, and reliance on volatile public markets creates a near-certain probability of total capital depletion before profitability is reached. The risks are not merely execution difficulties; they are structural contradictions in the financial model.

Waiting allows the company to prove a viable unit economic model privately, reducing the risk of a catastrophic public failure. The cost of delay is lower than the cost of a failed IPO and subsequent bankruptcy.

DECISION: NO-GO
