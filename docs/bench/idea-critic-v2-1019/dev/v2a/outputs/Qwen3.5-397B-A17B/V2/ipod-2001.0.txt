The claim is that a niche computer manufacturer should enter the crowded, low-margin portable music market with a premium, incompatible device despite lacking consumer electronics experience or legal content pipelines.

Steelman: A high-capacity, seamless ecosystem play could redefine the category if integration outweighs platform scarcity. However, the proposal fails the "fit" and "evidence" gates. The addressable market is critically small: only owners of a minority-share computer platform can use it, excluding the vast majority of potential buyers who use Windows. In early 2001, this restricts the total addressable market to a fraction of a percent of music listeners.

Cheaper alternatives exist but have "not sold well," indicating weak category demand or poor user experience. Entering with a higher price point ($400 vs. competitors) without a proven demand signal is financially reckless. The lack of licensed legal downloads is a fatal blocker; hardware without a legitimate, easy content source creates friction that kills adoption. The "better way" is to wait for legal licensing frameworks to mature or license software to Windows first to expand the base.

Pre-mortem: The product launches, sells poorly due to incompatibility and high price, inventory piles up, and the brand dilutes its core computer focus without gaining traction. The one-way door is the sunk cost of custom hardware tooling and supply chain commitments for a non-core competency.

Blocker: The proposal explicitly limits compatibility to a small-share computer ecosystem while pricing above established competitors, with no legal content pipeline. This contradicts the basic requirement for mass-market consumer electronics: accessibility and content availability. The math does not work; the niche is too small to sustain the R&D and marketing spend required to educate the market on a premium device.

DECISION: NO-GO
