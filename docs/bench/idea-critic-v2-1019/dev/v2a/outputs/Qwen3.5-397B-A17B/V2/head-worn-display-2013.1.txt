The claim is that a $1,500 head-worn camera device, launched via invitation-only in 2013, will succeed as a mainstream everyday consumer product. The steelman posits that exclusivity will drive hype among tech early adopters, masking initial utility gaps while the ecosystem matures.

However, the "better-way" analysis reveals a fatal flaw in the sequencing. The proposal attempts to skip the essential "prosumer/enterprise" validation phase where use-cases are proven before facing public scrutiny. By positioning it immediately as an "everyday consumer product," the plan invites maximum resistance on privacy and social acceptance without having established a non-negotiable utility value to offset those costs. The $1,500 price point restricts the user base to those who can afford a novelty, yet the "everyday" branding demands mass-market social permission, which a visible camera actively destroys in 2013.

The pre-mortem is clear: The device launches, privacy advocates label it a surveillance tool ("Glassholes"), social venues ban it, and the lack of a "killer app" for the average person causes churn once the novelty fades. The battery life and high cost compound the friction. The "do nothing" or "enterprise-first" alternative is superior: deploy to specific industrial or medical verticals where the camera provides documented ROI, normalizing the form factor and subsidizing R&D before risking consumer brand equity.

The strongest attack survived is the argument that "hype can bridge the utility gap." While hype drives initial sales, it accelerates the backlash when the product fails to deliver daily value, leading to a faster collapse than a slow rollout. The proposal's own admitted risks (privacy, social acceptance, no clear use) are not merely hurdles; they are blockers for a *consumer* launch strategy. The plan fails because it treats a prototype-grade limitation set as a mass-market feature set.

DECISION: NO-GO
