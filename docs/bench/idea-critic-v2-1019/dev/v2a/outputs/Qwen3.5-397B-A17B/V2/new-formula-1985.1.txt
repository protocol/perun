The claim is that replacing the flagship formula with a sweeter alternative, validated by blind taste tests, will reverse market share losses. The steelman: objective sensory data proves the new formula is superior, and removing the old one forces migration to the better product, maximizing sales.

However, the attack reveals a fatal flaw in the evidence. The blind taste tests isolate flavor from brand identity, measuring only immediate sensory pleasure (sweeter is better in a sip) rather than long-term consumption satisfaction or emotional connection. By withholding the fact that the original would be removed, the research failed to measure the specific risk of consumer backlash against loss of heritage. A sweeter drink winning a sip test does not equate to winning the market when the emotional anchor is severed.

The "do nothing" alternative (keeping the original, perhaps introducing the sweet version as a line extension) preserves the brand equity while capturing the sweet-preference segment. The proposed plan bets the entire century-old brand on a single metric (sip preference) that explicitly excluded the primary variable driving loyalty (heritage and availability). The risk is not merely "emotional attachment"; it is the certainty of alienating the core base without a fallback, turning a product launch into a brand crisis. The testing methodology itself is the blocker: it generated false positives by hiding the consequence of adoption.

No kill criteria or fallback exists in the proposal. If the backlash occurs, there is no immediate path to restore trust because the original is gone. The cost of delay is negligible compared to the cost of irreversible brand damage. The plan fails because its own evidence (the tests) is invalid for the specific decision being made (total replacement).

DECISION: NO-GO
