The claim is that a mandatory, paid, broadband-only online service will succeed despite market norms favoring free, publisher-led models and low broadband penetration. The steelman: a unified, high-quality network creates a defensible moat and superior user experience that justifies a premium, forcing an industry shift.

However, the failure hypothesis is severe. In 2002, broadband adoption is critically low; mandating it excludes the vast majority of the installed base immediately. Competitors offering free online play remove the primary incentive for users to pay extra. The company lacks operational history in hosting and support, introducing high execution risk for a service that must be flawless to justify its cost. If the player base remains fragmented due to cost and connectivity barriers, third-party publishers will not prioritize the platform, creating a death spiral of low content value and low subscription uptake.

Alternatives exist: a hybrid model supporting dial-up, a free tier funded by hardware margins, or deferring the mandatory aspect until broadband penetration reaches a critical mass. The "do nothing" approach (letting publishers handle online) carries zero infrastructure risk and aligns with current user expectations.

The proposal fails the blocker test. The factual constraint of low broadband availability combined with a mandatory paywall directly contradicts the requirement for a critical mass of users to make the service viable. The financial model relies on users accepting a paradigm shift they have shown no inclination to make, while the company assumes operational capabilities it does not possess. The risk of alienating the core user base and stifling software sales outweighs the potential long-term moat. A cheaper, staged alternative (optional service, dial-up support) reaches similar upside with significantly less risk of total adoption failure.

DECISION: NO-GO
