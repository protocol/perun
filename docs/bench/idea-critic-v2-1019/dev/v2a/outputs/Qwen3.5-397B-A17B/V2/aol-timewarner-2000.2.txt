**Steelman:** Merging dominant content with the fastest-growing distribution channel creates an unbeatable ecosystem, locking in subscribers and monetizing legacy media through new digital pipelines before competitors react.

**Attack:** The proposal relies on two fragile premises: that dial-up remains relevant during the broadband transition, and that cable infrastructure is readily available for high-speed delivery. The internet provider's valuation is based on a bubble metric (subscriber growth) that ignores the technological obsolescence of dial-up. If broadband adoption accelerates faster than the cable upgrade cycle, the "distribution" advantage evaporates, leaving the combined entity with an overvalued, shrinking asset controlling a content library it cannot effectively monetize digitally. The cultural clash between a fast-moving, stock-option-driven tech firm and a slow, process-heavy media conglomerate threatens to paralyze integration, preventing the promised cross-promotion. Furthermore, relying on cable partners for network access introduces a dependency that competitors (who own their pipes) do not face.

**Better Way:** Instead of a full merger transferring majority control to the volatile tech entity, the media conglomerate should pursue strategic licensing deals and minority investments in multiple broadband providers. This captures upside exposure without betting the entire legacy valuation on a single, unproven distribution model that may become obsolete within 24 months.

**Kill Criteria:** The deal fails if broadband adoption exceeds 15% of US households within two years while the merged company's dial-up base declines by more than 10% annually. The cultural integration is a failure if key technical leadership turnover exceeds 25% in the first year.

**Verdict:** The proposal exchanges stable, cash-generating assets for a majority stake in a business model facing imminent technological disruption. The valuation premium paid assumes a future where the acquired company's core product (dial-up) is already dying. The risks of cultural implosion and regulatory delay are secondary to the fundamental mismatch: buying a peak-valued sunset technology with sunrise-priced equity.

DECISION: NO-GO
