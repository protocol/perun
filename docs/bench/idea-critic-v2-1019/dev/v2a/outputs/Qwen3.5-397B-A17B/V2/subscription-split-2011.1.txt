The proposal attempts to solve a pricing backlash by fracturing the customer experience. The core failure is treating the symptom (brand association with a price hike) while ignoring the disease (a 60% price increase for combined utility). Splitting the services forces users to manage two accounts, two queues, and two bills, directly amplifying the "customer confusion" risk already identified.

The rationale claims the DVD business is declining and needs different management; however, operational separation does not require brand severance. A single brand can house distinct service tiers without alienating the user base. By creating a new name and separate website, the proposal incurs massive marketing costs to rebuild trust for the DVD unit while simultaneously signaling to streaming users that the legacy physical media side is being discarded.

The loss of cross-service recommendations is a critical strategic error. Viewing habits often span formats; severing data silos reduces the value proposition of both services. The "declining" nature of DVD suggests it should be managed as a cash cow to fund streaming growth, not spun off into a confusing, standalone entity that highlights its own obsolescence.

The strongest attack is that this move looks like a deceptive attempt to hide the price hike rather than a genuine strategic pivot. Customers will perceive two bills as a penalty for wanting both services, likely accelerating churn in both segments. The proposal fails the "do nothing" comparison: maintaining a single brand with clear communication about the distinct cost structures of physical vs. digital delivery is cheaper, less confusing, and preserves the integrated data advantage. The current plan introduces high execution risk and brand fragmentation without solving the underlying pricing dissatisfaction.

DECISION: NO-GO
