The claim is that a data-driven, all-at-once release of two seasons without a pilot will succeed where traditional models fail. The steelman: proprietary viewing data reduces pilot necessity, and binge-release creates cultural velocity that justifies the budget risk.

However, critical blockers exist. First, **data validity**: viewing data reflects consumption of *licensed* content, not appetite for original prestige drama. Users watching sitcoms does not prove they will pay for or watch a specific high-budget drama. This is a category error in evidence. Second, **financial exposure**: committing two full seasons (26 episodes) before seeing audience reaction to episode one violates basic capital allocation for an unproven product line. If the first three episodes fail, 88% of the budget is already sunk with no off-ramp. Third, **distribution unproven**: no online-only service has ever launched prestige scripted content; the technical and behavioral model for "all-at-once" consumption is purely theoretical in 2011.

The "better way" is a single-season order with a pilot or a shorter initial drop to validate the binge model and audience retention before committing to a second season. The proposal's reliance on "viewing data" as a substitute for market testing is an unsupported assumption that conflates different content types. The risk of wasting a large share of the content budget on a potentially unpopular format is too high without a staged approach.

The pre-mortem is clear: the show launches, viewership is modest because the audience wasn't primed for original content, and the company is locked into a second season of a failing asset with no ability to pivot. The cost of delay (waiting for a pilot result) is negligible compared to the cost of failure (sinking 26 episodes of budget).

This proposal fails the blocker test: the evidence (licensed content data) does not support the core claim (original drama success), and the financial structure (two seasons upfront) removes all reversibility.

DECISION: NO-GO
