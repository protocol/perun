The proposal describes a "blitzscale" strategy reliant on perpetual capital availability and negative unit economics to buy market share. The steelman is that speed creates an unassailable monopoly before competitors can react, allowing future price hikes to recoup losses.

However, the attack reveals fatal structural flaws. First, selling products below cost (negative gross margin) while expanding warehouses and buying competitors creates a cash-burn multiplier, not a path to profitability. Every additional sale increases the loss. Second, the plan depends on an IPO in early 2000 to fund a national TV campaign and acquisitions. This timing ignores the imminent dot-com bubble burst; if capital markets close post-IPO, the company cannot sustain the burn rate required by the below-cost pricing strategy. Third, heavy, low-margin items incur high shipping costs, exacerbating the negative margin per unit.

The "better way" analysis shows that "doing nothing" (organic growth) preserves capital, while a staged approach focusing on positive unit economics in limited regions reduces existential risk. The proposed plan fails the "fit" test because it assumes infinite capital access in a sector facing imminent contraction.

The pre-mortem is clear: the IPO proceeds are exhausted within 12 months due to the combined costs of the TV campaign, acquisition premium, warehouse construction, and per-unit losses. With no path to positive cash flow and a freezing credit market, the company faces immediate insolvency. The risks listed (capital markets closing, negative margins) are not mere possibilities; they are mathematical certainties under this specific combination of tactics.

This is a one-way door with a high probability of total loss. The kill criterion is the negative gross margin combined with heavy reliance on external funding for operations rather than infrastructure alone. No amount of market share compensates for losing money on every transaction when the funding tap is about to turn off.

DECISION: NO-GO
