The claim is that a 1999 grocery startup with no operating history should immediately commit $1 billion to build automated warehouses in a dozen cities before validating unit economics.

Steelman: Aggressive infrastructure preemption creates an unassailable moat, securing market dominance before competitors can react, justifying the risk of scaling unproven unit economics.

However, this plan fails the blocker test. The proposal explicitly admits "no operating track record" and "low-margin business" while proposing irreversible capital deployment. In 1999, e-commerce logistics were nascent; assuming automated warehouses will function efficiently without real-world data on order density, picking times, and spoilage rates is a fatal assumption. The "risks raised" (capital intensity, thin margins, density needs) are not merely risks; they are active blockers when combined with zero validation.

Better ways exist: Start with one manual or semi-automated fulfillment center in the initial metro. Validate the 30-minute slot profitability and customer density requirements. Only after proving positive unit economics should expansion begin. A staged approach preserves capital and allows the model to adapt to actual consumer behavior rather than theoretical projections.

Pre-mortem: The company burns through venture capital constructing facilities that are either underutilized due to lack of demand or operationally inefficient due to untested automation. With thin margins, there is no room for error. The cost of delay (waiting to learn) is negligible compared to the cost of failure (bankruptcy from over-expansion).

This is a one-way door decision with catastrophic downside. The lack of evidence that the core mechanism (profitable 30-minute delivery) works constitutes a definitive blocker. No amount of venture capital compensates for a fundamentally unproven business model at this scale. The plan commits real money without a capped, time-boxed first step.

DECISION: NO-GO
