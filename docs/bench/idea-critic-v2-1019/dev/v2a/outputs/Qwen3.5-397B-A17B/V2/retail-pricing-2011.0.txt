The claim is that replacing a coupon-dependent model with everyday low pricing (EDLP) and branded shops will reverse sales declines for a mid-market department store. The steelman: this eliminates margin-eroding promotions and modernizes the experience, mirroring success in electronics retail.

However, the skeptic hat identifies a fatal blocker: the customer base is explicitly "older" and "coupon-driven," with most sales historically occurring at a discount. Unlike electronics buyers who value convenience and specs, this demographic is trained to wait for sales. Removing coupons without a transition risks immediate revenue collapse rather than margin expansion. The "better-way" analysis shows that "do nothing" or a phased pilot in select locations is superior to a chain-wide immediate overhaul. A pilot would validate if the brand equity is strong enough to overcome decades of conditioning.

The pre-mortem reveals a high probability of failure: loyal customers defect immediately upon seeing full prices, new "branded shop" shoppers do not arrive fast enough to replace them, and the chain runs out of cash before the turnaround takes hold. The cost of delay (waiting for pilot results) is negligible compared to the risk of a total system crash. The proposal ignores the fundamental difference between selling branded electronics (where the manufacturer drives demand) and soft goods (where price sensitivity is higher).

The strongest attack survived is the leadership's prior success; however, that success was in a different category with different consumer psychology. Applying it here without verification is an unsupported assumption. The lack of a pilot for a strategy that contradicts the core historical behavior of the customer base constitutes a concrete failure scenario: a sharp, unrecoverable drop in transaction volume.

This is a one-way door with irreversible consequences if executed chain-wide immediately. The evidence provided (historical reliance on discounts) directly contradicts the core mechanism of the proposed solution (removing discounts).

DECISION: NO-GO
