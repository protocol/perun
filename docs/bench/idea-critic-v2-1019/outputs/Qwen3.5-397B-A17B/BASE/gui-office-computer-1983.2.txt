The proposal to launch a graphical business computer at $10,000 in 1983 presents critical flaws that cannot be ignored. The primary obstacle is the price point, which is several times higher than leading business PCs currently available. Office workers and their employers are unlikely to justify such a massive premium without a corresponding, undeniable leap in productivity, which this machine fails to demonstrate.

Compounding the pricing issue is the system's performance. The proposal admits the machine runs slowly. In a business environment where efficiency is paramount, a slow computer hinders rather than helps workflow. When combined with a scarcity of third-party software, the value proposition collapses. Businesses rely on a robust ecosystem of applications to handle accounting, word processing, and data management. A platform with little software support forces companies to operate in isolation, rendering the advanced graphical interface and mouse less useful.

Furthermore, the internal risk is significant. A cheaper machine with a similar interface is already in development within the same company. Launching the expensive, slower model now would likely cannibalize future sales of the more viable, cost-effective internal alternative. It would also damage the brand's reputation for delivering practical business solutions if customers purchase the high-priced unit only to see a superior, cheaper version arrive shortly after.

The market conditions of 1983 favor affordability and compatibility over experimental interfaces that lack software support and speed. Proceeding with this launch would likely result in poor adoption, financial loss, and strategic confusion. The product does not meet the basic requirements for a successful business tool in this economic climate. The existence of a better, cheaper internal alternative makes immediate approval of this specific configuration illogical. Resources should be redirected to accelerate the cheaper model or significantly reengineer this one to lower costs and improve speed before any market introduction.

DECISION: NO-GO
