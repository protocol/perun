The proposal to launch a graphical business computer at $10,000 in 1983 presents immediate and critical vulnerabilities. The primary obstacle is the price point, which is several times higher than leading business PCs currently available. Office workers, the target demographic, operate under strict budget constraints and will unlikely justify such a premium without overwhelming justification.

Furthermore, the machine runs slowly, directly undermining productivity claims essential for business adoption. A slow system frustrates users and reduces efficiency, making it a hard sell regardless of interface innovation. Compounding this issue is the lack of third-party software. Business environments rely on a robust ecosystem of applications to handle accounting, word processing, and data management. Without this support, the hardware is functionally isolated and practically useless for daily operations.

Perhaps most damaging is the internal threat: a cheaper machine with a similar interface is already in development within the same company. Launching the expensive, slower model now would cannibalize future sales of the superior, cost-effective alternative. It would also brand the company as out of touch with market realities, potentially damaging reputation before the better product arrives.

Proceeding with this launch ignores basic economic principles of value proposition. The combination of exorbitant cost, poor performance, software scarcity, and internal competition creates a scenario where failure is nearly guaranteed. Resources allocated to this launch would be better spent accelerating the development and marketing of the cheaper, internal alternative. To approve this proposal now would be to willingly enter the market with a product that is objectively inferior to both external competitors and upcoming internal solutions. The risks vastly outweigh any potential first-mover advantage gained by the graphical interface, especially when that same interface will soon be available at a much lower price point.

DECISION: NO-GO
