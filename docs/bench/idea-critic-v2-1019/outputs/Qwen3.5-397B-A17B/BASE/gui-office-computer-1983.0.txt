The proposal to launch a graphical business computer at $10,000 in 1983 presents immediate and critical barriers to success. The primary concern is the pricing strategy; at several times the cost of leading business PCs, the device places itself out of reach for the vast majority of target office workers. In a market driven by cost-efficiency and proven utility, such a premium requires undeniable superiority, which this product lacks.

Furthermore, the admitted performance issues directly contradict the needs of business environments where speed and reliability are paramount. A slow machine hampers productivity, making it an unattractive investment regardless of its interface innovations. This weakness is compounded by the scarcity of third-party software. Without a robust ecosystem of applications, the hardware offers limited functional value, rendering the advanced graphical interface and mouse peripherals as novel features rather than essential business tools.

Perhaps the most decisive factor is the internal competition. The existence of a cheaper machine with a similar interface currently in development within the same company creates a clear conflict. Launching the expensive, slower model now would likely cannibalize resources and market attention, only to be undercut shortly by the company's own superior, cost-effective alternative. This would result in significant financial loss and brand confusion.

Given the excessive price point, poor performance, lack of software support, and the imminent arrival of a better internal alternative, proceeding with this launch as proposed is strategically unsound. The risks far outweigh the potential benefits, and the product is not ready to compete effectively in the current landscape. Resources should be redirected toward the developing cheaper model or used to address the fundamental flaws of this unit before any market introduction is considered.

DECISION: NO-GO
