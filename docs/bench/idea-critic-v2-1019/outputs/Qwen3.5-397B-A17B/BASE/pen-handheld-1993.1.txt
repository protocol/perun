The proposal to launch a pen-based handheld computer at $700 in 1993 presents critical flaws that threaten immediate commercial failure. The core technology, handwriting recognition, is explicitly stated as unreliable during testing. Releasing a product where the primary input method fails undermines the fundamental user experience, rendering the device frustrating and practically unusable for its intended purpose.

Furthermore, the price point of $700 is prohibitively high for an unproven category with little existing software support. Without a robust ecosystem of applications, the value proposition cannot justify the cost to consumers. The physical design also poses a significant barrier; being too large for a pocket contradicts the essential requirement for a "handheld" or personal digital assistant, which demands portability and convenience.

The strategy relies heavily on publicity by the chief executive rather than product viability. While leadership endorsement can generate initial interest, it cannot sustain a product that fails to perform its basic functions reliably. The combination of an unproven market category, unreliable core technology, excessive size, high price, and a lack of software creates a perfect storm for rejection by the marketplace.

Proceeding now would likely result in significant financial loss and reputational damage. The risks identified are not minor hurdles but fundamental defects in the product concept and execution plan. The device does not yet meet the minimum standards for functionality, portability, or value required to succeed. Launching under these conditions ignores the clear warnings from testing and market realities. The proposal requires substantial revision to address the reliability of the input method, reduce the form factor, lower the cost, and encourage software development before any launch can be considered. Therefore, approval cannot be granted in its current state.

DECISION: NO-GO
