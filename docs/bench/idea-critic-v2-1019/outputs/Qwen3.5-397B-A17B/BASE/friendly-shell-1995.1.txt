The proposal suggests selling a cartoon-house interface for $100 to help new users, but critical flaws make immediate approval impossible. First, the hardware requirement is a major barrier; most home PCs in 1995 lack the necessary memory, severely limiting the addressable market to a tiny fraction of owners with high-end machines. Second, the timing is disastrous. With a major new operating system release featuring a native interface update arriving within months, launching a separate $100 shell now invites immediate obsolescence. Consumers will likely wait for the upcoming OS upgrade rather than pay for a temporary add-on that may become incompatible or redundant.

Furthermore, the product risks alienating experienced users who may find the cartoon aesthetic patronizing, while failing to secure a broad user base due to the lack of third-party add-ons. Without an ecosystem of compatible software, the utility of this "house" is restricted to basic functions, reducing its value proposition. The combination of high hardware barriers, imminent competition from the company's own core product, and a lack of software support creates a high probability of commercial failure. Releasing this now would waste resources on a product destined to be overshadowed by the impending OS launch. The strategic move is to halt this specific standalone product immediately and integrate any useful accessibility concepts directly into the upcoming major operating system release instead.

DECISION: NO-GO
