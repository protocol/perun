The claim is that switching to the dominant processor architecture with a translation layer is the only viable path to competitive thin laptops. The steelman is that this move secures performance parity and supply chain relevance, with the translation layer acting as a bridge to preserve the software ecosystem during the transition.

However, the proposal fails the blocker test. The risks listed are not merely potential downsides; they are structural contradictions to the proposal's success criteria. First, the admission that "translated software will run slower" directly conflicts with the primary goal of delivering "cool and efficient" performance for thin laptops. If the core user experience (speed) is degraded by the very mechanism intended to save the product line, the value proposition collapses. Second, the risk that "developers must rebuild their software" combined with "announcing early may freeze sales" creates a deadly valley: revenue drops immediately upon announcement, while the new platform lacks native applications to drive recovery. The company's history of a "painful" earlier transition suggests an organizational inability to execute this specific type of complex migration without severe brand damage.

The proposal offers no evidence that the translation layer can overcome the performance deficit sufficiently to satisfy the "thin and efficient" requirement. Without proof that the performance penalty is negligible, the plan guarantees a product that is objectively worse than competitors on the key metric of speed, while simultaneously alienating the developer base. The alternative of "doing nothing" or seeking a different supplier (even if currently lagging) avoids the immediate sales freeze and the certainty of performance degradation via emulation. The plan as stated ensures a loss of distinctiveness and a likely failure to meet the thermal/efficiency goals due to the overhead of translation.

Blockers: The proposal admits the solution (translation) degrades the primary value driver (performance/speed) and triggers immediate revenue loss (sales freeze) without a guaranteed native app pipeline.
Risks: Brand dilution, repeat of past painful transition, developer abandonment.

DECISION: NO-GO
