**Steelman:** A dedicated, always-listening home speaker creates a new "ambient computing" category, leveraging existing retail relationships to recover from a failed phone launch by offering high utility (music, shopping) at a disruptive member price ($99), using exclusivity to manage early adoption risks.

**Attack & Alternatives:**
The primary blocker is the **privacy paradox**: in 2014, consumers are highly skeptical of always-on microphones following recent surveillance revelations. Unlike a phone (which is personal and often muted), a home device implies constant surveillance. Voice recognition in 2014 is error-prone outside controlled environments; frequent failures in a static home setting will cause rapid abandonment. The "no existing category" risk is double-edged: it offers first-mover advantage but requires educating the market from zero, a costly endeavor for a company with recent hardware credibility damage.

Alternatives include:
1.  **Do Nothing:** Preserve capital and brand reputation; wait for the market to mature (as competitors eventually did).
2.  **Software-First:** License the assistant to existing speaker manufacturers to validate demand without hardware risk.
3.  **Hybrid Push-to-Talk:** Launch a high-fidelity speaker requiring manual activation, removing the privacy blocker but reducing convenience.

**Pre-mortem:** The product launches to tech enthusiast curiosity but fails to reach mainstream living rooms. Early reviews highlight accidental activations and privacy fears. The $99 member price cannibalizes higher-margin electronics without driving sufficient incremental retail goods sales. The "invitation only" strategy creates artificial scarcity that looks like weak demand rather than exclusivity. The project is killed after two quarters due to high return rates and negligible impact on core retail revenue.

**Verdict:**
The proposal relies on unproven consumer trust in 2014 regarding always-on audio and assumes voice accuracy is sufficient for daily reliance. The company's recent hardware failure suggests execution risk is high. The privacy concern is a fundamental market barrier, not just a feature risk. While the pricing strategy is aggressive, it cannot overcome the lack of category definition and the "creepy" factor of the core mechanism at this specific point in time. The safer path is to license the technology or wait for the market to be educated by others first.

DECISION: NO-GO
