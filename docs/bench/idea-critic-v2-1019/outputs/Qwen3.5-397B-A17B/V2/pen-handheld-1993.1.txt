The claim is that a $700 pen-based PDA with heavy CEO-led publicity will successfully define a new handheld category despite unproven technology and market fit.

Steelman: Only a bold, integrated hardware-software launch with top-down visibility can jumpstart an ecosystem before competitors lock in keyboards or styluses as secondary inputs.

However, the blocker test fails immediately on verified facts provided. The proposal admits handwriting recognition is "unreliable in testing." For a device where this is the *main* input, unreliable core functionality is a fatal defect, not a risk. Users cannot operate a computer that cannot read their writing; no amount of marketing fixes a broken primary interface.

Furthermore, the price ($700) is high, the form factor is too large for a pocket, and there is little software. These are not merely risks; they are compounding barriers to adoption for an unproven category. A "do nothing" or "wait" alternative is superior: wait until recognition reliability is proven, reduce size, and cultivate a software base before a costly, reputation-damaging launch.

The pre-mortem is clear: The device launches, users encounter constant recognition errors, the high price amplifies their frustration, the bulky size prevents daily carry, and the lack of software offers no reason to persevere. The category becomes synonymous with failure, delaying legitimate handheld adoption by years. The cost of delay (waiting for tech maturity) is far lower than the cost of launching a broken product that destroys brand trust and validates competitor skepticism.

There is no path where a device with unreliable main input, high cost, poor portability, and no apps succeeds simply because of CEO publicity. The evidence provided contradicts the core claim that this specific configuration will lead a new category.

Blockers: Unreliable main input (handwriting); High price + poor portability + no software for an unproven category.
Risks: Category rejection; Brand damage; Competitor advantage.

DECISION: NO-GO
