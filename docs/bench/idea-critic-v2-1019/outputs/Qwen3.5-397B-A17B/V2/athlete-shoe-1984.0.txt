The claim is that signing an unproven rookie to a record-breaking, rule-breaking deal will reverse market share losses in basketball. The steelman: only a discontinuous shock can dislodge entrenched incumbents; a high-risk, high-reward bet on a singular talent creates a new category rather than competing in an old one.

Alternatives include signing established stars (proven but expensive, low differentiation), incremental marketing (safe, unlikely to move the needle), or exiting the category (cuts losses, cedes ground). Doing nothing guarantees continued erosion. This proposal beats "do nothing" only if the player's cultural impact transcends athletic performance.

Fit check: The goal is leadership, not participation. A safe strategy fails the north star. However, the plan relies on unverified assumptions: that the league will allow the rule-breaking colorways to generate controversy rather than banning the player entirely, and that the rookie's style will resonate culturally regardless of team success.

Pre-mortem: The player suffers a career-ending injury in year one, leaving the company with dead inventory and no ROI. The league bans the shoes immediately, removing the marketing hook. The player underperforms, and the "signature" line becomes a clearance rack fixture. This is a one-way door with massive financial exposure; the cost of delay is losing the specific cultural moment, but the cost of error is existential for the division.

Evidence is entirely prospective; no sales data exists for this specific strategy. The "blocker" test reveals a critical flaw in the proposal as stated: it commits to a five-year term before a single minute of professional play is recorded. Without a performance clause or injury protection, the financial risk is not a calculated bet but a gamble. The proposal lacks kill criteria for the endorsement if the player fails to meet minimum playtime or cultural engagement metrics.

While the strategic logic of disruption is sound, the specific terms (five-year guaranteed deal for an unproven asset) create an unacceptable downside without defined off-ramps. The plan must be revised to include performance-based escalators and injury clauses before approval.

DECISION: NO-GO
