The proposal to launch four months early in May 1995 at $399 fails the blocker test. The primary failure mode is a self-inflicted ecosystem collapse. By shipping with "few games ready" and excluding major retailers, the console launches with no software library and no distribution network. In the video game industry, hardware is a loss leader; profit and retention depend entirely on software sales. A barren library at launch guarantees poor attach rates, signaling to developers that the platform is dying before it begins.

The price point creates a second blocker. At $100 above the competitor's announced price, the value proposition is negative. Consumers will not pay a premium for a machine that is harder to program for 3D, has fewer games, and is unavailable at their local stores. The "early mover" advantage is nullified when the product is demonstrably inferior in content, availability, and cost.

The pre-mortem is clear: Launching in May results in immediate market rejection. Retailers excluded from the initial shipment will prioritize the competitor's September launch, permanently freezing the console out of key accounts. Developers, blindsided by the date change, will miss the window entirely, leaving the console with a permanent software drought. The company burns its marketing budget on a failed launch, leaving no resources to recover by September.

The "cheaper" alternative is to wait for September. This allows time to secure a robust launch lineup, negotiate full retail distribution, and potentially adjust pricing to match or undercut the competitor. The cost of delay is zero compared to the cost of a catastrophic failure that brands the console as dead on arrival. There is no scenario where launching an incomplete, overpriced product to a hostile retail channel succeeds. The risks are not theoretical; they are immediate consequences of the proposed constraints.

DECISION: NO-GO
