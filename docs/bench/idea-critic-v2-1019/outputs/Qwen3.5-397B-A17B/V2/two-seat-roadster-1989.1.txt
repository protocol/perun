The claim rests on the assumption that "affordable" and "lightweight" can coexist with modern safety regulations without eroding margins or performance. The steelman is that a stripped-back, focused driver's car can revive a dormant niche by ignoring mass-market feature bloat, targeting enthusiasts underserved by heavy, expensive grand tourers.

However, the "better-way" analysis reveals a critical flaw: the proposal ignores the "do nothing" baseline where the segment shrank precisely because regulatory costs made low-volume convertibles unprofitable for mid-size makers. Without existing sports-car heritage, the company lacks the supply chain for lightweight chassis components, forcing expensive new tooling. The "safety rules" risk is not merely a hurdle but a potential blocker; complying with rollover and crash standards often adds weight that contradicts the "lightweight" core value proposition, creating a design contradiction.

The pre-mortem suggests failure occurs when the car launches too heavy and too expensive to be "affordable," alienating the pure enthusiasts while failing to attract mass-market buyers who need practicality. The cost of delay is low; the niche has been dead for years, waiting another quarter for a proper business case causes no lost opportunity.

Crucially, the proposal lacks evidence of current demand versus nostalgic sentiment. Enthusiasts talk, but do they buy? The sales fade risk is high if the initial burst of early adopters isn't sustained by broader appeal, which the two-seat limitation prevents. A cheaper alternative exists: license an existing platform or partner with a specialist manufacturer rather than launching a clean-sheet program with no heritage.

The strongest attack is the economic mismatch: a mid-size maker cannot achieve the economies of scale needed to make a safe, compliant, low-volume roadster "affordable" without subsidizing it heavily, violating the profit motive. The proposal assumes a market gap exists solely because competitors left, ignoring that they likely left because the unit economics broke. Without a verified path to profitability that accounts for regulatory weight penalties, this is a vanity project.

DECISION: NO-GO
