The proposal fails the steelman test: it asks price-sensitive youth to pay smartphone-level data costs for a device explicitly stripped of the primary value drivers for that demographic (apps and games). The "better way" is evident: leverage the company's upcoming main platform launch to integrate social features as a software layer on existing hardware, avoiding the capital expense of a dedicated device and the friction of a mandatory high-cost data plan.

The pre-mortem writes itself. Six months post-launch, adoption is near zero. Why? Parents refused the expensive data contract for a "dumb" social phone, while teens demanded the games and third-party apps explicitly banned by this architecture. The internal conflict between the legacy team and the acquisition unit exacerbates the delay, causing the product to miss the critical holiday window and cannibalize attention from the main platform launch later that year.

The kill criteria are missing entirely. There is no defined metric for carrier uptake, no cap on the burn rate for the delayed project, and no trigger to pivot to a software-only solution if hardware pre-orders miss targets. The assumption that a carrier will push a niche, high-friction device without an app ecosystem contradicts standard market dynamics for 2010. The "do nothing" option (waiting for the main platform) carries less risk and lower cost than forcing a fragmented, hardware-dependent social experiment.

The strongest attack survived is the notion that a "walled garden" ensures safety; however, without apps or games, the garden is empty, rendering the safety feature irrelevant to the user's desire for engagement. The proposal confuses "control" with "value."

Verdict: The plan requires a full smartphone data plan for a non-smartphone experience, targets the wrong economic segment with the wrong feature set, and ignores the imminent internal platform launch. It creates a high-cost distraction with a near-certain probability of market rejection.

DECISION: NO-GO
