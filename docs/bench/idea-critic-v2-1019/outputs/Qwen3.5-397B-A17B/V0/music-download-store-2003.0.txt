The claim is that selling individual songs for 99 cents with light DRM, restricted to proprietary hardware and dependent on fragile label licenses, will succeed despite ubiquitous free alternatives and competitor shifts to subscriptions.

The strongest defensible version: A seamless, legal, per-song purchase experience integrated tightly with the dominant portable player creates enough convenience and trust to convert users from free piracy, even at low margins and with platform restrictions.

**Skeptic:** The model assumes labels won't withdraw licenses or demand higher fees once dependence grows. It assumes 99 cents covers costs after label splits. It assumes users prefer owning restricted files over free unlimited sharing or emerging subscriptions. The "own computers only" restriction severely caps market reach initially.

**Better-way:** Do nothing and let piracy erode sales further. Pivot immediately to subscriptions like rivals. License content without DRM to maximize compatibility. Partner with Windows makers to expand hardware reach instantly.

**Kill-criteria:** If label negotiations fail to secure all major catalogs by launch, the value proposition collapses. If margin analysis shows negative unit economics after label payments and operations, the model is unsustainable. If adoption metrics show <5% conversion from free users within six months, the pricing/DRM model is rejected.

**Pre-mortem:** The store fails because labels later demand higher rates, squeezing margins to zero. It fails because users reject device lock-in. It fails because subscriptions offer better value, making per-song purchases feel archaic. It is a two-way door only if contracts allow exit; otherwise, sunk licensing costs are lost. Delay costs market share to subscription rivals.

**Decision:** The proposal relies on unverified margin math and fragile label relationships while intentionally limiting the addressable market to proprietary hardware users. The rise of subscriptions suggests this per-song model may be a transitional dead end. However, if the goal is to save the hardware business by legitimizing music consumption quickly, this is the only viable immediate path, provided margins are positive. Without evidence of positive unit economics or secured long-term label stability, the risk is too high.

DECISION: NO-GO
