The claim assumes a durable monopoly on global connectivity for business travelers despite a $5 billion debt load requiring immediate, massive adoption. The steelman posits that terrestrial networks cannot match satellite reach quickly enough to capture the high-margin traveler segment before debt servicing forces insolvency.

However, the "better-way" analysis reveals a fatal flaw: the status quo (terrestrial cellular) is improving exponentially at a fraction of the capital cost, while this proposal offers a bulky, expensive, indoor-incompatible alternative. The $3,000 handset and high per-minute costs create a barrier to entry that shrinks the addressable market precisely when volume is needed to service debt. The risk of ground networks spreading fast is not a minor variable; it is an existential threat that invalidates the core revenue model.

The pre-mortem is clear: within five years, terrestrial coverage fills the gaps, handsets become obsolete compared to sleek phones, and the company cannot pivot its cost structure. The "do nothing" alternative for the market is superior, as users wait for cheaper, better local solutions rather than adopting this bridge technology. The cost of delay for the company is high, but the cost of execution is catastrophic given the mismatch between fixed debt obligations and a shrinking, price-sensitive market.

There is no reversible path here; the capital is sunk, but further commercial commitment locks in losses. The kill criterion—subscriber growth required to meet debt payments—will almost certainly be missed because the value proposition (global reach) is being commoditized by competitors with lower costs and better user experiences (indoor coverage, smaller devices). The evidence suggests the market window is closing, not opening.

This proposal bets on a temporary infrastructure gap while ignoring the speed of terrestrial innovation and the rigidity of its own financial model. It fails the fit check against a rapidly changing landscape where "good enough" local coverage beats "expensive and bulky" global coverage for all but a tiny niche insufficient to support $5 billion in debt.

DECISION: NO-GO
