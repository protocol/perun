The claim is that selling individual songs for 99 cents with light DRM will succeed despite ubiquitous free alternatives and label dependency. The steelman: a seamless, legal, per-song purchase experience offers sufficient convenience and ethical clarity to convert users away from fragmented, risky file-sharing, while securing label cooperation through a controlled ecosystem.

However, critical failures loom. First, the "free vs. 99 cents" gap is massive; if the friction of file-sharing remains low, price sensitivity will crush adoption. Second, relying on major labels creates a single point of failure; if they withdraw licenses or demand unsustainable rates, the model collapses. Third, restricting the store to proprietary computers severely limits market reach compared to open rivals backing subscriptions. Finally, the shift to subscriptions suggests the market may skip per-song ownership entirely, making this a transitional dead-end.

Better ways exist: accelerate a subscription model immediately to match rival strategies, or make the store cross-platform to maximize volume before locking users into a hardware ecosystem. Doing nothing preserves cash while the market clarifies, rather than betting on a fragile per-song margin.

Pre-mortem: The store launches, but users refuse to pay for what they get free; labels raise fees citing low volume; the hardware lock-in prevents critical mass; the company pivots to subscriptions two years later having wasted resources. This is a two-way door only if the initial cap is tiny, but the reputational risk with labels is high.

Evidence is purely historical analogy within the prompt; no current data verifies label willingness or user price elasticity. The assumption that "light copy protection" satisfies labels without annoying users is unproven.

Kill criteria: If label licensing deals take longer than six months, or if initial attach rates on devices are below 5%, stop. The leading metric is songs sold per active device.

The proposal bets on a specific behavior change (paying for singles) in a hostile environment (free sharing) with a constrained distribution channel. The risks of label revolt and consumer resistance outweigh the potential first-mover advantage in a potentially obsolete format.

DECISION: NO-GO
