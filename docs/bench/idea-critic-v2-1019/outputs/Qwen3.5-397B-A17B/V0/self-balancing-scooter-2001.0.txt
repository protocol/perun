The claim is that launching a $5,000 self-balancing transporter with 40,000-unit capacity in 2001 is a viable business move. The steelman: this device solves last-mile transit so uniquely that early adopters and institutions will ignore high cost, legal ambiguity, and social stigma to buy it immediately.

However, the attack reveals fatal flaws. First, the legal status is "unclear" for sidewalks and roads. Launching a vehicle without defined regulatory approval invites immediate bans, seizures, or lawsuits, halting distribution before scale. Second, the price ($5,000 in 2001) is prohibitive for mass adoption, limiting the market to niche enthusiasts who may be deterred by the risk of looking "conspicuous." Third, the utility is narrow; it cannot carry cargo, reducing institutional value. Finally, building a factory for 40,000 units assumes demand that is explicitly described as "unproven." This is a massive one-way door: capital is sunk into fixed assets before validating if anyone will legally or socially accept the product.

The "better way" is a limited pilot program in controlled private environments (campuses, factories) where legal status is manageable, using contract manufacturing rather than a dedicated factory. This tests actual usage and social acceptance without the risk of a 40,000-unit overhang. The proposal fails the pre-mortem: if this launches as proposed, the most likely outcome is unsold inventory and regulatory shutdown because the foundational assumptions (legal clarity, mass market willingness at $5k) are unverified guesses, not facts. The kill criteria for any launch must include resolved legal status and confirmed orders matching at least 20% of capacity; neither exists here.

Proceeding now bets the company on a hypothesis that the market will instantly embrace an expensive, legally gray, socially awkward device. The evidence provided suggests the opposite: significant barriers exist in every category (legal, economic, social, utility).

DECISION: NO-GO
