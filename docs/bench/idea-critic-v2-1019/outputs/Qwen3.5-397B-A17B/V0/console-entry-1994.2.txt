The claim is that a consumer electronics manufacturer should enter the home console market with a CD-based, 3D graphics system, leveraging cheaper media and developer tools to disrupt two incumbents.

Steelman: The shift from cartridges to CDs offers a decisive cost and capacity advantage, enabling lower hardware pricing and attracting third-party developers squeezed by incumbent manufacturing costs, thereby creating a viable ecosystem despite lack of platform experience.

However, the risks are existential. The company has zero experience as a platform holder, a role fundamentally different from hardware manufacturing. The market is a duopoly with entrenched loyalty and killer proprietary software franchises that this entrant lacks. Crucially, a direct competitor is launching a comparable 3D system simultaneously, neutralizing the technological first-mover advantage. The business model relies on selling hardware at a loss to recoup via software royalties—a dangerous cash-flow strategy for a newcomer without a guaranteed hit pipeline.

The "better way" analysis fails to find a safe alternative path within this proposal. Doing nothing avoids burning capital in a hostile market. Partnering (the original cancelled plan) was safer but rejected by the partner, signaling low confidence from the industry leader. Courting outside developers is speculative; without a installed base, developers will not prioritize this new platform over established ones, regardless of tool ease.

The pre-mortem is clear: The console launches, fails to secure must-have exclusive games, loses money on every unit sold, and cannot sustain the loss-leading strategy against incumbents who can cross-subsidize from profitable software divisions. The cost of delay is low compared to the cost of failure, which is total write-off of R&D and marketing spend.

There is no evidence provided that the company has secured key software partners or has a plan to absorb multi-year hardware losses. The assumption that "cheaper discs" equals "developer adoption" ignores the reality that developers follow users, not media formats. Without a verified path to a critical mass of users and software, this is a high-risk gamble with a low probability of success.

DECISION: NO-GO
