The claim is that a cartoon house shell will solve home-user complexity in 1995. The steelman: a playful, low-friction interface reduces intimidation for novices, creating a new market segment before the OS update arrives.

However, the attack reveals fatal flaws. First, the hardware constraint is disqualifying: requiring a "high-end PC" when the risk explicitly states most home PCs lack the memory means the product cannot reach its target audience. Second, the timing is catastrophic; launching a major new interface months before a built-in OS update with similar goals guarantees immediate obsolescence and channel conflict. Third, the "patronizing" risk alienates the growing base of semi-literate users who want efficiency, not cartoons, while the lack of third-party add-ons ensures the ecosystem dies at launch.

The "do nothing" alternative is superior: wait for the upcoming OS release which likely addresses these needs natively without extra cost or hardware requirements. A "cheaper way" would be a software-only tutorial layer on existing interfaces, not a full shell replacement.

The pre-mortem is clear: the product launches, fails to install on 80% of target machines, is rejected by the 20% who can run it as childish, and is rendered irrelevant by the OS update within a quarter. The cost of delay is negative; waiting avoids sinking development and marketing funds into a dead end. There is no reversible path here; the brand damage from a failed, "kiddy" product on high-end machines would linger.

This proposal fails the fit check against market reality (hardware limits), timing (OS release), and user sentiment (patronizing design). The evidence provided in the risks alone is sufficient to kill the claim without further data. No amount of revision can fix the hardware mismatch or the impending OS competition.
