The claim is that a mass-market economy manufacturer can successfully launch a separate luxury brand with a new dealer network and a single flagship sedan to compete with established European leaders. The steelman is that structural separation (brand and dealers) combined with superior engineering at a lower price point can overcome the "economy" stigma and capture value-conscious luxury buyers.

However, critical failures loom. First, the "separate dealer network" requires massive upfront capital expenditure precisely when development costs have already been heavy; if initial sales volume is low due to the thin single-model lineup, the fixed cost of these dealers could bankrupt the division before scale is reached. Second, prestige is not engineered; it is accrued over decades. A single model, regardless of quality, lacks the breadth to convince luxury buyers to abandon heritage brands, risking the perception of a "cheap imitation" rather than a true rival. Third, the strategy assumes price sensitivity outweighs brand equity in the luxury segment, which may be false; luxury buyers often pay *for* the exclusivity and history this new brand lacks.

The pre-mortem reveals a scenario where the flagship launches to critical acclaim for its engineering but commercial failure due to brand dissonance. The expensive, underutilized dealer network becomes a financial anchor, dragging down the profitable parent company. The "better way" would be to leverage existing infrastructure or acquire an existing struggling luxury marque to gain immediate heritage and network, rather than building from scratch. The cost of delay is high, but the cost of immediate execution without a broader lineup or proven brand pull is potentially existential.

The strongest attack survived is the price advantage; however, price alone cannot sustain a luxury position if the brand narrative fails. Without a second model to spread dealer costs or a proven marketing engine to manufacture prestige, the risk of catastrophic capital loss is too high. The proposal relies on too many unproven assumptions about consumer behavior and capital efficiency.

DECISION: NO-GO
