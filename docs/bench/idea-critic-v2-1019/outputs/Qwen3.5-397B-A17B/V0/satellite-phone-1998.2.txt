The claim is that a $5 billion satellite network can succeed in 1998 by targeting business travelers with $3,000 handsets and high per-minute rates. The steelman: this is the only solution for global coverage where terrestrial cells cannot reach, creating a monopoly niche for urgent connectivity.

However, the attack reveals fatal flaws. First, the "do nothing" alternative (waiting 2–3 years) allows terrestrial networks to cover 90% of business travel routes at 1% of the cost, rendering the satellite value proposition obsolete for the primary target. Second, the economics are inverted: debt service on $5 billion requires millions of subscribers, but the $3,000 entry price and bulky, indoor-incompatible hardware restrict the addressable market to a tiny fraction of that need. Third, the sales channel risk is critical; without ready distribution, subscriber growth will lag debt payments, triggering insolvency before network effects can stabilize revenue.

The pre-mortem shows failure via cash crunch: the company runs out of liquidity waiting for a market that shrinks daily as ground towers expand. The cost of delay is actually negative; launching now locks in high fixed costs against a rapidly depreciating asset advantage. A cheaper alternative exists: pivot to government/military contracts or maritime safety where price sensitivity is lower and indoor use is irrelevant, rather than forcing a consumer business traveler model.

The strongest attack survived is the "global coverage" uniqueness, but this does not save the specific *commercial plan* proposed. The pricing and hardware constraints make the required volume impossible to achieve before debt defaults. The proposal assumes rapid adoption despite superior, cheaper substitutes emerging immediately. There is no kill criterion or pivot plan included to mitigate the high probability of missing subscriber targets.

This is a one-way door with massive downside and a vanishing window of opportunity. The plan relies on assumptions about market size and hardware adoption that contradict the stated risks of ground network expansion and device limitations.

DECISION: NO-GO
