The proposal fails the steelman test: a business-email leader launching a tablet *without* built-in email, calendar, or contacts (unless paired) directly contradicts its core value proposition. The "pairing" requirement creates friction that negates the convenience of a standalone tablet, especially for business users who expect immediate productivity.

The "better way" analysis reveals fatal flaws in the current plan. Competing against a rival with a large app store using an untested OS is high-risk. Matching the price of a larger, established competitor while offering less functionality (no native email) is a losing strategy. The cheapest and safest alternative is to either: (A) delay launch until native email apps are ready, or (B) launch with a web-based email solution that works independently of the phone, ensuring the core business promise is kept.

The pre-mortem is clear: developers will not commit to an untested OS without a clear user base, and business users will reject a device that cannot check email out of the box. The "one-way door" here is launching a product that damages the brand's reputation for business reliability. The cost of delay (waiting for apps or a web solution) is far lower than the cost of launching a flawed product that confirms market skepticism.

The evidence provided highlights that the proposal relies on assumptions that are currently unsupported: that users will accept pairing requirements, that developers will flock to an untested OS, and that price parity with a superior product is sufficient. These are not sound bets. The strongest attack—that this product undermines the company's primary strength (business email)—has not been refuted. Without native email or a clear path to app ecosystem viability, this launch invites failure.

DECISION: NO-GO
