The claim is that switching to the dominant processor architecture within two years, using a translation layer, is the optimal path despite known risks. The steelman: this move is an existential necessity to correct a decade-long performance deficit that threatens the product's core value proposition in the thin-laptop segment.

However, the proposal fails the "better-way" and "kill-criteria" tests based strictly on the provided facts. The risks identified are not merely potential; they are structural flaws in the current plan. Announcing early freezes sales of the only current revenue generator. Forcing developers to rebuild software creates immediate friction. Running translated software slower degrades the user experience the switch aims to improve. Most critically, weakening brand distinctiveness attacks the company's primary moat.

The proposal offers no mitigation for the "painful" previous transition, suggesting organizational learning was not applied. It sets a hard two-year deadline without defining what success looks like mid-stream or what specific metric would trigger a stop if the translation layer proves too slow or the sales freeze becomes catastrophic. There is no "do nothing" or "hybrid" alternative weighed against the total line replacement. The plan assumes the translation layer will be sufficient to retain users, yet admits it will be slower, creating a direct contradiction in value delivery.

Without a defined kill criterion (e.g., "if translated app performance is below X% of native by date Y, halt rollout") or a strategy to prevent the sales freeze, this is a high-risk gamble that could destroy current cash flow while failing to secure future loyalty. The distinctiveness risk alone warrants a revised approach that preserves brand identity rather than diluting it into the generic PC market. The plan is incomplete and dangerous as stated.

DECISION: NO-GO
