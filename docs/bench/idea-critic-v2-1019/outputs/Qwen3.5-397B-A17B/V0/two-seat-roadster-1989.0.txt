The claim rests on a contrarian bet: that the disappearance of small British roadsters created an unmet demand rather than proving the segment's economic unviability. The steelman is that a lightweight, affordable rear-drive convertible can capture nostalgic enthusiasts and casual drivers excluded by high-cost, complex modern sports cars.

However, the skepticism is severe. The market shrinkage is a leading indicator of fundamental unit economics failure, not just a temporary gap. Safety regulations in the late 1980s were escalating costs precisely for open-top vehicles, threatening the "affordable" pillar. Without sports-car heritage, the brand lacks the credibility to command a premium or justify the purchase to non-enthusiasts, limiting the total addressable market to a shrinking pool of purists. The "two-seat limit" is a structural ceiling on volume that mass-market makers already rejected.

The "better way" analysis suggests that if the niche were viable, existing players with lower cost bases or established heritage (like Mazda, which was exploring this exact space with the MX-5) would be better positioned to exploit it. A mid-size maker without heritage faces higher marketing costs to educate buyers and higher per-unit engineering costs to meet safety standards without existing platforms.

The pre-mortem is clear: the car launches to initial press acclaim from journalists nostalgic for the past, but retail sales collapse after the first wave of early adopters. The company is left with a low-volume, high-complexity production line that drains resources from core high-volume models, unable to achieve the economies of scale required for profitability. The risk of fading sales after the enthusiast rush is not a possibility; it is the historical pattern for this segment.

While the idea is romantically appealing, the structural headwinds (safety costs, limited buyer pool, lack of heritage) outweigh the potential reward of capturing a ghost market. The proposal fails to demonstrate how it will overcome the specific reasons mass-market makers abandoned the niche.

DECISION: NO-GO
