The claim is that launching a $10,000 graphical business computer in 1983 will succeed despite superior cheap alternatives, slow performance, and a software vacuum.

**Steelman:** High pricing signals premium enterprise value, and early adoption of graphical interfaces secures long-term lock-in before competitors mature.

**Attack:** The proposal fails on three verified fronts. First, at several times the cost of leading business PCs, the price point excludes the volume necessary to sustain a platform, especially when a cheaper internal alternative exists. Second, "little third-party software" is a fatal flaw for business utility; without immediate productivity apps, the hardware is a toy, regardless of interface innovation. Third, releasing a slow machine invites ridicule against faster, text-based incumbents. The internal cheaper alternative creates channel conflict, cannibalizing sales before they start.

**Better Way:** Delay launch until the internal cheaper machine is ready, or pivot this hardware to a specialized high-end niche with bundled software, rather than a general office release. Alternatively, license the interface to the cheaper internal project to accelerate its readiness.

**Kill Criteria:** Do not proceed if the internal cheaper machine reaches market within 12 months (high probability given it is "being developed"). Do not proceed if fewer than 10 critical business applications are committed to launch.

**Pre-mortem:** The product launches, sells 5% of forecast due to price, receives reviews citing slowness and lack of software, and the internal cheaper team captures the actual graphical market six months later. The brand is damaged as overpriced and underpowered.

**Decision:** The risks are structural and unmitigated. The price is unjustifiable against internal and external competition, and the lack of software renders the value proposition hollow. This is a one-way door to significant financial loss and reputational damage.

DECISION: NO-GO
