The claim assumes a separate brand and dealer network are required to escape the "economy" stigma, ignoring that modern luxury buyers often value engineering over badges. The proposal bets heavily on a single flagship sedan; if this one model fails to perfectly match European refinement, the entire brand collapses with no fallback. The cost of building a new dealer network from scratch creates massive fixed overhead before a single unit is sold, violating the principle of capital efficiency for a company known for economy.

A better way exists: launch a premium sub-brand using existing, upgraded showrooms within current dealerships (shop-in-shop model) to drastically reduce upfront capital risk. Alternatively, acquire a struggling European niche maker to instantly gain prestige and engineering credibility rather than building it from scratch at high cost. The "do nothing" option preserves cash while the company incrementally improves its highest-tier current models to test price elasticity.

The pre-mortem reveals the most likely failure mode: the market perceives the car as a "rich person's economy car" due to the parent company's overwhelming identity, rendering the separate dealer network a sunk cost with zero traffic. The door is one-way; once billions are spent on distinct tooling and real estate, retreat is financially catastrophic. The cost of delay is low; European incumbents are not launching new mass-luxury segments immediately, and waiting allows for a multi-model launch strategy rather than a thin, risky single-lineup.

The strongest attack survived is the fear of brand dilution; however, history shows distinct branding can work if the product is genuinely differentiated. Yet, the specific execution here—building a whole new dealer network for one car—is financially reckless without proven demand. The proposal lacks a kill criterion for the dealer rollout if initial pre-orders miss targets.

DECISION: NO-GO
