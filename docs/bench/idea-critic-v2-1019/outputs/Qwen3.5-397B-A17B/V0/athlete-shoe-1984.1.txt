**Steelman:** Secure a generational talent early to dominate basketball culture, using controversy and scarcity to drive demand that transcends on-court performance.

**Attack & Alternatives:**
The primary failure mode is the athlete never playing due to injury or underperformance, wasting a massive budget share. However, the alternative—bidding for established stars—guarantees higher costs for diminishing cultural relevance. "Doing nothing" cedes the category to competitors. The risk of league fines for rule-breaking colorways is not a bug; it is a marketing engine that generates free media coverage, offsetting the financial penalty.

**Fit & Pre-mortem:**
This aligns with a "challenger" vision requiring disruption, not incremental gain. If we assume the player becomes a star, the ROI is infinite; if he fails, the brand remains a running specialist (status quo). The cost of delay is losing the only candidate with this specific cultural ceiling.

**Evidence & Verification:**
No external data exists to verify the player's future skill, making this a high-conviction bet on scouting intuition rather than historical stats. The proposal admits the lack of pro track record but argues the upside justifies the variance.

**Kill Criteria & Decision:**
The proposal lacks explicit stop-losses (e.g., "terminate if rookie averages <10 points"), but the five-year cap limits exposure. The "fine" risk is self-mitigating by the publicity value. While the financial concentration is dangerous, the potential to redefine the category outweighs the probability of total loss. The strategy transforms a product launch into a cultural movement, rendering standard sales projections irrelevant.

The strongest attack (career-ending injury before game one) cannot be fully eliminated, but no safer alternative offers comparable upside. The plan survives because the "risk" of fines is actually a feature, and the "unproven" status is the only reason the deal is affordable.

DECISION: GO
