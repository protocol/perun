The claim is that launching a $5,000 self-balancing transporter with 40,000-unit capacity in 2001 will succeed despite regulatory ambiguity and unproven consumer demand. The steelman: this is a category-defining breakthrough where first-mover advantage and patent protection outweigh early adoption friction, allowing premium pricing to fund scaling before competitors emerge.

However, the attack reveals fatal flaws in the current plan. First, the "unclear legal status" is not a minor risk but a potential existential blocker; if municipalities ban sidewalk use and roads are unsafe, the addressable market collapses to private property, rendering a 40,000-unit factory a massive stranded asset. Second, the price point ($5,000 in 2001) exceeds most consumer vehicles' down payments, restricting sales to wealthy early adopters or niche institutional buyers, which cannot sustain the proposed factory scale. Third, the "conspicuous" nature and lack of cargo utility suggest limited repeat usage or word-of-mouth growth beyond a novelty phase.

The "do nothing" alternative (waiting for regulatory clarity and battery cost reductions) or a "limited pilot" (producing only 2,000 units to test real-world legal pushback and usage patterns) are superior. The current proposal bets the company on mass adoption before verifying if the product is legally permissible or socially acceptable. The cost of delay is low compared to the cost of building a factory for a product that may be banned or ignored. The pre-mortem shows failure via regulatory bans and inventory glut. There is no kill criterion defined for stopping production if initial sales miss targets or if key cities issue bans.

Without a phased approach to validate legal viability and actual consumer behavior at this price point, proceeding with full factory scaling is reckless. The proposal fails to account for the high probability that the market size is orders of magnitude smaller than the factory capacity.

DECISION: NO-GO
