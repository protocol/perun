The claim rests on the assumption that "affordable" and "lightweight" can coexist with modern safety regulations without eroding margins, despite the segment's historical shrinkage. The steelman is not merely building a retro car, but creating a high-volume entry point for a new demographic excluded by current high-price niche offerings.

However, the "better-way" analysis reveals a fatal flaw in the "affordable" premise. Safety rules in 1989 increasingly mandate structural rigidity (roll-over protection, crash zones) that directly conflicts with lightweight, low-cost convertible construction. Mass-market makers abandoned this niche not due to lack of imagination, but because the unit economics collapsed under regulatory weight. To be "affordable" while meeting these standards likely requires subsidizing the vehicle with shared components that compromise the "pure roadster" driving dynamics, creating a product that satisfies neither purists nor value seekers.

The pre-mortem shows the project failing because early enthusiast sales spike then vanish, leaving no repeat buyers or fleet potential to sustain production lines. Unlike a halo car that boosts brand prestige for high-margin SUVs, a standalone low-margin roadster from a mid-size maker with no heritage risks becoming a financial drain with no spillover value. The "do nothing" option preserves capital for the core business where volume exists.

The strongest attack is the economic contradiction: you cannot simultaneously achieve low weight, low cost, and regulatory compliance in a two-seat convertible without sacrificing the very qualities that define the genre. If the car is cheap, it feels unsafe or flimsy; if it is safe and rigid, it is too heavy or expensive to be "affordable." The proposal offers no evidence of a breakthrough manufacturing method or regulatory exemption to solve this trilemma. Without a verified path to positive unit economics beyond the initial enthusiast wave, the risk of a slow, expensive failure is too high.

DECISION: NO-GO
