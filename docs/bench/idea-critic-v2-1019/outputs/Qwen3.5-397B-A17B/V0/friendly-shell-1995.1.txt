Steelman: A cartoon house shell lowers the learning curve for novice 1995 users by mapping digital tasks to familiar physical metaphors, creating an immediate revenue stream ($100) while the core OS matures.

However, the attack on this claim reveals fatal flaws in the current context. First, the hardware constraint is a market killer: requiring a "high-end PC" in 1995 excludes the vast majority of the target "home user" demographic who own standard machines. The addressable market is too small to sustain development costs. Second, the timing is catastrophic. With a major OS release featuring a new interface arriving in months, this product faces immediate obsolescence. Customers will not pay $100 for a stopgap solution that the native OS will likely render redundant or incompatible shortly after purchase. Third, the "patronizing" risk alienates the influential early adopters and mixed-use households, while the lack of third-party add-ons creates a dead-end ecosystem with no longevity.

The "do nothing" alternative is superior: wait for the upcoming OS release to address interface improvements natively, avoiding the cost of building a siloed, hardware-restricted shell. A "cheaper way" would be a software tutorial layer, not a full spatial metaphor requiring high memory.

The pre-mortem is clear: six months post-launch, sales are flat because the hardware base is insufficient, and the product is abandoned when the new OS arrives. The cost of delay is negative; waiting actually saves development capital and prevents brand damage from a perceived "toy" overlay. The claim that this generates immediate value fails because the friction of hardware upgrades and the shadow of the incoming OS release destroy the value proposition before launch.

This proposal attempts to solve a usability problem with a heavy, temporary, and exclusionary solution right before a platform shift. It fails the fit check against market reality and strategic timing.

DECISION: NO-GO
