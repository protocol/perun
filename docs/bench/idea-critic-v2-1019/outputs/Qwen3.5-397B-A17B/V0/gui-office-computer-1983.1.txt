The claim that launching a $10,000 slow PC with scarce software in 1983 will succeed fails the steelman test. The strongest defensible version—that early GUI adoption justifies a massive premium—collapses under the weight of internal and external realities.

**Skeptic:** The price is several times higher than leading business PCs, which likely rely on proven command-line interfaces. Office workers, the target demographic, prioritize productivity and compatibility over novel interaction models they do not yet understand. Running slowly exacerbates this; a sluggish expensive machine is a harder sell than a fast cheap one. The lack of third-party software creates a barren ecosystem, rendering the hardware useless for specialized business tasks.

**Better Way:** The "do nothing" or "wait" option is superior. An internal competitor is developing a cheaper similar machine. Launching the expensive version now cannibalizes future sales and damages brand reputation when the cheaper, presumably faster or better-supported unit arrives. The optimal path is to delay this specific SKU, learn from the internal cheaper project, or pivot this hardware to a niche vertical where GUI offers undeniable, unique value not met by text-based rivals.

**Kill Criteria:** If the machine cannot run standard business applications faster or more reliably than existing $2,000–$4,000 competitors, it should not launch. The absence of a software ecosystem is a fatal flaw for a general "office worker" target.

**Pre-mortem:** Assume failure. The cause is clear: sales teams cannot justify the 5x price premium to CFOs when the machine offers lower performance and fewer applications. Inventory piles up, the brand is tagged as out of touch, and the internal cheaper team loses momentum due to market confusion.

**Decision:** The proposal ignores basic unit economics and market readiness. The internal alternative alone dictates a hold. Launching now guarantees financial loss and strategic distraction.

DECISION: NO-GO
