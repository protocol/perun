The proposal fails the steelman test: it asks price-sensitive youth to pay a premium data plan for a device stripped of the very features (apps, games) that drive smartphone adoption. The "better way" is evident: leverage the company's main platform launching later, which likely supports apps, rather than forcing a delayed, acquired project to launch early with a crippled value proposition.

The pre-mortem is clear: these phones will sit unsold. Young buyers will reject a high-cost data mandate for a "dumb" social phone when competitors offer full smartphones. The internal conflict history suggests the acquired team is being set up to fail with inadequate resources, further jeopardizing execution. The cost of delay is negligible compared to the certainty of channel conflict with the main platform and brand damage from a flopped launch.

There is no evidence that a carrier will successfully sell a non-smartphone at smartphone data prices to teens in 2010, especially one lacking an app store. The assumption that "social networking" alone justifies the cost ignores the rapid shift toward multifunctional devices. The proposal creates a cannibalization risk with the upcoming main platform while offering no unique advantage to offset the high price and limited functionality.

The strongest attack survived is the notion of speed-to-market via the acquired team; however, this speed is illusory if the product is unsellable. A revised approach would integrate the social features into the main platform or delay this specific hardware until the software stack matures. As proposed, the unit economics and product-market fit are broken.

DECISION: NO-GO
