The claim rests on a contrarian bet: that the disappearance of small British roadsters represents an unfilled demand rather than a market verdict. The steelman is that a lightweight, affordable rear-drive convertible can revive a dormant emotional segment by ignoring modern bloat, capturing enthusiasts ignored by mass-market safety and cost inflation.

However, the skeptic identifies fatal gaps in the provided facts. The proposal admits the segment has "shrunk for years" and sales may "fade once early enthusiasts have bought." This suggests a finite, dying pool of buyers, not a sustainable market. Without data on the size of this remaining pool versus the development cost of a new chassis, the financial viability is unknown. The "affordable" price point conflicts with the admitted high cost of meeting safety rules for convertibles; unless "lightweight" implies stripping safety features (a regulatory non-starter), the margin math is unproven.

The "better-way" analysis highlights that if the niche is truly dead due to safety costs and low volume, the superior alternative is to license an existing platform or wait for a competitor to prove the water is safe. Launching a clean-sheet design for a "dead niche" with no company heritage in the class maximizes risk. The proposal offers no evidence of a waiting list or pre-orders to contradict the "fade" prediction.

The pre-mortem is clear: the car launches, sells to the initial wave of nostalgic buyers, and then inventory piles up as the broader market rejects a two-seat car with compromised practicality and high insurance costs. The company lacks the heritage to command a premium that would offset low volumes. The kill criteria are missing; there is no defined sales threshold to halt production before losses become catastrophic.

While the emotional appeal is strong, the factual basis provided describes a shrinking market with high barriers and no proven demand beyond a fleeting enthusiast wave. Approving this now ignores the high probability of a "one-hit wonder" failure that drains capital from core mid-size operations. The risks outweigh the unproven opportunity.

DECISION: NO-GO
