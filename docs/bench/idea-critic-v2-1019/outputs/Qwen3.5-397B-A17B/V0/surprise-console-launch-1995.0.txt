The claim is that launching four months early in May 1995 at $399 with limited inventory and software will secure a market advantage against a rival launching in September.

The steelman: Seizing the "first-mover" narrative and shelf space before the competitor arrives creates an insurmountable lead, justifying the friction with excluded retailers and the premium price.

However, this plan fails the pre-mortem. The "first-mover" advantage relies on a playable library; shipping with "few games ready" for a machine known to be "hard to program for 3D" guarantees a disastrous consumer experience. Early adopters who buy the hardware will find nothing to play, turning the early launch into a public relations liability rather than a victory. The $100 price premium is indefensible without a robust software lineup to demonstrate superior value. Furthermore, alienating major retailers by excluding them from the initial shipment invites retaliation precisely when broad distribution is needed to compete with the rival's September arrival.

The better way is to use the extra four months to secure launch titles and unify the retail channel. A coordinated September launch with a strong game lineup and full retail support at a competitive price point outweighs the hollow victory of being first with an empty console. The cost of delay (waiting until September) is lower than the cost of a failed early launch that burns brand reputation and retailer goodwill before the real competition begins.

The strongest attack survived is the fear of losing shelf space; however, shelf space without sell-through due to lack of games is worthless. The proposal sacrifices long-term viability for a short-term headline. The kill criterion is clear: if the software lineup cannot support a premium price and broad appeal by September, the launch window itself is flawed, but rushing to May exacerbates the software deficit.

DECISION: NO-GO
