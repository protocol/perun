The claim is that selling individual songs for 99 cents with light DRM, restricted to proprietary hardware, will succeed despite ubiquitous free alternatives and industry skepticism. The steelman: this creates a legal, high-convenience ecosystem that locks users into the hardware, monetizing the player rather than the software margin.

However, critical failures loom. First, the "widespread free file-sharing" creates a zero-price anchor; converting users requires frictionless experience, yet restricting the store to "own computers" severely limits market penetration compared to open rivals. Second, the margin risk is existential: if label payments consume most of the 99 cents, the model relies entirely on hardware uplift, which may stall if the ecosystem feels too closed. Third, the industry shift toward subscriptions suggests this per-song model is a legacy play; rivals backing subscriptions may capture long-term value while this proposal fights a rear-guard action for ownership.

The strongest attack survived is the hardware lock-in strategy. While restricting the store seems limiting, it is the only defensible moat: it ensures the music player remains the primary gateway, forcing competitors to replicate the entire integrated stack rather than just the software. Free sharing lacks integration and safety; subscriptions in 2003 face bandwidth and catalog hurdles this proposal sidesteps by focusing on ownership. The 99-cent price point, while thin, serves as a loss-leader to drive hardware sales, where the real margin lies.

Yet, the dependency on "all major record labels" is a single point of failure. If even one withdraws, the catalog gap drives users back to piracy. Furthermore, the "light copy protection" may be cracked instantly, nullifying the exclusivity without providing enough user benefit to justify the cost over free.

The proposal bets on integration over openness and ownership over access. While risky, the alternative—doing nothing while piracy erodes the hardware's value proposition—is worse. The specific constraint of proprietary hardware is actually the strength, not the weakness, provided the user experience is superior to file-sharing.

DECISION: GO
