The claim is that removing the headphone jack justifies launching proprietary wireless earbuds at $159 despite high risks of ridicule, loss, and customer anger.

Steelman: This move forces a necessary ecosystem transition to wireless audio, leveraging a custom chip for seamless pairing to create a sticky, high-margin accessory that offsets the backlash of removing a legacy port.

However, the proposal fails the "better-way" and "fit" tests. First, pricing at $159 against free wired earphones creates an immediate, sharp value disconnect for the average user, risking mass rejection rather than adoption. Second, the "easy to lose" risk is not mitigated; without a proven tracking mechanism or tether, the form factor invites frequent replacement purchases that feel like penalties to users, damaging brand trust. Third, a five-hour battery life is insufficient for a "fully wireless" promise if the charging case is also bulky or inconvenient, reinforcing the "odd" aesthetic criticism.

The pre-mortem reveals a likely failure mode: customers perceive the removal of the jack not as innovation, but as a cost-cutting measure by the manufacturer that forces them to buy an overpriced, fragile accessory. The "do nothing" alternative (keeping the jack and offering wireless as an optional, lower-cost entry) preserves customer goodwill while still testing the market. The cost of delay is low; waiting for battery density to improve or prices to drop would reduce the "ridicule" factor.

The strongest attack survived is the ecosystem lock-in potential via the custom chip, but this only works if adoption is high. Given the high price point and lack of tangible durability benefits over wired options, adoption will likely be too low to justify the reputational damage. The proposal lacks a kill criterion for customer sentiment or a phased rollout to test the $159 price elasticity.

DECISION: NO-GO
