The claim is that a consumer electronics manufacturer should enter the home console market with a CD-based, 3D graphics system after a partnership cancellation, competing directly against two entrenched incumbents.

Steelman: Leveraging existing manufacturing scale and lower disc costs to undercut cartridge pricing could attract third-party developers excluded by incumbent policies, creating a software library sufficient to overcome hardware inexperience.

However, the failure hypothesis is severe. The proposal admits zero experience as a platform holder, while competitors possess decades of developer relations and loyal user bases tied to iconic first-party franchises. Entering a market where hardware is sold at a loss requires massive capital reserves to sustain subsidies until software royalties mature—a cash flow dynamic distinct from consumer electronics retail. The simultaneous launch of an incumbent's 3D system creates a direct feature parity clash where the newcomer lacks brand trust.

Critically, "easier tools" and "cheaper discs" are table stakes, not differentiators, if the install base is zero. Developers follow users, not manufacturing margins. Without a killer first-party application to drive initial hardware adoption, the platform cannot reach the critical mass required to sustain third-party interest. The risk of becoming a niche failure with unsold inventory and alienated developers is high.

The "do nothing" option preserves capital and avoids a costly distraction in a low-margin, hit-driven entertainment sector. The "better way" would be to license the technology to existing players rather than bearing the full platform risk alone. The pre-mortem reveals a likely outcome: strong hardware engineering wasted on a barren software library, leading to a rapid exit and reputational damage in the gaming sector.

The evidence provided highlights fatal gaps: no platform experience, strong incumbent moats, and a crowded launch window. The proposal relies on the assumption that cost advantages alone can displace network effects, which history suggests is false without a paradigm-shifting exclusive title or service model not mentioned here.

DECISION: NO-GO
