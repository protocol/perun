**Steelman:** Launching a premium, mouse-driven computer at $10,000 captures the high-end office market by offering an intuitive graphical interface that justifies the cost through productivity gains, despite current software limitations.

**Attack & Alternatives:**
The proposal fails on three critical fronts. First, the **price** is prohibitive; at several times the cost of leading business PCs, it excludes the mass corporate market required to sustain a new platform. Second, the **ecosystem** is empty; without third-party software, the hardware's unique interface offers little practical utility to office workers, rendering the premium price unjustifiable. Third, **internal cannibalization** is imminent; a cheaper machine with a similar interface is already in development. Launching the expensive model now guarantees it will be undercut by the company's own future product, destroying its value proposition before it matures.

The "do nothing" option (waiting for the cheaper internal model) or pivoting to a lower-cost hardware configuration are superior strategies. The current plan bets on a niche that likely cannot support the R&D recovery, especially when a direct, cheaper substitute is coming from within. The risk of market rejection due to price and lack of software is near-certain, while the internal competition ensures even early adopters will feel buyer's remorse shortly after launch.

**Kill Criteria:**
If the company cannot secure commitments for at least three major software suites before launch, or if the internal cheaper model's release date is within 12 months, this specific $10,000 configuration must not launch. The leading metric would be pre-order volume from Fortune 500 companies; if this does not cover 20% of projected first-year sales within 60 days of announcement, the project should be halted.

**Verdict:**
The proposal ignores basic market dynamics of price sensitivity and ecosystem dependency. It proposes selling an underpowered, expensive tool with no apps, directly competing with a forthcoming cheaper version from the same maker. This is a recipe for financial loss and brand confusion.

DECISION: NO-GO
