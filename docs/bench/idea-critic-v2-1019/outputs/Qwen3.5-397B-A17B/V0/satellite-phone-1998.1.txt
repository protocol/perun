The claim assumes a durable monopoly on global connectivity for business travelers despite a $5B debt load requiring immediate, massive scale. The steelman posits that terrestrial networks cannot match satellite reach quickly enough to capture the high-value traveler before debt servicing cripples the venture.

However, the "better way" analysis reveals a fatal flaw: the status quo (terrestrial cellular) is improving exponentially, not linearly. Building ground towers is cheaper, offers better indoor penetration, and supports smaller handsets. The proposal's $3,000 handset and high per-minute cost create a tiny addressable market, insufficient to service the debt. The "do nothing" alternative for the customer—waiting 12–24 months for roaming agreements between expanding terrestrial networks—offers a superior product (smaller phones, lower cost) at zero capital risk to the user.

The pre-mortem is clear: if terrestrial coverage hits 80% of business hubs within two years (a conservative estimate given current growth), the satellite network becomes a niche luxury with no path to profitability. The debt structure demands speed; the market dynamics demand patience the company cannot afford. The kill criterion is simple: if terrestrial coverage expands faster than subscriber acquisition, the model collapses. Given the bulkiness of handsets and poor indoor performance, adoption will lag, while competitors undercut price and quality rapidly.

The strongest attack survived is the argument for immediate global reach. Yet, "global" includes oceans and deserts where few business travelers roam. The revenue density in those areas is too low to justify the infrastructure cost compared to high-density urban centers served by ground towers. The proposal bets on a temporary gap in coverage that is closing faster than the debt can be retired. Without a verified path to millions of subscribers at a lower price point, the financial math does not hold. The risk of obsolescence before break-even is near certainty.

DECISION: NO-GO
