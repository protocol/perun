The proposal contains fatal contradictions that invalidate the business case. First, the core value proposition is "business email leadership," yet the device ships without built-in email, calendar, or contacts unless paired with a specific phone. This directly undermines the primary use case for the target demographic, making the product non-functional as a standalone business tool. Second, the pricing strategy is illogical: matching the price of a rival's larger, established tablet with a mature app store while offering a smaller screen, an untested operating system, and zero native productivity apps guarantees market rejection.

The "better way" analysis reveals a superior alternative exists: delay launch to integrate native email or bundle the device aggressively with the phone at a lower price point. Launching now creates a "worst of both worlds" scenario—too expensive for consumers, too crippled for enterprises. The risk of developer abandonment is compounded by the lack of killer apps at launch; without native email, the incentive for developers to build for this new, untested OS is negligible.

The pre-mortem is clear: the product launches, reviewers highlight the absence of email on a "business" device, sales flatline due to the high price-to-feature ratio, and the new operating system fails to gain traction, wasting the acquisition cost. The assumption that users will accept a crippled device at a premium price to access a nascent ecosystem is unsupported by the provided facts. The conflict between the "business email" brand promise and the "no email app" reality is unreconcilable in the current plan.

This is not a matter of minor revision; the fundamental value proposition is broken. The device cannot compete on hardware (smaller screen, same price), software (untested OS, no apps), or utility (no email). Proceeding guarantees financial loss and brand damage.
