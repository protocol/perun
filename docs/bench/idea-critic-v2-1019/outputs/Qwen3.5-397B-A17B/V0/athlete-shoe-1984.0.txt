The claim rests on a single, unproven variable: a rookie with zero professional track record. The steelman argues that transcendent talent exists before proof, and that rule-breaking controversy generates more value than compliance. However, the failure modes are catastrophic and immediate.

First, the injury risk is total. A single preseason tear renders the five-year financial commitment a sunk cost with zero return, draining a "large share" of the marketing budget from a brand already losing ground. There is no diversification; the entire basketball strategy hinges on one pair of ankles.

Second, the regulatory gamble is flawed. While fines generate headlines, they do not guarantee sales. If the league bans the shoe entirely rather than just fining the player, the inventory becomes worthless. The proposal assumes the league will tolerate the violation long enough for the shoe to gain traction, an assumption unsupported by the strict uniform enforcement of the era.

Third, the market precedent is negative. Signature shoes for unproven players historically fail after one season. Betting on a multi-year horizon contradicts the data without a mechanism to shorten the commitment if early sales lag. The "do nothing" alternative—signing an established veteran for less money—offers lower upside but significantly higher probability of recovering costs.

The proposal lacks a kill criterion. There is no defined metric for when to cut losses if the rookie underperforms or if the league escalates penalties beyond fines. Without a clause to terminate the deal based on performance or regulatory hostility, the company is locked into a failing bet. The "better way" is a performance-based contract with opt-outs, not a rigid five-year guarantee.

This is a one-way door with high irreversibility. Once the money is committed and the shoe designed, the capital is gone. The potential reward relies on a specific chain of events (talent manifests, no injury, league tolerates rules, consumers buy controversy) that has a low joint probability. The downside threatens the company's remaining marketing liquidity.

DECISION: NO-GO
