The claim is that removing the headphone jack justifies launching proprietary $159 wireless earbuds despite high risks of customer backlash and product ridicule. The steelman: this forces a necessary ecosystem transition, turning a hardware removal into a high-margin accessory revenue stream while establishing a new wireless standard.

However, the proposal fails the "better-way" and "fit" tests. First, the price point ($159) is unjustifiable against the included free wired alternative without proven superior value; early adopters may reject this cost for a product that looks "odd" and is easily lost. Second, the five-hour battery life is a functional downgrade for power users, creating a concrete failure scenario where the accessory frustrates rather than delights. Third, the risk of angering customers by removing a universal standard without a mature, affordable replacement is extreme; this could depress core phone sales, outweighing accessory margins.

The "kill criteria" are missing. There is no defined metric for acceptable adoption rates or a threshold for customer sentiment that would trigger a rollback or price adjustment. The proposal assumes the brand loyalty is strong enough to withstand the removal of a legacy feature, but offers no evidence that the wireless experience is seamless enough to prevent churn. The "do nothing" option (keeping the jack while offering wireless as an optional, lower-cost add-on) presents significantly less risk and preserves customer goodwill while testing the market.

The strongest attack survived is the potential for ridicule regarding the design; however, this is superficial compared to the functional risks of battery life and loss. The financial risk of alienating the install base is not mitigated by the proposed price or features. Without a cheaper entry point, a clear battery solution, or a phased removal of the jack, the proposal bets the core product's reception on an unproven accessory strategy. The cost of delay (waiting for battery tech to improve or prices to drop) is low compared to the cost of a botched launch.

DECISION: NO-GO
