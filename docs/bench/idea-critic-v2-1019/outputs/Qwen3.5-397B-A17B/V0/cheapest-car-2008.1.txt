The claim that launching the world's cheapest car at $2,500 will succeed relies on the assumption that price is the sole barrier to car ownership. However, this ignores critical non-price factors. First, the "cheapest" label may signal inferior safety and quality, deterring families who view a car as a long-term safety investment rather than a disposable upgrade from scooters. Second, the active farmer protests over the factory site introduce severe execution risk; delays or stoppages could destroy the high-volume economic model required to sustain the $2,500 price point. Third, the alternatives—scooters and used cars—are not just cheaper but proven; a new, unproven vehicle must offer more than just four wheels to justify switching from established, lower-cost options.

The proposal fails to address how it will overcome the stigma of being the "cheapest" or mitigate the supply chain disruption from land disputes. A "do nothing" or "wait" approach allows the company to resolve land issues and perhaps position the car as "affordable and safe" rather than "the cheapest," avoiding the race-to-the-bottom perception. The cost of delay is likely lower than the cost of a brand-damaging launch failure or a halted production line. Without a concrete plan to secure the factory site independently of protests or to reframe the marketing narrative away from pure price leadership, the current path is too fragile. The strongest attack—that the brand positioning invites fatal quality skepticism while operational risks threaten volume targets—remains unrefuted by the proposal's current facts.

DECISION: NO-GO
