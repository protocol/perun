The claim is that a cartoon house shell will solve home-user complexity in 1995. The steelman: a visual metaphor reduces cognitive load for novices, creating an immediate on-ramp to computing.

However, the failure hypothesis is strong: this proposal ignores the hardware reality and market timing. Most 1995 home PCs lack the memory required for high-end graphics, limiting the addressable market to a tiny fraction of users. The $100 price point creates a barrier for the very demographic struggling with cost and complexity. Furthermore, the "patronizing" risk is not merely aesthetic; experienced users (who influence household purchases) will reject it, while the impending major OS release renders a standalone shell obsolete before it gains traction. The lack of third-party add-ons confirms a dead ecosystem; without extensibility, it is a toy, not a platform.

Alternatives:
1. **Do nothing:** Wait for the upcoming OS release to integrate accessibility features natively at no extra cost.
2. **Software-only guide:** A lightweight tutorial overlay requiring minimal memory, avoiding the "cartoon house" overhead.
3. **Hardware bundle:** Partner with manufacturers to preload simplified interfaces on new high-end machines rather than selling a separate $100 box.

The proposal fails the fit test. It solves a real problem (usability) with a solution that contradicts the constraints (memory, cost, timing). The cost of delay is low because the native OS update is imminent; rushing this product cannibalizes future integration opportunities. The pre-mortem is clear: six months post-launch, sales are flat because the hardware base is too small, and the product is perceived as childish by the mass market.

There is no reversible path to success here; the development sunk cost for a niche, high-requirement product with an expiration date (the new OS) is unjustifiable. The leading metric would be "install base compatibility," which is currently near zero.

DECISION: NO-GO
