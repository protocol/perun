First call: NO-GO. The unit economics fail immediately: paying theaters full ticket price (approx. $10–$15) while charging members $10/month for unlimited viewing guarantees a loss on every active user, regardless of volume.

Steelman: Aggressively subsidize membership to achieve monopoly scale, leveraging new owner capital to withstand losses until data monetization and studio deals create profitability.

Alternatives:
1. Do nothing: Avoids certain cash burn; preserves capital for viable models.
2. Tiered pricing: Charge enough to cover average usage, limiting growth but ensuring solvency.
3. Revenue share: Negotiate discounted bulk rates with theaters before launching; impossible at proposed price point without prior leverage.

Fit: Assumes "growth at all costs" overrides basic arithmetic. No strategy can monetize data or deals if the core transaction loses money on every unit sold.

Pre-mortem: The service launches, heavy users attend 3+ films monthly. The company pays $30+ in ticket costs against $10 revenue. Cash reserves deplete rapidly. Theater chains, seeing no margin for themselves and bearing capacity strain, refuse cooperation or demand higher rates. Data revenue proves insufficient to cover the massive operational deficit. The company collapses before reaching scale.

Evidence: The proposal admits the company pays "full ticket price" while charging "$10... for one film a day." A single ticket exceeds the monthly revenue. The "new revenue streams" are explicitly labeled "unproven." Risks raised confirm theater refusal and negative unit economics.

Blockers:
1. Negative Unit Economics: Cost per user (> $10 for one ticket) exceeds revenue ($10) even at minimum usage. This is a mathematical impossibility to sustain, not a risk.
2. Unproven Revenue: Reliance on speculative future income to cover immediate, guaranteed losses.
3. Supply Side Friction: Theater chains have no incentive to cooperate at full price while risking their own pricing integrity.

Risks: Capital depletion speed, regulatory scrutiny on predatory pricing, failure to secure studio data deals.

The plan requires paying more for a product than the selling price, relying on unproven future miracles to close the gap. This is not a business strategy; it is a liquidation event.

DECISION: NO-GO
