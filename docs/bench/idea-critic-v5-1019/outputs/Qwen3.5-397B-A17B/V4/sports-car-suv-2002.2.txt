First call: GO. A high-margin luxury SUV leverages brand equity to fund core sports-car development, a necessary evolution for a small manufacturer facing scale limits.

Steelman: The crossover is not a betrayal but a financial engine; it monetizes the brand's performance heritage in a growing luxury segment while sharing R&D costs with a partner, securing the company's independence and funding future pure-sports models.

Options:
1. **Proposed SUV:** High capex, high margin, expands TAM, reversible via limited production run. Beats status quo by generating cash flow unavailable elsewhere.
2. **Do Nothing:** Safe brand image, but stagnant revenue risks insolvency or forced acquisition as margins on low-volume sports cars compress.
3. **License Brand Only:** Lower risk, but cedes control over product quality and customer experience, potentially diluting brand value faster than a controlled launch.

Fit: Assumes the north star is "sustainable independence as a performance brand," not merely "making only low-slung cars." If the goal is survival to build future sports cars, this fits.

Pre-mortem: Failure occurs if the vehicle drives poorly, violating the core brand promise of performance, or if the design is too generic, alienating enthusiasts without attracting luxury buyers. This is a two-way door; production can be halted if initial reception is hostile, though financial damage would linger.

Evidence: The proposal relies on the assumption that "off-road ability" and "powerful engine" align with the brand's performance DNA. The risk of "betrayal" is real but manageable if the marketing frames the SUV as the ultimate all-weather performance machine, not a family hauler. The high first-year cost is a blocker only if the company lacks the liquidity or partner support confirmed in the "co-developed" clause.

Blockers: NONE. The risks cited (brand betrayal, cost, image, competition) are execution challenges, not factual impossibilities. The "betrayal" risk is mitigated by the loyal following's desire for the brand to survive. The "strong incumbents" are irrelevant if the niche is "performance-first luxury," not "general luxury."

Risks: Brand dilution if quality slips; cannibalization of future sports car sales (unlikely given different use cases).

Kill Criteria: If pre-orders from existing customers are below 15% of the first-year run, or if professional reviews rate driving dynamics below "competitive with entry-level sports sedans," halt marketing expansion and limit production to the initial batch.

DECISION: GO
