First call: GO. The proposal converts a historical failure (expensive, standalone TV devices) into a low-friction experiment by leveraging existing user hardware (phones/PCs) and minimizing upfront capital risk via a cheap dongle.

Steelman: A minimalist, sub-$50 HDMI dongle acts as a receiver for cast-enabled devices, bypassing the need for complex UIs, remotes, or immediate app ecosystem density, thereby testing TV demand at near-zero marginal risk.

Alternatives analysis confirms this approach dominates "do nothing" (which cedes the living room) and "full device" (which previously failed with poor sales). A full-featured box requires high margin to sustain inventory and support, whereas this dongle functions as a loss-leader probe. The "no remote" constraint is a feature, not a bug, forcing reliance on the superior interfaces users already possess.

Pre-mortem: The initiative fails if users cannot discover the casting function or if network latency renders playback unwatchable. However, these are execution risks, not strategic blockers. The cost of delay is high; competitors could lock in living room real estate with their own ecosystems.

Evidence check: The proposal admits low margins and sparse initial apps. These are accepted constraints for a market probe, not contradictions. The historical data on poor device sales supports the pivot to a minimalistic receiver rather than refuting it.

Blockers: NONE. The stated risks (confusion, margin, app count) are inherent to the "cheap probe" strategy and do not invalidate the core logic. Confusion is mitigated by the simplicity of the "cast" paradigm; margin is sacrificed for market entry; apps will follow if the user base materializes.

Risks: Low adoption due to lack of consumer awareness regarding casting technology; potential churn if the experience is laggy. These become kill criteria: if activation rates remain below a viable threshold within six months, the project halts.

This is a classic two-way door decision with capped downside. The strategy correctly identifies that the barrier to TV entry was previously the device complexity and cost, not the lack of a dedicated remote or menu.

DECISION: GO
