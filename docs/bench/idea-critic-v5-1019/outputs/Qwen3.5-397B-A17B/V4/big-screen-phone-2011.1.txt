First call: GO. The market lacks a high-end device bridging the phone-tablet gap, and screen real estate is a defensible premium differentiator despite skepticism.

Steelman: A large-screen stylus device creates a new "phablet" category, capturing power users underserved by 4-inch screens while commanding a premium price that offsets cannibalization risks.

Options analysis: Doing nothing cedes the emerging large-screen segment to competitors. A tablet-only approach misses the always-connected use case. A small phone with software scaling fails to deliver the productivity benefits of native screen real estate. The proposed hybrid offers unique value no existing alternative matches.

Fit: Aligns with the vision of expanding mobile utility beyond communication into productivity. Assumes users prioritize screen size over one-handed ergonomics, a trade-off early adopters often accept for capability.

Pre-mortem: Failure occurs if reviewers successfully frame the size as "awkward" rather than "spacious," or if stylus input feels like a legacy gimmick rather than a precision tool. However, this is a two-way door; the company can iterate on size or marketing without irreversible damage. The cost of delay is losing first-mover advantage in a potentially defining category.

Evidence: The proposal relies on the observable gap between 4-inch phones and tablets. No internal data contradicts the existence of this gap. Risks regarding reviewer sentiment and cannibalization are speculative uncertainties, not factual blockers. Cannibalization is preferable to losing customers to competitors entirely.

Blockers: NONE. No fact presented proves the device cannot succeed or that the market explicitly rejects large screens; opposition is based on convention, not data.
Risks: Reviewer backlash, niche appeal, internal sales shift. These are managed by positioning the device as premium and distinct, not by abandoning the concept.

The strongest attack—that the device is too big for a phone and too small for a tablet—fails because it assumes rigid category definitions that consumers may not share. History shows categories blur when utility increases. The lack of a stylus demand is an assumption, not a proven negative; the device creates its own demand by enabling new use cases.

DECISION: GO
