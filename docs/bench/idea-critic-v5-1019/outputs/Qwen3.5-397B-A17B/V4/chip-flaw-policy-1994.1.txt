First call: NO-GO. The proposal attempts to manage a reputational crisis with a technical statistic, ignoring the psychological reality of trust.

**Steelman:** Limiting replacements to provably affected users minimizes immediate financial loss while technically adhering to the low probability of error occurrence for the average user.

**Attack:** The core failure is the burden of proof placed on the customer. Users cannot easily demonstrate the flaw's impact on their specific workloads, rendering the "proof" requirement a de facto denial of service. This transforms a technical glitch into an adversarial relationship. While the error rate is low (once in thousands of years), the *perception* of a defect in a precision instrument destroys the brand's value proposition entirely. The cost of reputation loss and market share erosion from appearing to hide a defect vastly exceeds the one-time cost of a blanket replacement program. The "do nothing" or "limited replacement" options fail because they do not address the loss of confidence, which is the actual business risk.

**Pre-mortem:** Assume the strategy fails. Why? Because competitors will highlight the company's reluctance to stand behind its product, and high-profile users will publicly share their inability to "prove" the bug, creating a narrative of corporate deceit. The delay in resolving the issue allows negative sentiment to compound.

**Blockers:**
1.  **Verification Impossibility:** The proposal requires users to prove an effect that is statistically rare and technically difficult to isolate, creating an insurmountable barrier to the offered remedy.
2.  **Reputational Contradiction:** Stating the flaw is "not serious" while simultaneously acknowledging it exists contradicts the premium nature of the product, signaling that the company values cost savings over integrity.

**Risks:** High financial cost of full replacement; however, this is a known quantity, unlike the unbounded risk of brand destruction.

**Kill Criteria:** If public sentiment turns negative within two weeks or if major enterprise clients threaten to switch vendors, the limited policy must be immediately abandoned for a full recall. Since the current proposal guarantees these triggers by design, it cannot proceed.

DECISION: NO-GO
