First call: NO-GO. Launching a multi-billion dollar aircraft program on a single order of 34 units is financially insolvent; the revenue cannot cover the non-recurring engineering costs before further sales are secured.

Steelman: Securing eight airlines early creates a perfectly market-fit product that dominates the mid-size wide-body niche, using the initial order as a catalyst for regulatory approval and further sales.

Options:
1. Do nothing: Preserve capital, but cede the market gap to the rival currently building new models.
2. Seek more orders: Delay launch to secure commitments from the other seven airlines, reducing unit cost and risk.
3. Proceed as proposed: High risk of bankruptcy if the single customer cancels or if regulators deny twin-engine overwater certification.

Fit: Assuming the goal is market leadership, this move gambles the entire company on unproven regulatory outcomes and insufficient capitalization.

Pre-mortem: The program fails because regulatory approval for long-haul twin-engine flight is delayed by five years, or the sole launch customer defaults, leaving the manufacturer with an unfinished, unfunded project while the rival captures the market. The cost of delay is high, but the cost of failure is existential.

Evidence: The proposal admits regulatory approval "has not been granted." Historical data (implied) suggests certification timelines are unpredictable. A single order of 34 units is mathematically insufficient to amortize development costs for an all-new airframe.

Blockers:
1. Financial insufficiency: One order (34 units) cannot fund the development of an all-new aircraft family; the business case collapses without immediate additional commitments.
2. Regulatory uncertainty: The core value proposition (long-haul efficiency) relies on certification that does not yet exist and is not guaranteed.

Risks: Design creep from eight airlines, rival acceleration, cancellation by the launch partner.

The plan fails the basic test of financial viability and risk mitigation. It bets the company on a regulatory change and a single customer's solvency. The correct path is to secure more launch commitments or provisional regulatory guidance before committing to full-scale development.

DECISION: NO-GO
