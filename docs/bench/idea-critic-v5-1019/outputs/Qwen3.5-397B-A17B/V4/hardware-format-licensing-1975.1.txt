First call: NO-GO. The proposal ignores the fundamental dynamics of format wars where compatibility and content availability outweigh marginal technical superiority.

Steelman: Proprietary control preserves margin and quality perception, betting that early lead and superior picture will lock in consumers before the rival scales.

Attack: The "better picture" advantage is negated if the device cannot record a full movie or sports event, which are primary use cases for home video. A one-hour limit is a functional disqualifier for the mass market. Furthermore, restricting licenses while a rival floods the market with cheap players ensures the rival achieves network effects first. Pre-recorded title availability correlates directly with installed base; fewer manufacturers mean fewer titles, creating a death spiral for the proprietary format. The proposal relies on technical merit in a market driven by ecosystem size and utility.

Alternatives:
1. Do nothing (status quo): Lose the format war as the rival becomes the de facto standard.
2. Open licensing immediately: Sacrifice short-term per-unit margin to maximize installed base, secure pre-recorded content deals, and undercut rival pricing through competition among manufacturers. This matches the rival's strategy but leverages the existing early lead.
3. Hybrid: License broadly but charge a royalty, prioritizing speed to market over total control.

Fit: The goal is market dominance, not just selling high-quality units. A proprietary approach with limited capacity (1 hour) and limited distribution contradicts the north star of becoming the standard.

Pre-mortem: In 1980, the proprietary format is a niche product for enthusiasts, while the rival's long-tape, widely-licensed format is in every rental store and living room. The company has lost the war due to lack of content and high hardware prices.

Blockers:
1. One-hour tape capacity fails the core user requirement of recording full movies/events.
2. Restricted licensing cedes the installed base advantage to the rival, preventing the ecosystem growth necessary for pre-recorded content.

Risks: Price erosion if licensed broadly; loss of brand exclusivity. These are secondary to the existential risk of irrelevance.

Kill criteria: If pre-recorded title count for the proprietary format is <50% of the rival's within 12 months, pivot to licensing or exit hardware. Leading metric: Installed base growth rate vs. rival.

DECISION: NO-GO
