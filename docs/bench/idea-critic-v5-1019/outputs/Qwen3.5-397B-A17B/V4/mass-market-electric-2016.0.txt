First call: NO-GO. The proposal asks a company with negligible mass-production experience to execute a massive scale-up in volume and supply chain complexity within one year, while relying on unproven demand at a low-margin price point.

Steelman: The company can leverage early adopter hype to fund a gigafactory and achieve economies of scale before competitors react, using refundable deposits as a risk-free demand signal.

Alternatives:
1. Do nothing: Preserve cash, avoid bankruptcy risk from overextension, but cede the mass market to incumbents.
2. Incremental scale: Increase production of existing expensive models to fund gradual R&D for a cheaper car later.
3. Partnership: License technology to an established manufacturer to handle volume while retaining brand control.

Fit: Assumes the goal is rapid mass adoption over financial stability. A sound idea must move the north star of sustainable profitability; this plan jeopardizes the company's existence to chase it.

Pre-mortem: Production hell ensues because supply chains for batteries cannot be built in 12 months by a novice team. Refundable deposits surge then collapse when delays occur, leaving zero revenue but massive fixed costs. The low margin offers no buffer for errors, leading to insolvency.

Evidence: The proposal admits zero experience in mass production and sharp supply scaling requirements. Historical data for hardware startups shows that jumping from low-volume prototypes to high-volume mass market is the primary cause of failure ("production hell"). The financial math relies on assumptions (conversion rate, supply cost) that are unverified and critical.

Blockers:
1. Capability Gap: The company has never produced at the proposed volume; the plan assumes this capability can be instantiated in one year without evidence.
2. Financial Fragility: Low margins combined with unproven supply scaling create a single-point failure mode where any delay causes immediate cash burn without revenue coverage.

Risks: Deposit conversion rates, battery supply constraints, competitor response.

The lack of a proven path from "two expensive vehicles" to "mass production" is a factual blocker, not merely a risk. The plan fails as proposed because it assumes the solution to its biggest constraint (manufacturing capability) is simply willing it into existence.

DECISION: NO-GO
