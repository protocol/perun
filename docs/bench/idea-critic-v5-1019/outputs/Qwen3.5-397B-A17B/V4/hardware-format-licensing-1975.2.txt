First call: NO-GO. The proposal ignores basic market dynamics where compatibility and content availability outweigh marginal technical superiority in consumer electronics.

**Steelman:** Proprietary control preserves margins and brand prestige while leveraging a temporary quality lead to secure a premium niche before rivals catch up.

**Attack:**
1.  **Content & Utility Gap:** A one-hour tape cannot record a standard movie or full sports event. Consumers will reject a format that requires swapping tapes mid-movie, regardless of picture quality. The rival's longer tapes solve the primary use case.
2.  **Network Effects:** The rival's strategy of broad licensing creates a faster install base of players. Retailers and studios will prioritize the format with more users and cheaper hardware. A proprietary wall limits player availability, keeping prices high and adoption low.
3.  **Software Barrier:** Pre-recorded titles drive hardware sales. Fewer licensed manufacturers means less incentive for studios to produce content for this format. Without a critical mass of movies, the hardware is worthless.

**Alternatives:**
*   **Do Nothing/Status Quo:** Lose the format war entirely as the rival becomes the de facto standard (VHS scenario).
*   **Broad Licensing:** Sacrifice some per-unit margin to flood the market with players, secure studio content deals, and establish the format as the industry standard before the rival does.

**Pre-mortem:** Five years from now, this format is obsolete. Stores only stock the rival's tapes. The company is stuck with unsold inventory of proprietary players and tapes, having lost the war for standards because they prioritized control over adoption. The "better picture" is irrelevant if no one can buy a player or rent a movie.

**Blockers:**
*   **Utility Failure:** One-hour capacity fundamentally fails the core user job (recording a movie/event).
*   **Distribution Chokehold:** Restrictive licensing guarantees higher prices and lower market penetration compared to the rival's open strategy.

**Risks:** Broad licensing might dilute brand premium, but this is secondary to surviving the format war.

**Decision Logic:** The proposal relies on technical specs winning a network-effect battle. History and economics dictate that the format with broader content, longer utility, and wider hardware availability wins. The current plan guarantees failure on all three counts.

DECISION: NO-GO
