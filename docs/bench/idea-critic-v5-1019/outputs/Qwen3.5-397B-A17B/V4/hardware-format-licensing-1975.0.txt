First call: NO-GO. The proposal ignores the fundamental dynamics of format wars where compatibility and content availability outweigh marginal technical superiority.

Steelman: Proprietary control preserves brand prestige and margins while leveraging a temporary picture-quality lead to capture early adopters before a rival floods the market.

Attack: The "better picture" advantage is negligible to consumers compared to the inability to record full-length movies or access a wide library of pre-recorded titles. By restricting licensing, the proposal intentionally limits the install base of players. In a format war, the value of a format is directly proportional to the number of users and available content (network effects). The rival's strategy of broad licensing will rapidly drive down hardware prices and increase content production, creating a self-reinforcing cycle that renders the superior-but-scarce format obsolete. History shows that in such conflicts (e.g., VHS vs. Betamax), the open, content-rich format almost always defeats the closed, technically superior one.

Options:
1. **Proprietary (Proposed):** High margin per unit, but likely tiny market share and eventual irrelevance.
2. **Broad Licensing:** Sacrifices some per-unit margin to maximize install base, ensuring content availability and establishing the standard.
3. **Do Nothing/Wait:** Cedes the entire market to the rival immediately.

Fit: The goal is market leadership, not just selling a premium niche product. A standard cannot be established by a few partners.

Pre-mortem: Two years from now, the rival's format is in every rental store and living room. Our superior players sit unsold because no movies are available for them, and consumers refuse to buy a machine that can't record a whole football game.

Evidence: The proposal admits the rival is signing many makers and that our tape length is insufficient for primary use cases (movies/sports). These are fatal flaws for a consumer format.

Blockers:
1. **Content/Compatibility Gap:** Restricting licenses guarantees fewer pre-recorded titles and higher player prices, directly contradicting the requirements for mass-market adoption.
2. **Usage Mismatch:** One-hour capacity fails the core user need of recording full events, making the "better picture" irrelevant.

Risks: Price erosion if we license broadly; loss of brand exclusivity. These are manageable compared to total market exclusion.

DECISION: NO-GO
