The proposal to embed self-installing copy-protection software on audio discs presents unacceptable risks that outweigh the potential benefit of reducing unauthorized copying. The core mechanism relies on installing software without clear, explicit consent from the user. In the current digital landscape, any software that executes silently upon media playback is functionally indistinguishable from malware. This approach fundamentally violates user trust and autonomy over their own computing environments.

The risks identified in the proposal are not merely theoretical; they are imminent and severe. Treating paying customers as potential thieves by subjecting their systems to intrusive, difficult-to-remove software will almost certainly trigger a massive backlash. Such actions damage brand reputation far more deeply than lost sales from piracy ever could. Once consumers perceive a product as a security threat, they will boycott the brand and potentially pursue legal action.

Furthermore, the reliance on security through obscurity is a flawed strategy. Security researchers will inevitably analyze the software, expose its weaknesses, and publish methods to bypass it or remove it. When these vulnerabilities are revealed, the company will be exposed as distributing unsafe code that compromises system integrity. The difficulty in removing the software exacerbates the situation, turning a customer service issue into a systemic security crisis.

The market in 2005 is increasingly aware of digital rights management overreach. Implementing a solution that prioritizes control over user experience and security is strategically unsound. It invites regulatory scrutiny, encourages the very hacking communities it seeks to deter, and alienates the loyal customer base required for long-term viability. The proposal fails to address the root causes of piracy while introducing significant new liabilities. Proceeding with this plan would be a catastrophic error in judgment, sacrificing long-term brand equity for a short-term, likely ineffective, technical fix. The potential for public relations disaster and legal liability is too high to ignore.

DECISION: NO-GO
