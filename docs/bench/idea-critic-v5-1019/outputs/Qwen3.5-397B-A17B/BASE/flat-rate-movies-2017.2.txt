The proposal suggests slashing the monthly price to roughly ten dollars for unlimited daily films, relying on a new owner's cash subsidies to fuel rapid growth. The strategy hinges on future monetization through data sales and studio deals rather than ticket margins. However, the fundamental unit economics are broken from day one. Paying theaters the full ticket price for every visit while collecting only ten dollars monthly guarantees a loss on every active member, especially heavy users who attend frequently.

The risks identified are severe and immediate. The revenue streams intended to fix this deficit—data and special deals—are explicitly unproven. Banking the entire business model on speculative future income while burning cash on every transaction is financially reckless. Furthermore, theater chains have a clear incentive to refuse cooperation, as this model devalues their product and could cannibalize their own direct sales without offering them a sustainable share of the subscription revenue.

The presence of a cash-rich owner does not validate a flawed operational model; it merely delays the inevitable failure if the core math does not work. Subsidizing a product that costs significantly more to deliver than its price tag allows is not a growth strategy; it is a liquidation strategy disguised as expansion. Without a clear path to profitability that does not rely entirely on unproven ancillary markets or the indefinite benevolence of an investor, proceeding as proposed exposes the company to catastrophic financial loss. The disconnect between the fixed low revenue per user and the variable, potentially high cost per user creates an unsustainable burden that cannot be resolved simply by growing membership numbers.

DECISION: NO-GO
