The proposal presents a fundamentally flawed economic model that ignores basic unit economics. The core offering promises free delivery on small orders with no minimum spend. The risks explicitly stated confirm that the cost of fulfilling these small orders exceeds the profit margin generated by them. This means every single transaction results in a direct financial loss. Scaling this operation by expanding to more cities and hiring more staff directly will not fix this; it will only multiply the losses at a faster rate.

The plan relies on raising venture capital to subsidize these inherent losses while employing a large, direct workforce. This creates a massive fixed-cost structure (salaries, vehicles, city infrastructure) built on a variable-revenue model that loses money on every sale. There is no path to profitability described, nor is there a mechanism to eventually charge for delivery or enforce order minimums. The risk that customers will only use the service while it is subsidized indicates a lack of genuine product-market fit once subsidies are removed.

In 1998, logistics technology and mobile connectivity were insufficient to optimize routes dynamically enough to overcome these cost barriers for one-hour delivery of low-margin items like snacks and books. The combination of high operational complexity, negative unit economics, and total dependence on continuous external funding makes this venture unsustainable. Approving this proposal would lead to rapid capital depletion without building a viable business. The structural defects are too severe to attempt a "revise" phase; the entire premise contradicts the stated risks.

DECISION: NO-GO
