The proposal presents a high-stakes opportunity to capture a specific market segment between two existing wide-body models, leveraging early involvement from eight major airlines. However, the foundational elements required for a successful launch in 1990 are critically deficient. Relying on a single order for 34 aircraft to justify the "very large cost" of developing an all-new aircraft is financially precarious. This volume is insufficient to amortize development expenses or sustain a production line, especially given the inherent risks of designing a clean-sheet aircraft.

The most immediate and potentially fatal obstacle is the regulatory environment. The proposal explicitly acknowledges that aviation authorities have not yet granted approval for twin-engine jets to operate on long overwater routes. Without this certification, the aircraft's primary operational advantage and market viability are nullified. Launching a program before securing this regulatory framework exposes the company to indefinite delays and the possibility that the aircraft cannot perform its intended mission profile upon completion.

Furthermore, the strategy of co-designing with eight airlines introduces significant scope creep. While customer input is valuable, managing conflicting requirements from multiple carriers during the design phase frequently leads to engineering changes, schedule slippage, and cost overruns. Combined with the presence of a rival actively building new models, the window for error is non-existent. Proceeding now, without confirmed regulatory clearance, a robust order book beyond one carrier, and a stabilized design scope, invites catastrophic financial exposure. The risks overwhelmingly outweigh the potential rewards at this specific juncture. The program cannot be approved in its current state due to the unresolved regulatory barrier and insufficient commercial commitment.

DECISION: NO-GO
