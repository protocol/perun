The proposal suggests slashing the monthly price to approximately ten dollars for unlimited daily access while the company pays theaters the full ticket price for every visit. Even with a new owner willing to subsidize operations initially, the fundamental unit economics are broken. If a member watches just three films in a month, the company likely pays out more than thirty dollars in ticket costs alone, excluding operational overhead, against a ten-dollar revenue stream. This creates an immediate, steep loss on every active user.

The plan relies entirely on future, unproven revenue streams from data monetization and studio deals to eventually offset these massive operating losses. History shows that building a massive user base on a loss-leading model does not guarantee future profitability, especially when the core service costs more to deliver the more it is used. The risk of "heavy users" is not just a possibility; it is a mathematical certainty in an unlimited usage model priced this low.

Furthermore, the proposal admits that theater chains may refuse to cooperate. If major exhibitors reject the terms because the subsidy model threatens their own pricing structures or profitability, the service loses its inventory immediately. Without confirmed partnerships, the subscription has no product to sell. Relying on speculative future deals to fix a present-day cash bleed is financially reckless. The gap between the fixed low revenue per user and the variable high cost per ticket cannot be bridged by hope alone. The current structure guarantees that rapid membership growth will only accelerate financial hemorrhaging without a clear, verified path to solvency.

DECISION: NO-GO
