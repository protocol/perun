The proposal outlines a direct-to-consumer model for assembling and selling IBM-compatible computers at prices below established brands. The core strategy relies on incorporating the entity, maintaining a strict direct-order channel, and funding growth exclusively through generated revenue. This approach eliminates the capital expenditure associated with physical retail locations and third-party reseller margins, directly enabling the promised lower price point.

The risks identified are significant but not insurmountable given the specific market context of 1984. While buyers traditionally prefer seeing products before purchase, the substantial cost savings offered here create a compelling value proposition that can overcome hesitation, particularly for knowledgeable early adopters and small businesses. The lack of a physical service network is a valid concern; however, this can be mitigated through clear warranty terms, mail-in repair protocols, and the inherent modularity of IBM-compatible systems which allows for easier third-party servicing.

Regarding supply chain constraints, the inability to secure parts on credit initially is a typical hurdle for new ventures. The proposal's directive to "grow from revenue" explicitly acknowledges this limitation, suggesting a bootstrap strategy where initial sales fund subsequent inventory purchases. This reduces financial exposure and prevents over-leverage. The dominance of established brands with large sales channels presents competition, yet their higher overhead costs inherently limit their ability to match the proposed pricing structure. By targeting price-sensitive customers who prioritize value over brand prestige or hands-on inspection, the business carves out a viable niche. The direct model also allows for tighter inventory control and faster adaptation to component price fluctuations compared to traditional retailers.

The plan is lean, focused, and directly addresses the primary barrier to entry in the personal computer market: high cost. The risks are operational rather than existential and can be managed through customer education and disciplined cash flow management. The potential reward of disrupting the market with affordable computing power outweighs the challenges of establishing trust without a physical storefront.

DECISION: GO
