The proposal presents a fundamentally flawed business model that misaligns with consumer expectations and market realities. The core value proposition relies on restricting access to content after an initial 48-hour window, forcing customers to pay additional fees for continued viewing. This approach directly contradicts the established consumer preference for ownership or unlimited rental periods, creating immediate friction and likely resistance.

Furthermore, the hardware requirements create a significant barrier to entry. Requiring customers to purchase specialized players that are more expensive than standard units, while offering no backward compatibility with existing discs, isolates the product from the broader market. The necessity for these players to connect to a phone line adds further complexity and potential privacy concerns for users, reducing appeal.

The success of this initiative is entirely dependent on the simultaneous cooperation of two distinct and powerful industries: movie studios and electronics manufacturers. Securing unanimous agreement from studios to support a restrictive new format, while convincing electronics makers to produce costly, proprietary hardware, represents a monumental coordination challenge. The risk of fragmentation is high; if either group hesitates, the entire ecosystem collapses before launch.

Given the high cost of adoption for consumers, the unproven demand for time-limited viewing fees, and the extreme difficulty in aligning multiple external partners around a restrictive standard, the probability of commercial failure is substantial. The market conditions in 1998 favor convenience and compatibility, neither of which this proposal offers. Proceeding now would expose the company to significant financial loss and reputational damage without a clear path to profitability. The risks overwhelmingly outweigh the potential benefits.

DECISION: NO-GO
