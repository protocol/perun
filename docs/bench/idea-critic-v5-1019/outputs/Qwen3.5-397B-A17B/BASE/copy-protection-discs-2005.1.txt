The proposal to embed self-installing copy-protection software on audio discs in 2005 presents unacceptable risks that outweigh potential sales protection. The core mechanism relies on installing software without clear, explicit user consent. In the digital landscape of 2005, users are increasingly vigilant about system integrity and unauthorized modifications. Software that installs silently upon disc insertion will almost certainly be classified as malware or spyware by security vendors and the public, regardless of the vendor's intent.

The risk of security weaknesses is critical. Any software designed to intercept system calls and restrict user actions creates a new attack surface. If security researchers or malicious actors exploit these vulnerabilities, the record company becomes liable for compromising millions of consumer computers. The reputational damage from such a breach would far exceed losses from piracy. Furthermore, making the software difficult to remove violates basic principles of consumer trust and system ownership. Treating paying customers as potential thieves by subjecting them to intrusive, persistent software will likely trigger a severe backlash, including boycotts, class-action lawsuits, and negative media coverage.

History shows that consumers reject technologies that compromise their control over personal devices. The proposed method ignores the necessity of transparent installation processes and easy uninstallation. Instead of securing revenue, this approach invites regulatory scrutiny and destroys brand loyalty. The technical execution described is fundamentally flawed because it prioritizes restriction over user experience and security. Implementing this strategy now would be a strategic error, likely resulting in immediate public relations crises and long-term legal entanglements. The proposal fails to address the legitimate concerns regarding consent, removability, and system safety. Therefore, the project cannot proceed in its current form.

DECISION: NO-GO
