The proposal presents an extremely high-risk strategy for a company with minimal operational history. The manufacturer has sold only two expensive vehicles and lacks any experience in mass production. The plan demands an immediate leap to far higher volumes the following year, ignoring the profound complexities of scaling manufacturing processes, supply chains, and quality control.

The reliance on scaling battery supply sharply introduces a critical bottleneck. Battery production is capital-intensive and technically difficult; failing to secure this supply would halt the entire operation. Furthermore, the financial model is fragile. A lower price point leaves little margin for error, meaning any production delays or cost overruns could be catastrophic.

The strategy depends heavily on refundable deposits to gauge demand and fund operations. However, refundable commitments do not guarantee actual sales. If the product is delayed or fails to meet expectations, customers can easily withdraw funds, leaving the company with no revenue despite having incurred massive setup costs. There is no evidence of a secured order book or non-refundable commitments to ensure cash flow.

Attempting to transition from a niche, low-volume producer of expensive cars to a mass-market manufacturer of cheap vehicles in a single year is operationally unrealistic. The gap between current capabilities and proposed targets is too wide to bridge without intermediate steps, pilot programs, or proven supply chain contracts. The combination of unproven production capacity, uncertain supply lines, thin margins, and non-binding customer interest creates a scenario where failure is highly probable. Approving this now would expose the organization to unacceptable financial and reputational damage.

DECISION: NO-GO
