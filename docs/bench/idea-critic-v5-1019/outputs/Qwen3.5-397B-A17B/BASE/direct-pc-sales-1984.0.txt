The proposal outlines a direct-to-consumer model for assembling and selling IBM-compatible computers at prices below established brands. The core strategy relies on incorporating the entity, maintaining a strict direct-order channel, and funding growth exclusively through generated revenue. This approach eliminates the capital expenditure associated with physical retail locations and third-party reseller margins, directly enabling the promised lower price point.

The risks identified are significant but manageable within this specific context. While buyers traditionally prefer seeing products before purchase, the technical nature of early IBM-compatible systems suggests a customer base that is already informed and specification-driven. The lack of a physical showroom is offset by the substantial cost savings passed to the consumer. Regarding the dominance of established makers, their large sales channels represent high overhead costs that this model intentionally bypasses to gain a competitive pricing advantage.

The absence of a proprietary service network is a valid concern; however, the modular nature of IBM-compatible parts allows for third-party repair or mail-in service models without requiring a widespread physical footprint. Finally, the challenge of securing parts on credit for a new entity without assets is mitigated by the proposal's commitment to grow strictly from revenue. This bootstrapping method avoids debt accumulation and forces disciplined inventory management, ensuring that expansion is funded by actual market demand rather than speculative borrowing.

The year 1984 presents a unique window where the standardization of the IBM architecture allows a small assembler to compete directly with giants on price and configuration flexibility. The direct sales model aligns perfectly with the emerging trend of knowledgeable corporate and enthusiast buyers who prioritize value and specs over brand prestige or physical interaction. The risks are inherent to any market entry but are structurally addressed by the low-overhead, cash-flow-positive operational design. The proposal offers a viable path to disrupt the market by leveraging efficiency against incumbent inertia.

DECISION: GO
