The proposal to maintain a proprietary format with limited licensing in 1975 carries unacceptable strategic risks. While the current device offers superior picture quality and a compact design, these technical advantages are outweighed by critical market dynamics favoring the rival's approach. The competitor's strategy of broad licensing will rapidly expand the installed base of players, driving down consumer prices and creating a network effect that this proposal ignores.

A one-hour recording capacity is a fatal flaw for a home video product. Consumers require the ability to record full-length movies and major sporting events, which typically exceed sixty minutes. Without this utility, the product fails to meet primary user needs regardless of image clarity. Furthermore, restricting manufacturing partners limits the potential volume of hardware sales and, more critically, reduces the incentive for content studios to release pre-recorded titles in this specific format. A smaller library of available movies further diminishes the value proposition for early adopters.

History in format wars demonstrates that widespread availability and content libraries often triumph over marginal technical superiority. By choosing exclusivity, the company risks isolating its technology, allowing the rival format to become the de facto industry standard through sheer market penetration. Once the competitor establishes dominance in both hardware units and software titles, reversing the trend will be nearly impossible, rendering the superior picture quality irrelevant. The risk of obsolescence is too high to proceed with the current plan. The strategy must shift toward broader compatibility or extended recording times before any launch can be considered viable. Approving this now would likely result in significant financial loss and market irrelevance.

DECISION: NO-GO
