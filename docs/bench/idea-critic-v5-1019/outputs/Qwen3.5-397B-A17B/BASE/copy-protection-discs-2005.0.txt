The proposal to embed self-installing copy-protection software on audio discs presents unacceptable risks that outweigh potential sales protection. In 2005, the computing landscape is increasingly sensitive to unauthorized system modifications. Software that installs without explicit, clear consent violates the fundamental trust between a vendor and a consumer. This approach treats legitimate customers as adversaries, creating immediate hostility among the very demographic the company relies on for revenue.

The technical implications are severe. Software designed to be difficult to remove inherently compromises system stability and security. If security researchers identify weaknesses in this protection layer, as is highly probable, the resulting vulnerabilities could expose users to malicious attacks unrelated to music copying. The record company would then be directly responsible for damaging customer computers, leading to potential legal liability and catastrophic reputational damage.

Furthermore, the perception of intrusiveness cannot be overstated. Users expect control over their hardware. A mechanism that operates covertly to restrict usage rights will likely trigger a backlash far more damaging than current piracy rates. The optical disc market is fragile; introducing a product known to degrade computer performance or privacy will drive consumers toward alternative, non-protected media formats or digital sources that do not compromise their systems.

The risks of being labeled a purveyor of malware are too high. Once trust is broken in this manner, it is nearly impossible to regain. The potential for negative media coverage, class-action lawsuits, and a permanent brand stain makes this strategy untenable. Protecting intellectual property is necessary, but not at the expense of user security and autonomy. This specific method of enforcement is flawed in both execution and ethics. Proceeding now invites disaster.

DECISION: NO-GO
