The proposal to maintain a proprietary format with limited licensing in 1975 carries unacceptable strategic risk. While the current device offers superior picture quality and an early market entry, these advantages are insufficient against the rival's strategy of broad licensing. History in consumer electronics demonstrates that market dominance is often determined by availability and price, not solely by technical superiority. By restricting licenses to a few partners, production volumes will remain low, preventing the cost reductions necessary to compete with the rival's likely lower prices.

The one-hour tape limitation is a critical functional flaw. Consumers require the ability to record full-length movies and major sporting events, which typically exceed sixty minutes. A format that cannot accommodate these primary use cases will be rejected by the mass market, regardless of image clarity. Furthermore, a restricted manufacturing base will inevitably result in a smaller library of pre-recorded titles. Content availability is a decisive factor for consumers; if the rival format secures more movie deals due to its wider hardware installation base, the proprietary format will become obsolete.

The risk of the rival's format becoming the industry standard is imminent. Once a critical mass of manufacturers adopts the competing open format, network effects will lock out any closed system. Relying on an early lead without securing widespread adoption allows the rival to overtake the market quickly. The combination of shorter recording time, higher costs due to limited manufacturing, and potential content scarcity creates a path to failure. To survive, the company must pivot immediately to an open licensing model and extend tape capacity, rather than proceeding with the current restrictive plan. Approving this proposal as written guarantees loss of market relevance.

DECISION: NO-GO
