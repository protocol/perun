The proposal to maintain a proprietary format with limited licensing in 1975 ignores critical market dynamics favoring widespread adoption. While superior picture quality and an early lead offer initial advantages, the one-hour tape limitation is a fatal flaw for consumers wishing to record full movies or sports events. This functional deficit alone significantly reduces the product's appeal compared to competitors offering longer recording times.

Furthermore, the strategy of restricting licenses to a few partners directly contradicts the rival's approach of broad licensing to numerous manufacturers. History in consumer electronics demonstrates that formats with wider manufacturing support achieve faster price reductions, greater market penetration, and broader availability of pre-recorded content. By limiting production partners, the company risks higher unit costs, slower inventory scaling, and a scarce library of available titles.

The risk that the rival's format becomes the industry standard is substantial. If the competitor successfully floods the market with affordable players and longer tapes, they will likely define consumer expectations. A proprietary ecosystem with fewer manufacturers cannot compete against a coalition of rivals driving down prices and expanding content libraries. Consumers typically prioritize recording duration and content availability over marginal gains in picture quality, especially when the price difference is significant.

Relying solely on technical superiority without addressing format length and distribution scale is a strategic error. The current plan invites the rival to capture the mass market, leaving this company with a niche, expensive product lacking sufficient content support. To avoid obsolescence, the strategy must shift toward open licensing and extended tape capacity immediately. As it stands, the proposal fails to mitigate the primary threats of standardization loss and consumer rejection based on utility.

DECISION: NO-GO
