The proposal presents a revolutionary personal transport device with significant potential to alter urban mobility. However, the current plan contains critical flaws that make immediate approval inadvisable. The pricing strategy is the most immediate barrier; at $5,000, the unit costs significantly more than established alternatives like bicycles, scooters, or even reliable used cars. This price point severely limits the addressable market to early adopters with high disposable income, contradicting the goal of widespread city adoption.

Furthermore, the operational risks are substantial. Requiring users to learn a new balancing skill creates a steep adoption curve that will hinder mass market penetration. Unlike a bicycle or car, which utilize intuitive mechanics for most adults, this device demands training, increasing liability concerns and reducing impulse purchases.

Regulatory uncertainty poses an existential threat. Current laws regarding sidewalks and roads do not account for this specific type of vehicle. Launching without clarified legal status invites immediate bans, fines, or confiscation, potentially halting operations before they begin. Finally, the utility gap is wide; the inability to carry cargo or a passenger removes it from many practical daily use cases where cars or larger bikes excel.

Approving this proposal as-is ignores these fundamental market, legal, and usability hurdles. A successful launch requires a revised strategy that addresses pricing, perhaps through leasing or lower-cost models, secures regulatory approval in pilot cities, and develops a structured user onboarding program. Without these adjustments, the risk of commercial failure and legal entanglement is too high. The executive team cannot authorize a full-scale launch under these conditions.

DECISION: NO-GO
