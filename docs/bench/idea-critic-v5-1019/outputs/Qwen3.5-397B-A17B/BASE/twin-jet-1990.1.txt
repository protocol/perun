The proposal presents a high-stakes opportunity to capture a unique market segment between existing wide-body models, leveraging early commitment from eight major carriers. However, the foundational risks are currently too severe to justify an immediate launch based on a single order for 34 aircraft.

The most critical barrier is regulatory. Operating a twin-engine jet on long overwater routes requires specific authority approval that has not yet been granted. Launching a program of this magnitude without confirmed certification creates an unacceptable risk of developing an aircraft that cannot legally fly its intended primary missions. This single factor could render the entire investment futile.

Furthermore, the financial exposure is disproportionate to the current commitment. The development costs are described as "very large," yet the program relies on one airline for its launch viability. While eight airlines are involved in the design process, their participation introduces significant uncertainty regarding scope creep, potential changes, and delays, which could further inflate costs and extend time-to-market. With a rival actively building new models, speed and efficiency are paramount; a design process prone to constant modification jeopardizes competitive timing.

Proceeding now would lock the company into massive expenditures before resolving the fundamental question of airworthiness for the target route structure. The lack of multiple firm orders fails to provide a sufficient financial buffer against the identified technical and scheduling risks. A more prudent path requires securing the necessary regulatory clarity and broadening the order book to share the development burden before committing to full-scale production.

DECISION: NO-GO
