The proposal presents an extreme leap in operational complexity for a company with virtually no proven track record in mass manufacturing. Having sold only two expensive vehicles in small numbers, the organization lacks the fundamental infrastructure, supply chain relationships, and production line experience required to suddenly scale to "far higher volumes." The jump from boutique assembly to mass production is historically one of the most difficult transitions in the automotive industry, often causing severe delays and quality failures even for established giants.

The supply chain risk is particularly acute. Scaling battery supply sharply requires long-term contracts and capital commitments that a company with limited sales history likely cannot secure without significant external backing, which is not mentioned. Without a guaranteed battery supply, production cannot begin, rendering the timeline unrealistic.

Furthermore, the financial model relies heavily on refundable deposits. While this generates cash flow, it creates a significant liability. If the company fails to deliver due to production bottlenecks—a highly probable outcome given the lack of experience—those deposits must be returned, potentially draining resources and destroying brand reputation before the product even launches. The combination of a lower price point and thin margins leaves zero room for error. Any cost overrun or delay will immediately threaten solvency.

The risks identified in the proposal are not merely challenges; they are existential threats. Proceeding without first demonstrating the ability to manufacture at a moderate scale, securing firm battery supply agreements, and validating the cost structure at the new price point is reckless. The gap between current capabilities and proposed execution is too wide to bridge in a single step. Approval should be withheld until a phased scaling plan is presented, proving intermediate production milestones and securing non-refundable supply commitments.

DECISION: NO-GO
