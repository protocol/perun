The proposal suggests acknowledging a floating-point division error in a new processor while limiting replacements only to users who can prove the flaw impacts their specific work. The internal analysis indicates the error occurs extremely rarely, perhaps once every few thousand years for a typical user. However, the risks identified are severe and immediate. Users face a significant burden in demonstrating that their specific calculations were affected by such a rare event, effectively denying most people a remedy for a known defect.

Furthermore, the issue is already gaining widespread public attention. In this environment, appearing to hide behind technical rarity while placing the proof burden on the customer will likely be interpreted as evasive or dishonest. The potential damage to the company's reputation outweighs the immediate financial savings of avoiding a full recall. Trust is difficult to regain once lost, especially when a company is perceived as minimizing a defect that undermines the fundamental reliability of its product.

While the cost of replacing every chip is described as a very large sum, the long-term financial impact of a tarnished brand and lost market confidence could be far greater. The current approach fails to address the core concern of buyer confidence. By requiring proof of impact, the company signals that it prioritizes short-term cost containment over customer assurance. Given the high visibility of the problem and the impracticality for users to validate the error's effect on their work, this strategy is likely to exacerbate the reputational crisis rather than contain it. The proposal does not adequately mitigate the risk of buyers believing the defect is being hidden. Therefore, approving this plan as it stands would be a strategic error.

DECISION: NO-GO
