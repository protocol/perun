First call: GO. The direct-to-consumer model bypasses retailer margins, enabling lower prices that offset the lack of physical presence, a viable trade-off for cost-sensitive buyers in a nascent market.

Steelman: A direct-sales assembler can undercut established brands by eliminating retail markups and inventory overhead, targeting informed buyers who prioritize price and specification over brand prestige or hands-on inspection.

Alternatives:
1. Do nothing: Remain a small unincorporated entity, limiting liability protection and growth capital; loses the first-mover advantage in direct sales.
2. Retail distribution: Increases reach but erodes price advantage via retailer margins and requires upfront inventory costs; high risk of cash flow failure.
3. Hybrid model: Adds complexity and cost without immediate volume; dilutes the core efficiency of the direct model.

Fit: Aligns with a "low-cost provider" strategy. Assumes a segment of buyers exists who trust specifications over physical inspection.

Pre-mortem: Failure occurs if suppliers demand cash-on-delivery, starving operations of parts, or if return rates due to "buyer remorse" (lack of try-before-buy) exceed thin margins. This is a two-way door; incorporation is reversible, and sales channels can be added later if direct sales stall. Cost of delay allows competitors to solidify retail dominance.

Evidence: The proposal relies on the arithmetic of margin elimination. No external data confirms supplier credit terms or return rates; these are assumed risks.

Blockers: NONE. The risks cited (desire to try, service network, supplier credit) are execution hurdles, not fundamental flaws. They do not contradict the core economic logic that lower prices can drive volume without retail. Supplier credit is a standard startup hurdle, not a unique disqualifier.

Risks: High return rates, supply chain interruption, slow brand adoption. These become kill criteria: if return rates exceed 15% or suppliers refuse net-30 terms within six months, the model must pivot to hybrid or cease.

Kill criteria: Halt expansion if cash conversion cycle exceeds 45 days or if return rate surpasses 20% in the first year. Leading metric: Monthly recurring revenue growth vs. return rate.

DECISION: GO
