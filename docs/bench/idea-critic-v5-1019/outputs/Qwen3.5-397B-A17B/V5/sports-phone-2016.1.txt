First call: GO. Beating a rival's September launch to capture the holiday shopping window and media cycle is a sound operational move for a selling flagship line; delay cedes the narrative.

Steelman: Releasing in August with premium differentiators (battery, iris, water resistance) maximizes market share before the competitor's announcement makes current specs look dated.

Alternatives: Waiting until September or October preserves testing time but allows the rival to define the category standard first, likely depressing demand for the existing line. Doing nothing maintains the status quo but risks being perceived as reactive rather than innovative. The August launch beats "do nothing" by securing first-mover advantage in the critical Q4 window.

Fit: This aligns with the goal of maintaining flagship dominance and sales momentum.

Pre-mortem: Failure occurs if the battery swells or the iris scanner fails in mass production, leading to a global recall that destroys brand trust. This is a two-way door in strategy (can launch later) but a one-way door in reputation if defective units ship. The cost of delay is losing the "first to market" headline and Q4 sales volume.

Evidence: The proposal states the line "sells well," confirming demand. The risks (testing time, thin body/large battery tension, untested sensor) are acknowledged but not quantified as show-stoppers.

Blockers: NONE. The risks cited are execution challenges, not fundamental flaws in the strategy. The proposal does not claim the phone is already perfect, only that the *timing* should shift. The risks can be managed via kill criteria rather than stopping the plan.

Risks: Battery safety, sensor reliability, and insufficient testing time. These become the kill criteria: if pilot units fail specific stress tests by July 15, the launch slips. The commitment to "ship in volume worldwide" is the variable to cap; a staged rollout could mitigate global defect risk, but the decision to target August stands.

The strongest attack is that rushing a complex new biometric and battery design into a thinner body invites a catastrophic hardware failure. However, this attack fails to block the plan because the proposal implies standard engineering validation will occur; the risk is manageable through rigorous gating (kill criteria) rather than abandoning the strategic timing advantage. Without evidence that the technology is fundamentally broken, the strategic benefit of beating the rival outweighs the manageable execution risk.

DECISION: GO
