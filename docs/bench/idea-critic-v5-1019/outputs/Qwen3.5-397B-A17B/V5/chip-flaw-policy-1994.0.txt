First call: NO-GO. The proposal attempts to trade a verified technical defect for a reputational gamble based on low statistical probability, ignoring the psychological reality of trust.

**Steelman:** The company should minimize immediate financial outflow by restricting replacements to provably affected users, betting that the error's rarity prevents widespread practical impact.

**Attack:**
1.  **Verification Gap:** The proposal requires users to prove they are affected, yet admits users "cannot easily show" this. This creates an impossible barrier, transforming a warranty issue into an adversarial standoff.
2.  **Incentive Misalignment:** By forcing customers to prove harm for a known manufacturing defect, the company signals that it values cost savings over product integrity. This validates the fear that the company is "hiding a defect."
3.  **Risk Asymmetry:** The cost of replacement is finite and known. The cost of reputational collapse in the enterprise hardware market is existential and unbounded. Once engineers believe a vendor will dispute known silicon errors, they switch architectures permanently.
4.  **Alternatives:** A proactive, no-questions-asked replacement program absorbs the "very large sum" but purchases trust and halts the narrative of concealment. Doing nothing or gating replacements guarantees the reputation risk materializes.

**Pre-mortem:** Six months post-announcement, a major client switches suppliers citing "unacceptable risk tolerance," triggering a sector-wide loss of confidence. The stock tanks far below the cost of the original recall. The "rare" error becomes the defining story of the brand, not the processor's speed.

**Blockers:**
*   **Fact:** Users cannot verify the error themselves.
*   **Contradiction:** The plan relies on user verification while stating it is impossible for them to perform.
*   **Outcome:** The policy guarantees frustration and confirms suspicions of deceit, directly causing the reputational damage it hopes to avoid.

**Risks:** High immediate cash outflow (managed by pricing it as a quality assurance investment rather than a loss).

The proposal fails because it solves an accounting problem while creating a survival problem. You cannot ask customers to audit your silicon when you already know it is flawed.

DECISION: NO-GO
