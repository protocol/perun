First call: NO-GO. The proposal attempts to manage a mathematical defect with a public relations filter, ignoring the erosion of trust in a core engineering promise.

Steelman: Limiting replacements minimizes immediate financial bleed for a statistically negligible error, preserving capital for future R&D while technically honoring warranties for the tiny fraction of impacted users.

Attack: The flaw is not in the frequency of occurrence but in the nature of the product. A processor is purchased as a tool of truth; any known deviation, however rare, invalidates the fundamental value proposition. The proposal fails because it shifts the burden of proof to the customer ("show that their work is affected") when the vendor possesses the definitive data on the defect's existence. In an environment of "wide discussion," requiring users to prove harm signals defensiveness and confirms fears of a cover-up. The reputational damage from appearing to hide a defect outweighs the cost of replacement. The "very large sum" is a known, finite cost; the loss of market confidence is an unbounded liability.

Alternatives:
1. Do nothing: Guarantees a narrative of incompetence and deceit.
2. Full voluntary recall: High immediate cost, but captures the narrative of integrity and control.
3. Proposed plan: Saves money now, risks the brand forever.

Pre-mortem: Six months post-announcement, a major academic institution or media outlet demonstrates the flaw in a visible, embarrassing way. The company is forced into a full recall anyway, but now pays the replacement cost plus massive legal fees and permanent brand stigma for having initially refused responsibility.

Blockers: The proposal relies on the assumption that users can verify the flaw, which contradicts the stated risk that "users cannot easily show that they are affected." This creates an impossible barrier to warranty service, effectively denying valid claims. The strategy treats a binary trust issue as a variable cost problem.

Risks: Financial strain from a full recall. However, this is a manageable operational risk compared to the existential threat of being labeled untrustworthy.

DECISION: NO-GO
