First call: NO-GO. Launching a multi-billion dollar aircraft program on a single order of 34 units is financially insolvent; the revenue cannot cover non-recurring engineering costs, let alone generate profit.

Steelman: Securing eight airlines early creates a perfect market fit, de-risking sales, while a single anchor order provides enough cash flow to start.

Attack:
1.  **Financial Blocker:** 34 aircraft is a fraction of the break-even point for a clean-sheet design. Even at a high unit price, this covers tooling for maybe one year. The "very large cost" implies billions; the order value is likely under $2B. The math fails immediately.
2.  **Regulatory Blocker:** The core value proposition (twin-engine efficiency on long overwater routes) relies on regulatory approval (ETOPS) that does not yet exist. Betting the company on a future rule change is speculation, not strategy. If authorities deny or delay approval, the aircraft is useless for its primary mission.
3.  **Design Risk:** "Designing with airlines" often leads to feature creep, conflicting requirements, and delays. Without a frozen specification, costs will balloon beyond the initial estimate.
4.  **Competition:** A rival is already moving. Waiting for perfect consensus among eight airlines while a competitor launches a "good enough" solution first cedes the market.

Better Way: Secure launch orders from at least three distinct airlines totaling 150+ units to demonstrate market depth and share development risk. Alternatively, delay launch until ETOPS regulations are clarified or provisionally granted. Do not start cutting metal based on a regulatory hope and a single customer.

Pre-mortem: The program launches, costs double due to airline-driven changes, ETOPS approval is delayed by two years, the single anchor airline cancels due to financial distress, and the company faces insolvency with an unfinished, uncertifiable airframe.

Blockers:
-   Order book (34 units) is mathematically insufficient to fund development.
-   Core operational capability depends on ungranted regulatory approval.

Risks:
-   Design delays from committee-based engineering.
-   Competitor capturing market share during development.

DECISION: NO-GO
