First call: NO-GO. The proposal trades long-term brand trust and security for a fragile, easily bypassed technical control, creating massive reputational and legal liability.

**Steelman:** Auto-installing copy-protection is the only way to immediately stem revenue loss from casual copying in 2005 before legal streaming matures.

**Attack:**
1.  **Security & Trust:** Software that installs silently without clear consent is functionally indistinguishable from malware (rootkits). Security researchers will reverse-engineer it, exposing vulnerabilities that compromise user systems. The company becomes an adversary to its own customers.
2.  **Effectiveness:** History shows copy-protection on open platforms (PCs) is broken within days. The cost of developing and maintaining this arms race exceeds the marginal loss from casual copying.
3.  **Alternatives:** "Do nothing" preserves brand goodwill. A "better way" is competing on convenience and pricing (e.g., early digital stores) rather than punishing legitimate buyers. Treating customers as thieves drives them to pirate sources that offer better usability.
4.  **Pre-mortem:** Assume the software causes system crashes or creates security holes. The resulting PR nightmare, class-action lawsuits, and mandatory recalls would dwarf any saved sales. The "one-way door" of releasing malware-like software cannot be undone once users are infected.

**Blockers:**
*   **Fact:** The proposal admits the software installs "without clear consent." This violates basic user autonomy and security norms, guaranteeing backlash.
*   **Fact:** The mechanism treats all users as criminals, alienating the paying base while failing to stop dedicated pirates.
*   **Kill Criterion:** If security researchers label the software a rootkit (highly probable), the brand is permanently tainted as untrustworthy.

**Risks:** High probability of technical failure, legal action, and permanent reputation damage. The "risk" of copying is financial; the risk of this solution is existential.

**Decision Logic:** The proposal fails the "do no harm" baseline. The cure is worse than the disease. No amount of projected sales recovery justifies turning customer computers into security liabilities.

DECISION: NO-GO
