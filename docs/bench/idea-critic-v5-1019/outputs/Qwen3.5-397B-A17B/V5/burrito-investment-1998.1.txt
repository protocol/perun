First call: NO-GO. The proposal asks a mass-market operator to back a tiny, slow, high-cost niche that contradicts its core efficiency model without a clear path to scale.

Steelman: A minority stake in 16 units offers a low-capital option to learn about a premium segment without operational distraction, preserving the right to scale if demand proves durable.

Options:
1. **Invest as proposed:** High cost per unit, slow throughput, limited upside given the small footprint.
2. **Do nothing:** Zero risk, zero learning, but misses any potential shift to premium fast-casual.
3. **Licensing/Tech deal:** Extract value from the brand's novelty without capital exposure or management drag.

Fit: The "slow, skilled, expensive" model clashes with the giant's north star of speed, standardization, and low price. It serves a different customer with different economics.

Pre-mortem: The chain fails to scale beyond 50 units because the made-to-order model bottlenecks throughput during peak hours, and the price point limits frequency. The investment becomes a distracting vanity project. This is a two-way door (minority stake), but the cost of delay is low; the market isn't disappearing tomorrow.

Evidence: The proposal admits the chain is "tiny," "slower," and "higher price." These are not bugs to be fixed later; they are the current reality. No data suggests these constraints can be broken while maintaining quality.

Blockers:
- **Economic Mismatch:** The core claim relies on future scaling, but the current unit economics (slow labor, high price) directly oppose the investor's comparative advantage. The proposal offers no mechanism to resolve this tension.
- **Strategic Distraction:** Management attention is explicitly cited as a risk; a minority stake still requires oversight, diverting focus from core high-volume optimization.

Risks:
- The concept is a fad rather than a durable shift.
- Operational complexity contaminates the parent brand's culture.

Kill Criteria: If the pilot (the 16 stores) cannot grow same-store sales by >5% annually without price hikes, the thesis fails. However, since the fundamental model contradicts the investor's strengths, the risk is structural, not just metric-based.

The proposal asks to bet on a model that negates the investor's primary moat (speed/cost) without a plan to fix it.

DECISION: NO-GO
