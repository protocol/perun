First call: NO-GO. The proposal attempts to solve an engineering defect with a bureaucratic filter that ignores the psychological reality of trust.

Steelman: Limiting replacements to provable cases saves capital while technically addressing the tiny fraction of affected workflows.

Attack: The claim that users can "show their work is affected" is false; the error rate (once in thousands of years) makes empirical verification impossible for any single user. This creates an impossible burden of proof, signaling arrogance rather than precision. The "reputation risk" is not a hypothetical; it is an active, compounding liability when a market leader appears to hide defects behind fine print.

Alternatives:
1. Do nothing: Guarantees brand erosion as the narrative shifts from "glitch" to "cover-up."
2. Universal replacement: High immediate cost, but converts a crisis into a demonstration of uncompromising quality standards.
3. Proposed plan: Saves money now, but invites regulatory scrutiny and long-term customer churn due to perceived dishonesty.

Fit: A hardware vendor's north star is absolute reliability. This plan contradicts that by asking customers to gamble on statistical probability.

Pre-mortem: Six months post-announcement, sales stall not because chips fail, but because procurement officers refuse to risk liability on a vendor that disputes its own error rates. The cost of delay (waiting for outrage to peak) exceeds the cost of immediate recall.

Evidence: The proposal admits users cannot easily prove impact. Therefore, the condition for replacement is functionally a denial. The "large sum" of replacement is a known, fixed cost; the reputational damage is an uncapped, variable liability.

Blockers:
1. The core mechanism (user proof) is impossible given the stated error frequency, rendering the offer illusory.
2. The stated fact that the problem is "widely discussed" means opacity will be interpreted as guilt.

Risks: Financial hit from universal recall (mitigated by long-term brand equity).

Kill criteria: If the company cannot guarantee 100% trust, it cannot sell precision hardware. The current plan fails this immediately.

DECISION: NO-GO
