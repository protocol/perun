First call: NO-GO. The proposal commits to high-volume production and a lower price point without verified battery supply, manufacturing capability, or non-refundable demand, creating an immediate cash-flow and execution cliff.

Steelman: The company can leverage early adopter hype to fund mass-market scaling, assuming deposits convert to sales fast enough to bridge the gap between production ramp and revenue.

Attack: The plan fails on three verified gaps in the proposal itself. First, the company has produced only two vehicles in small numbers; jumping to "far higher volumes" ignores the "production hell" inherent in automotive manufacturing, where yield rates often crash before recovering. Second, battery supply must be "scaled up sharply," yet no supplier contracts or capacity reserves are cited; without secured cells, production cannot begin regardless of demand. Third, deposits are refundable. If production delays occur (likely given the inexperience), depositors will likely demand refunds, draining cash precisely when capital is most needed. The lower price leaves little margin to absorb these inevitable delays or cost overruns.

Alternatives: "Do nothing" preserves cash but kills momentum. A "pilot batch" at current volume limits would test supply chains without overcommitting. However, the proposal explicitly rejects staging, demanding full production next year.

Pre-mortem: Assume it is late 2017. Production is months behind; battery costs are higher than projected; refund requests surge as delays mount. The company burns through cash reserves trying to build a factory it cannot staff and supply chains it does not control, forcing a bailout or bankruptcy before a single mass-market car is delivered.

Blockers:
1. No evidence of secured battery supply capacity for the proposed volume.
2. No proof of manufacturing capability beyond tiny batches; the jump to mass production is unbridged.
3. Refundable deposits create a liability, not working capital, if the inevitable production delays occur.

Risks: Margin erosion, brand damage from delays, competitor response. These are secondary to the hard blockers.

The proposal asks to bet the company on unproven operations and unsecured supply. The risks are not merely "possible"; the lack of current capacity makes failure the baseline expectation unless the specific blockers are removed first.

DECISION: NO-GO
