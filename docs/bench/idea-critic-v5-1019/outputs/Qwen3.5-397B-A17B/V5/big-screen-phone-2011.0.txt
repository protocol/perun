First call: GO. The market lacks a high-end device bridging the phone-tablet gap, and screen real estate is a defensible premium differentiator despite skepticism.

Steelman: A large-screen stylus device creates a new "phablet" category, capturing users who need more than a phone but carry only one device, justifying a premium price through unique utility rather than specs alone.

Options:
1. Do nothing: Cede the emerging large-screen segment to competitors; risk irrelevance as media consumption shifts to mobile.
2. Small tablet: High friction (carry two devices); fails the "always with you" test.
3. This proposal: High risk of niche failure, but highest upside if it defines a new standard. It beats "do nothing" by creating a market rather than fighting for share in an existing one.

Fit: Aligns with a vision of mobile productivity and media convergence. Assumes users prioritize screen utility over pocketability.

Pre-mortem: Failure occurs if reviewers successfully frame the size as "comical" rather than "productive," or if the stylus is perceived as a gimmick without software support. This is a two-way door; if it fails, the line is discontinued with minimal brand damage compared to missing the trend. Cost of delay is losing first-mover advantage in the large-screen category.

Evidence: Based on the stated observation of 4-inch norms and the unmet need for tablet-like utility in a phone form factor. No internal data contradicts the potential for a premium segment.

Blockers: NONE. The risks cited (reviewer bias, stylus adoption, cannibalization) are uncertainties, not facts proving failure. Cannibalization is acceptable if it retains customers within the ecosystem who might otherwise leave for competitors.

Risks: Market rejection of form factor; stylus remains underutilized. These become kill criteria: if Q1 sales do not exceed X units or attach rate for stylus features is <Y%, pivot or discontinue.

The strongest attack is that the device is "too big for a phone and too small for a tablet." This fails because it assumes rigid category definitions; user behavior often bends categories to fit needs, and "one device to rule them all" is a compelling value proposition that overrides purist definitions.

DECISION: GO
