First call: NO-GO. The proposal ignores the fundamental dynamics of format wars where compatibility and content availability outweigh marginal technical superiority.

Steelman: Proprietary control preserves brand prestige and unit margins while leveraging a temporary quality lead to secure early adopters.

Attack: The "better picture" advantage is negligible to consumers compared to the inability to record full-length movies or access a wide library of pre-recorded titles. In format wars (e.g., VHS vs. Betamax), the ecosystem with broader licensing wins by driving down hardware prices and incentivizing content creators to support the dominant user base. Restricting licenses to "a few partners" artificially caps market penetration, ensuring the rival's open format becomes the de facto standard. Once the rival achieves critical mass, the proprietary format becomes obsolete regardless of picture quality. The one-hour tape limit is a fatal functional defect for the primary use case (recording movies/events).

Options:
1. **Proprietary (Proposal):** High margin per unit, low volume, high risk of total obsolescence.
2. **Open Licensing:** Lower margin per unit, massive volume, drives standard adoption, secures content library.
3. **Do Nothing/Status Quo:** Cedes the market entirely to the rival immediately.

Fit: The goal is market dominance, not just selling high-end units. A standard that cannot play full movies fails the core user need.

Pre-mortem: Two years post-launch, the rival format is in 80% of homes; video stores stock only their tapes; our superior quality is irrelevant because no one owns our player.

Evidence: History shows closed formats lose to open ones in consumer electronics when network effects (content + hardware availability) are the primary value driver. The rival's strategy directly attacks our weakness (library size and price).

Blockers:
1. **Functional Defect:** One-hour capacity fails the core use case of recording full films.
2. **Ecosystem Failure:** Restricted licensing guarantees fewer pre-recorded titles and higher hardware costs, ceding the standard to the rival.

Risks: Price wars, slow adoption (mitigated only by opening the format).

The proposal relies on technical specs to win a distribution battle. Without full movie capacity and broad manufacturer support to flood the market and attract content providers, the format will fail.

DECISION: NO-GO
