First call: GO. The asymmetry of low fixed costs against incumbent inertia favors a rapid, cheap test over deliberation.

Steelman: A minimal viable launch using humor and direct-to-consumer logistics can exploit incumbent pricing inefficiencies before they react.

Alternatives: Doing nothing guarantees zero market share. Building a factory requires prohibitive capital. Licensing existing brands forfeits the margin needed for the low-price strategy. Only the proposed asset-light model allows immediate market validation.

Fit: Assumes the goal is rapid customer acquisition and brand disruption rather than immediate manufacturing depth. This aligns with a "land grab" strategy in a fragmented retail environment.

Pre-mortem: Failure occurs if the supplier raises prices post-launch or if the video fails to go viral, leaving no traffic. However, these are risks, not blockers. The cost of delay is high; waiting allows incumbents to digitize their own offerings or new entrants to capture the "cheap" narrative. A one-way door does not exist here; the website and video are reversible assets.

Evidence: The proposal relies on the assumed fact that third-party manufacturing quality is sufficient and that digital video distribution is cost-effective. While unverified externally, the low capital requirement makes these acceptable assumptions for a pilot. The financial math holds: low overhead + high volume potential = positive unit economics if conversion occurs.

Blockers: NONE. No stated fact proves the model cannot work. The risks of litigation or price wars are speculative responses by incumbents, not current barriers. Dependence on a single supplier is a standard startup constraint, not a fatal flaw at this stage.

Risks: Supplier reliability, viral failure, customer churn. These become kill criteria: if the supplier fails to deliver within 30 days of the first order, or if customer acquisition cost exceeds lifetime value after the first video, the project stops.

The strongest attack—that incumbents will instantly crush a small player—fails because large organizations suffer from innovation inertia and cannot easily match low-price subscription models without cannibalizing their core retail revenue. The low-budget approach minimizes downside while maximizing optionality.

DECISION: GO
