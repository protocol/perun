The claim is that a sub-£400, license-free, pedal-assisted tricycle can be successfully launched via mail order in January 1985. The steelman posits that low cost and regulatory exemption create a mass market for short-distance urban mobility, overcoming performance limitations.

However, the proposal fails the pre-mortem and fit checks. Launching in January ignores the stated constraint of "British winters," which severely limit the utility of an open, low-speed vehicle with no weather protection. Mail order relies on impulse and immediate utility; a product unusable for three to four months of the year due to weather and safety concerns (low visibility in traffic) will face catastrophic return rates and reputational damage before spring.

The "better way" analysis reveals a fatal flaw in the go-to-market strategy. A January launch for a fair-weather vehicle is economically irrational. The risk of safety group objections regarding low-speed traffic integration is heightened by winter conditions (darkness, wet roads). Furthermore, selling essential parts as "optional extras" while marketing a complete vehicle creates friction and potential safety gaps if users omit critical items to keep costs down. The top speed of 15 mph places it in a dangerous limbo: too slow for main roads, yet potentially too fast for sidewalks, inviting regulatory crackdown.

The strongest attack is the seasonality mismatch. A mail-order launch requires immediate adoption to sustain cash flow and word-of-mouth. Releasing a weather-exposed vehicle at the onset of the worst driving season guarantees poor initial reviews and stalled momentum. The assembly agreement with a large appliance maker does not mitigate the demand-side failure of launching a summer product in winter.

To survive, the launch must be delayed until spring, or the value proposition must fundamentally change to address winter usability (e.g., included weather shielding). As proposed, the timing and configuration ensure failure.

DECISION: NO-GO
