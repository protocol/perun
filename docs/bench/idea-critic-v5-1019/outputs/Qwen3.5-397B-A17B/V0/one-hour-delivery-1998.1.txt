The claim is that a 1998 venture-backed model employing direct staff for free, sub-hour delivery of mixed goods in major cities will succeed despite acknowledged unit-economic deficits.

**Steelman:** Rapid market share capture via superior convenience forces competitor exit, allowing future price increases to subsidize current losses, while direct employment ensures quality control impossible with contractors.

**Attack:** The proposal explicitly admits the core mechanism is broken: free delivery on small orders costs more than the margin. In 1998, mobile internet penetration was negligible, preventing the dynamic routing density required to lower costs. Employing staff directly creates massive fixed costs before revenue stability, violating the "expand quickly" mandate which requires capital efficiency. The "subsidy dependency" risk is not a possibility but a certainty given the stated pricing model. Without a verified path to positive unit economics on the first order, scaling only accelerates cash burn. The "network effect" argument fails because convenience is local, not global; winning one block does not help win the next if each block loses money.

**Better Way:** Start with a minimum order value that covers variable costs, use third-party contractors to keep fixed costs low, or limit the catalog to high-margin items only. Do not raise venture money for a model known to lose money on every transaction.

**Kill Criteria:** If the cost per delivery exceeds the gross margin of the average order after 1,000 orders in a single pilot zone, the model is fundamentally flawed. If customer retention drops below 20% once subsidies are removed, the business does not exist.

**Verdict:** The proposal asks to fund a known loss-leader strategy with high fixed costs in an era lacking the technological infrastructure to optimize logistics. It fails the basic test of selling a dollar for less than a dollar. The risks raised are not mitigated; they are the business plan.

DECISION: NO-GO
