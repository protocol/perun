The claim that superior picture quality and an early lead will secure market dominance against a widely licensed rival with longer recording capacity is historically fragile in format wars. The steelman argues that a controlled ecosystem ensures higher margins and brand prestige, betting that consumers prioritize fidelity over convenience. However, this ignores the critical "better-way" reality: the rival's strategy directly addresses the primary use case (recording full movies/events) and leverages network effects to lower hardware costs and expand content libraries.

The pre-mortem reveals a high probability of failure: if the one-hour limit prevents recording standard feature films, the product becomes functionally obsolete for its main purpose regardless of picture clarity. Furthermore, restricting licenses limits manufacturing scale, keeping prices high while the rival floods the market with cheaper units. The "door" is two-way only initially; once the rival establishes an installed base and content library, the switch cost for consumers becomes prohibitive, locking out the proprietary format permanently.

The strongest attack survived is the assumption that "early lead" matters more than "ecosystem breadth." In this specific context, an early lead with inferior utility (shorter tape) and higher cost (fewer manufacturers) accelerates obsolescence rather than securing dominance. The evidence suggests that in format wars, compatibility and content availability consistently defeat technical superiority when the technical gap (picture quality) is subjective or secondary to the functional constraint (recording duration).

No kill criteria or leading metrics are provided to pivot if adoption lags, making this a blind bet on a losing value proposition. The proposal fails to address the fundamental mismatch between the product's limitations (one hour) and market needs (full movies). Proceeding as proposed guarantees a costly defeat against a more open, convenient alternative.
