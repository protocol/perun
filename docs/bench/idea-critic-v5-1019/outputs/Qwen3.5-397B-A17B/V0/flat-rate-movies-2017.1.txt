The claim that slashing the price to $10/month while paying theaters full ticket price will succeed relies on the assumption that rapid user growth will outpace immediate cash burn long enough for unproven data and studio deals to materialize. This is a classic "blitzscaling" bet where unit economics are intentionally negative at the start.

The strongest attack is the **infinite liability trap**: if a member sees one film daily at an average ticket cost of $12, the company loses ~$360 per member monthly against $10 revenue. With no cap on usage, a surge in heavy users accelerates insolvency linearly with "success." The proposed future revenue streams (data, studio deals) are speculative and cannot be counted on to plug a hole that widens with every transaction. Theater chains refusing cooperation is a secondary risk; the primary failure mode is running out of the owner's cash before proving the model, as the cost structure guarantees a loss on every active user.

The "do nothing" or "keep high price" alternative preserves capital and tests demand without guaranteed ruin. A "limited films per month" alternative aligns costs closer to revenue. The current proposal fails the fit check because it assumes the owner's cash is infinite relative to the burn rate required to acquire enough users to leverage data monetization. There is no kill criterion mentioned to stop the bleed if adoption outpaces deal-making.

Without a mechanism to cap liability per user or verified contracts guaranteeing the future revenue streams, this plan is a bet on miracles rather than math. The unit economics are fundamentally broken as described; subsidizing a service where every additional use increases the loss is not a strategy, it is a donation to theater chains funded by the owner's savings. The risk of total capital loss before achieving profitability is near certainty.

DECISION: NO-GO
