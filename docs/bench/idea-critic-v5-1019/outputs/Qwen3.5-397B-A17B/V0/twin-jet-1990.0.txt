**Steelman:** Launching a mid-size twin-engine wide-body with eight airline partners secures immediate market fit and shared development risk, leveraging a single anchor order to justify the program despite regulatory uncertainty.

**Attack:** The proposal fails on three critical fronts. First, regulatory approval for twin-engine long-haul overwater flights (ETOPS) was not guaranteed in 1990; building an aircraft dependent on this rule change creates existential certification risk. If authorities delay or deny, the aircraft becomes commercially useless for its primary long-haul mission. Second, designing with eight airlines simultaneously invites "design by committee," guaranteeing scope creep, conflicting requirements, and severe delays, inflating the already massive development cost. Third, anchoring the entire program on a single order of 34 units is insufficient capitalization for an all-new aircraft family; this volume likely fails to cover non-recurring engineering costs, let alone generate profit, exposing the maker to catastrophic loss if other partners withdraw.

**Better Way:** Delay the full launch until ETOPS regulations are clarified or secured. Secure anchor orders from multiple partners to dilute risk and validate demand before committing to tooling. Adopt a modular design approach to satisfy diverse airline needs without customizing the core airframe for every partner.

**Kill Criteria:** Do not proceed if ETOPS approval timelines remain undefined by regulatory bodies. Do not proceed if the total committed orders do not exceed a break-even threshold significantly higher than 34 units. Do not proceed if the development timeline exceeds a fixed cap due to partner-induced changes.

**Verdict:** The risk profile is unacceptable. The regulatory hurdle is unproven, the order book is too thin to support the cost, and the multi-partner design process threatens viability. The proposal relies on hopeful assumptions rather than verified constraints.

DECISION: NO-GO
