The claim is that a $5,000 self-balancing transporter will successfully replace short car trips and walking if launched widely to consumers in 2001. The steelman: this device offers a unique, compact mobility solution that justifies its premium price through superior technology and convenience, creating a new market segment.

However, the skeptic hat identifies fatal flaws in the 2001 context. First, the price point ($5,000) exceeds that of a reliable used car, a bicycle, or a motor scooter, destroying the economic logic for "replacing car trips" for the mass market. Second, the regulatory environment is undefined; launching "widely" without established rules for sidewalks and roads invites immediate bans and liability suits, halting adoption. Third, the learning curve for balancing creates a safety barrier that discourages the casual users needed for widespread replacement of walking.

The "better-way" analysis shows superior alternatives exist: bicycles are cheaper and legally clear; scooters are cheaper and familiar; used cars offer cargo and passenger capacity. The proposal fails to address why a consumer would choose this expensive, limited device over these established options. The "do nothing" option (waiting for regulations and cost reductions) is safer than a wide launch that risks brand destruction through regulatory backlash and poor sales.

The pre-mortem reveals a likely failure mode: the product launches, is immediately restricted by city ordinances due to safety concerns, sells only to niche enthusiasts due to price, and fails to achieve the volume necessary to sustain manufacturing. The cost of delay is low; waiting for regulatory clarity and price reduction via iteration is preferable to a premature wide launch. The proposal lacks a kill criterion or a phased approach (e.g., limited pilot), making the "wide launch" strategy reckless.

The strongest attack survived is the technological novelty, but novelty does not equal market fit given the price and regulatory void. The proposal assumes consumers will pay a car-like price for a device with less utility than a bicycle, which is unsupported.

DECISION: NO-GO
