The claim that the flaw is "not serious" relies entirely on a probabilistic model of typical usage that ignores the psychological reality of trust. While the mathematical frequency of error (once in thousands of years) suggests low operational risk for most, the proposal fails the "what would have to be true" test: it assumes users will accept a manufacturer's calculation of risk over their own need for certainty. This is false in high-stakes domains where even a single error is catastrophic, regardless of probability.

The "better way" analysis reveals a critical omission. The proposal offers replacement only to users who can *prove* impact. This creates an impossible barrier: verifying a rare floating-point error requires resources most users lack, effectively denying relief to affected parties while admitting guilt. This asymmetric burden guarantees resentment. The alternative—proactive replacement for verified critical sectors or a universal, no-questions-asked swap program—costs more upfront but purchases reputation insurance. The current plan bets the company's long-term brand equity against a short-term cash preservation, a wager with undefined downside if the narrative shifts from "rare glitch" to "cover-up."

The pre-mortem is clear: if a prominent user encounters this error and publicizes the difficulty of proving it to get a fix, the story becomes "arrogant manufacturer hides defect." The reputational damage will far exceed the cost of a blanket replacement. The proposal treats this as a math problem; it is a trust problem. By forcing users to jump through hoops to prove harm, the company signals it values its balance sheet over user integrity. The kill criterion here is simple: if the cost of replacing all chips exceeds the estimated loss of future sales due to reputation damage, the plan fails. Given the intangible nature of brand trust, that threshold is likely already crossed.

The strongest attack survived is the sheer cost of a universal recall; however, the cost of *not* doing so includes the potential collapse of confidence in the entire product line, which is unquantifiable but potentially existential. The proposal lacks a mechanism to restore trust, focusing only on minimizing immediate payout.

DECISION: NO-GO
