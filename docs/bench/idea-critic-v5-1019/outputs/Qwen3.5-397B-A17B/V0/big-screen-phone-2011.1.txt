The claim is that a >5" stylus phone will succeed as a premium "phablet" despite market skepticism regarding size and utility. The steelman posits an untapped segment needing more screen real estate than a 4" phone but greater portability than a tablet, where the stylus enables precise input impossible on smaller screens.

Alternatives include maintaining the 4" standard (low risk, capped innovation), waiting for competitors to test the water (low cost, lost first-mover advantage), or releasing a tablet instead (misses the "pocketable" niche). The status quo fails to address users hitting the physical limits of current touch interfaces.

A pre-mortem suggests failure if the device is perceived as a clumsy hybrid rather than a distinct category. However, the risk of cannibalization is inherent to premium upgrades; if the new device offers significantly higher value, internal displacement is preferable to losing customers to rivals. The "too big" criticism is subjective and likely to shift once the utility of the larger display for media and productivity is demonstrated. The stylus, often dismissed as niche, becomes a key differentiator for note-taking and creative work, justifying the premium price point.

The cost of delay is high: ceding the emerging large-screen segment to competitors could permanently relegate the brand to "small phone" status. The one-way door risk is limited to initial inventory; if sales lag, the form factor can be quietly discontinued or adjusted in the next cycle without brand destruction. The leading metric should be the attach rate of premium accessories and active stylus usage, not just unit volume, to validate the unique value proposition.

The strongest attack—that the market rejects the size—fails because screen size preferences are elastic and historically trend upward when content consumption drives usage. The specific combination of size and precision input creates a moat that neither small phones nor large tablets can easily cross.
