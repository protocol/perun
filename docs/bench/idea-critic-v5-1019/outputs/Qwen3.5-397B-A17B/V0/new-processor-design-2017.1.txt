The claim rests on a high-stakes gamble: an all-new architecture from a team with a recent failure, funded by a balance sheet that cannot absorb another miss. The steelman is that flooding the market with cheap, high-core-count chips forces a price war the larger rival cannot ignore without ceding share, while software trends finally favor parallelism.

However, the failure modes are catastrophic and unmitigated. First, the "all-new" requirement ignores the cheaper, reversible path: refining the existing flawed design or licensing proven IP to close the gap immediately. Building from scratch guarantees delay, allowing the rival to launch faster chips or slash prices first. Second, the assumption that software will utilize extra cores is unverified; if applications remain single-thread dominant, the performance-per-dollar advantage vanishes, leaving the company with expensive, underutilized silicon. Third, the financial constraint is absolute; a second miss implies insolvency. The proposal offers no kill criteria, no phased rollout, and no fallback if the new architecture underperforms like the last one.

The strongest attack is the timing mismatch: developing an all-new architecture takes years, while the rival's reaction time is months. By the time this line launches, the rival will have already optimized their pricing or released a superior product, rendering the new chips obsolete before they generate revenue. The proposal treats a survival-level bet as a standard product cycle, ignoring the "do nothing" or "incremental fix" alternatives that preserve cash. Without a proven architectural win or a guaranteed software ecosystem shift, the risk of total capital destruction is too high. The company is betting its entire remaining equity on a technical miracle against a dominant incumbent, with no evidence that the core technical hurdle (software scaling) has been cleared.

DECISION: NO-GO
