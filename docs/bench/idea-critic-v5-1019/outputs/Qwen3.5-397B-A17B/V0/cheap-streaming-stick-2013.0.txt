The claim is that a remote-less, low-margin HDMI dongle relying on second-screen control will succeed where prior dedicated TV devices failed, despite app scarcity and user confusion risks.

Steelman: By eliminating the remote and on-screen menu, the device forces a modern, phone-centric interaction model that drastically reduces hardware costs, allowing a price point low enough to impulse-buy into existing ecosystems, while an open kit accelerates app development organically.

However, critical failures loom. First, the "no remote" assumption ignores living room reality: shared devices require shared control. If a user's phone battery dies or they lack the specific app, the device becomes a brick for other household members, creating immediate friction and returns. Second, the "low margin" strategy relies on volume to subsidize platform growth, but with "few apps at launch," the value proposition is thin; users buy content access, not hardware novelty. Third, established competitors have deep catalogs; an empty store cannot compete on content, so the open kit must yield results faster than historical developer adoption curves suggest.

Better ways exist: bundle a minimal infrared blaster to leverage existing TV remotes for basic navigation, or subsidize a simple remote to reduce support costs without sacrificing the low-price anchor. Alternatively, delay launch until key streaming apps commit, trading speed for viability.

Pre-mortem: The device launches, early adopters struggle to explain playback to guests, support tickets spike regarding "broken" units with no physical interface, and developers ignore the open kit due to low install base, creating a death spiral. The cost of delay is losing the "first cheap dongle" headline, but the cost of failure is brand damage in the living room.

Kill criteria are absent. There is no defined threshold for app adoption by day 90, nor a return-rate limit that triggers a recall or remote bundle. Without a physical fallback or committed content, the risk of consumer rejection is too high to approve as proposed.

DECISION: NO-GO
