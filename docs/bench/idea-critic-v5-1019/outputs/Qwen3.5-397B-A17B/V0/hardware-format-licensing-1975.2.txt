The proposal to keep the format proprietary while a rival pursues aggressive licensing ignores the fundamental dynamics of the home video market: network effects. The "better picture quality" advantage is negligible to consumers if the device cannot fulfill its primary utility: recording a full movie or sports event. The one-hour tape limit is a fatal functional defect, not a minor trade-off.

The rival's strategy of licensing to many manufacturers directly addresses the two biggest barriers to adoption: hardware price and content availability. By restricting licenses, this proposal guarantees higher unit costs and slower market penetration. Furthermore, pre-recorded title availability correlates directly with the installed base; a smaller partner ecosystem means fewer movies available for rent or purchase, creating a negative feedback loop that drives consumers to the competing format.

History in format wars demonstrates that technical superiority rarely defeats superior distribution and content ecosystems. Relying on an "early lead" is dangerous when that lead is built on a restrictive model that caps growth. The risk of the rival becoming the standard is not just possible; it is the probable outcome if the current proposal stands. The market will standardize around the format that offers the most convenience (longer recording time) and the lowest cost (mass manufacturing), not the one with the sharpest image for the first six months.

To proceed as proposed is to bet against the economic incentives of both manufacturers and consumers. The "better picture" is a luxury feature; the ability to record a whole film and buy cheap players are necessities. Without immediate revision to extend tape capacity and open licensing to drive down costs and increase content, this product will become a niche curiosity while the rival defines the industry standard.

DECISION: NO-GO
