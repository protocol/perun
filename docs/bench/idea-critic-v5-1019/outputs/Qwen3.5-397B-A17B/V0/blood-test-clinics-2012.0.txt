Steelman: Securing exclusive rights to a revolutionary, low-cost diagnostic tool could transform the supermarket into a primary healthcare destination, capturing massive market share before competitors react.

However, the attack on this claim is devastating. The proposal demands a $350 million capital expenditure for unproven technology. The core failure mode is technical invalidity: the device lacks published studies or peer verification. In medical diagnostics, accuracy is binary; a false negative or positive creates immediate liability and reputational ruin. The founder's refusal to share methods prevents independent validation, violating the basic tenet of scientific evidence.

The "better way" analysis reveals fatal flaws. The status quo (doing nothing) carries zero capital risk. Alternative paths include waiting for regulatory approval or third-party validation before investing, or piloting in a single location with strict performance milestones rather than a chain-wide rollout. The current proposal ignores Chesterton's Fence: the chain lacks medical expertise, creating an operational gap it cannot fill internally.

Pre-mortem: Assume the venture failed in 18 months. Why? Regulators banned the device due to inaccuracies, or the technology proved physically impossible at the claimed scale. The $350 million is sunk, stores are left with unused clinic infrastructure, and the brand suffers association with faulty medical advice. This is a one-way door; once the clinics are built and the exclusive contract signed, exiting is costly and public.

The cost of delay is negligible compared to the cost of error. Waiting for published data or regulatory clearance costs nothing but time, whereas acting now risks hundreds of millions. The "exclusivity" premium is a trap if the underlying asset is worthless. No kill criteria can save this because the baseline evidence is missing; you cannot set a metric for success when the mechanism of action is a black box denied even to the investor.

This is not an investment; it is speculation on a magic trick. The absence of evidence is not evidence of absence, but it is absolutely a reason to withhold $350 million.

DECISION: NO-GO
