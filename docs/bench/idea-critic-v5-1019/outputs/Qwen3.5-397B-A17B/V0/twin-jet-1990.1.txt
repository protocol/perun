The claim rests on launching a high-cost, all-new aircraft program based on a single order of 34 units from one airline. The steelman argues that co-designing with eight major carriers creates immediate market fit and locks in demand, offsetting the risk of a single anchor customer.

However, the skeptic identifies a fatal structural flaw: the regulatory environment for twin-engine jets on long overwater routes (ETOPS) had not yet granted the necessary approvals in 1990. Building an aircraft dependent on ungranted certification is a speculative gamble, not a secured program. Furthermore, the development cost is described as "very large," while the committed revenue is limited to 34 aircraft from one buyer. This creates a massive exposure where the project's viability hinges entirely on future regulatory changes and the conversion of seven other airlines' interest into hard orders, despite an active rival developing competing models.

The "better way" analysis suggests waiting for regulatory clarity or securing orders from multiple carriers before incurring the massive sunk costs of an all-new design. Designing with eight airlines simultaneously introduces significant risk of feature creep, delays, and conflicting requirements, potentially bloating the program before it begins.

The pre-mortem reveals a likely failure mode: regulatory approval is delayed or denied, the single anchor customer cancels due to shifting economics, and the co-design process causes fatal schedule slips, allowing the rival to capture the market segment. The cost of delay is negligible compared to the cost of launching a potentially uncertifiable or unprofitable aircraft.

There is no evidence provided that the regulatory hurdle will be cleared in time to match the development cycle, nor that the seven other airlines will convert interest to orders sufficient to cover the "very large" development costs. The single order of 34 units is insufficient to underwrite the risk of an all-new program in an uncertain regulatory and competitive landscape. The proposal fails to demonstrate a viable path to profitability or certification.

DECISION: NO-GO
