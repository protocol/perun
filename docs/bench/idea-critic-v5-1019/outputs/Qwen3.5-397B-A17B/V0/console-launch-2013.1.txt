The proposal to undercut the rival's price while using standard PC-like hardware addresses the previous generation's failures of lateness and cost. However, the strategy contains a fatal economic contradiction. Launching below a competitor's price point with new hardware inherently demands selling at a loss, relying on software margins to recoup costs. The proposal explicitly admits the maker's own studios have "little new software at launch." Without a strong first-party lineup to drive high-margin attachment rates immediately, the company will bleed cash on every unit sold with no near-term mechanism to recover it.

The reliance on "standard PC-like hardware" to ease development is a double-edged sword. While it reduces friction for third parties, it eliminates the unique performance advantage that typically justifies a dedicated console purchase, making the device directly comparable to increasingly powerful gaming PCs. Given the risk that consumers are "spending less on dedicated consoles," entering a price war without a differentiated library or a clear path to profitability per unit is reckless.

The rival's head start in online services is noted, but the proposal's focus on offline play and used games, while consumer-friendly, further erodes potential recurring revenue streams that could offset the low hardware margin. The combination of reduced margin per unit, a weak launch software slate, and a commoditized hardware architecture creates a scenario where market share gains would directly accelerate financial losses. The pre-mortem is clear: the company runs out of capital before the software library matures enough to sustain the ecosystem. The claim that this approach will regain ground ignores the immediate liquidity crisis it precipitates.

DECISION: NO-GO
