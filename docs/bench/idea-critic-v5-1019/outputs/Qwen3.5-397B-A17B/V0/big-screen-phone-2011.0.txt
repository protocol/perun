**Steelman:** A large-screen device with a stylus captures users needing more productivity than a phone offers but more portability than a tablet, creating a new premium category.

**Attack & Alternatives:**
1.  **Do Nothing:** Safest short-term, but cedes the emerging "phablet" space to competitors.
2.  **Tablet Only:** Ignores the one-handed usage gap; tablets are too bulky for pockets.
3.  **Larger Phone (No Stylus):** Misses the productivity angle that justifies the premium price and size increase.

**Fit & Pre-mortem:**
Assuming the goal is market expansion and premium positioning, this fits. However, if the market rejects the size as "awkward" rather than "spacious," inventory will stagnate. The "too small for a tablet, too big for a phone" risk is real but represents an opportunity to define a new category rather than fit an old one. Cannibalization is acceptable if the new device carries a higher margin and retains users within the ecosystem who might otherwise switch brands for screen size.

**Evidence & Constraints:**
Based solely on the provided text: The proposal explicitly targets the gap between phone and tablet. The risks are speculative consumer perceptions (reviewers, buyers) rather than technical impossibilities or financial insolvency. No data suggests the current four-inch line satisfies all user needs; the existence of the proposal implies unmet demand.

**Kill Criteria & Decision:**
The only valid reason to stop is if the company cannot sustain a potential initial sales miss while educating the market. However, the proposal asks to *announce and sell*, implying a market test is the intended mechanism to validate demand. Waiting for perfect certainty guarantees missing the first-mover advantage in this specific form factor. The risks are manageable marketing challenges, not fatal flaws. The "stylus" differentiates it from a mere size bump, offering a functional reason to exist.

The strongest attack—that it satisfies neither use case perfectly—fails because user behavior often adapts to new form factors that offer *more* utility (screen real estate) even if they sacrifice some ergonomics, provided the software leverages the space. The potential reward of defining a new category outweighs the risk of confusing reviewers.

DECISION: GO
