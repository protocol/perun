The claim is that a $5,000 self-balancing transporter will successfully replace short car trips and walking if launched widely to consumers in 2001. Steelman: This device offers a unique, compact mobility solution that justifies its premium price through novelty and efficiency, creating a new market segment.

However, the attack reveals fatal flaws in the 2001 context. First, the price point is prohibitive; at $5,000, it costs more than a reliable used car, a motorcycle, or hundreds of bicycles, offering no cargo capacity or passenger utility to justify the differential. Second, the regulatory environment is hostile; cities in 2001 have no frameworks for motorized stand-up devices on sidewalks or roads, inviting immediate bans and liability suits. Third, the learning curve creates a safety hazard; requiring users to learn dynamic balancing invites falls and lawsuits before mass adoption can occur.

Alternatives are superior: improving bicycle infrastructure costs less and carries no regulatory ambiguity; electric scooters (if developed later) could be cheaper and simpler. The "do nothing" option avoids massive capital loss on a product the market cannot legally or financially absorb.

The pre-mortem shows inevitable failure: inventory piles up as cities ban the device following early accidents, and consumers reject the high cost for a single-person gadget with no practical advantage over existing transport. The cost of delay is negligible; waiting for battery costs to drop, regulations to evolve, and safety mechanisms to improve is essential. There is no evidence that 2001 consumers value "novelty" over utility at this price point.

Kill criteria are clear: If the unit cost cannot drop below $2,000 or if major cities issue cease-and-desist orders within three months, the project must stop. The leading metric of "units sold" will be artificially suppressed by regulatory blocks, not demand.

This proposal ignores fundamental economic and legal realities of the time. It is a solution in search of a problem that cannot be solved at this price or regulatory moment.

DECISION: NO-GO
