The claim is that a free language-learning platform, powered by user-generated translations sold to publishers, can succeed without immediate revenue. The steelman: leveraging real-world translation tasks creates a sustainable flywheel where learning and value creation happen simultaneously, deferring monetization until scale validates the model.

However, critical failures loom. First, the "product" (translations) relies on novices; publishers rarely buy unverified, low-quality work, creating a mismatch between supply quality and market demand. Second, the "free forever" model attracts users with zero intent to pay, making later conversion to a paid tier or ad-supported model statistically difficult compared to established competitors who charge upfront for polished content. Third, the "revenue later" approach ignores the high burn rate of hosting and maintaining a global web app; without a verified path to cash flow, the project risks collapsing before reaching critical mass.

Better ways exist: a hybrid model where basic lessons are free but "real work" requires a small fee or subscription, ensuring user commitment and funding quality assurance before selling to publishers. Alternatively, partnering with existing translation agencies to validate learner output before sale reduces market risk.

The pre-mortem reveals the platform launches, accumulates thousands of learners, but generates negligible revenue because publisher contracts fail due to quality issues, and the team runs out of funds to maintain servers. This is a two-way door only if the initial build cost is near-zero; otherwise, it is a one-way sink of capital. The cost of delay is low, as the language market is not rapidly shifting in a way that requires immediate capture.

Evidence is absent for publisher demand for novice work or the conversion rate of free learners to value-generating contributors. The proposal assumes quality will emerge from volume, a dangerous gamble in professional services.

Kill criteria: If no paid pilot with a publisher is secured before launch, or if user-generated translations fail a blind quality test against professional standards within three months, the project must pivot or stop.

DECISION: NO-GO
