Steelman: A smaller chipmaker can regain desktop relevance by launching an all-new architecture offering significantly more cores at lower prices than the dominant rival, leveraging multi-core efficiency to bypass single-thread deficits.

However, the proposal fails the pre-mortem and financial reality check. The company's finances explicitly allow "no room for a second miss," yet the strategy relies entirely on an "all-new architecture" immediately following a disappointing all-new design. This ignores the primary cause of the previous failure: if the prior new architecture underperformed, repeating this high-risk approach without a proven interim fix or architectural validation invites catastrophic failure.

The "better way" analysis reveals a critical omission. The proposal assumes software will utilize extra cores, yet admits this is a risk. Without confirmed software optimization, higher core counts yield diminishing returns, failing to offset the rival's potential price cuts or faster single-thread performance. The plan lacks a kill criterion for software readiness before full commitment.

Furthermore, the financial constraint makes the proposed "all-new" gamble irrational. A "do nothing" or "incremental update" strategy preserves capital while the rival potentially stumbles. The proposal offers no evidence that the specific architectural flaws of the previous generation have been resolved, nor does it provide a fallback if the rival engages in a price war.

The strongest attack is the financial cliff: betting the company's survival on a repeat of the exact strategy (all-new architecture) that just failed, while assuming market conditions (software support, rival inaction) will align perfectly, violates basic risk management for a cash-constrained entity. The proposal treats a existential gamble as a standard product cycle.

To proceed, the company must first demonstrate a working prototype that outperforms the previous failure in independent benchmarks and secure binding commitments from major software vendors regarding core utilization. Without these, the risk of total insolvency outweighs the potential gain.

DECISION: NO-GO
