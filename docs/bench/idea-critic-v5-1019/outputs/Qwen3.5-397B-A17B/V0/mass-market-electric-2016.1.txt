The claim is that a niche luxury EV maker can instantly pivot to mass-market volume production of a cheaper sedan within one year, funded by refundable deposits. The steelman: leveraging brand hype from limited high-end releases to secure cash flow via deposits, forcing supply chain scaling to achieve economies of scale before margins exist.

However, the failure hypothesis is concrete and unaddressed: **production hell**. The company has zero experience building cars at the proposed volume. Scaling battery supply sharply while simultaneously retooling factories for a completely new, cheaper chassis introduces massive execution risk. History shows that transitioning from low-volume hand-assembly to high-volume automation often results in severe bottlenecks, quality control failures, and missed delivery dates.

Refundable deposits are not revenue; they are liabilities that vanish if confidence erodes due to delays. If production slips—even slightly—depositors will demand refunds, causing a cash crunch precisely when capital intensity is highest. The lower price point eliminates the buffer for errors; any cost overrun or delay immediately threatens solvency.

The "better way" analysis reveals a fatal flaw in the "do nothing" vs. "go big" dichotomy. A phased approach—validating the cheaper platform with a limited run before committing to mass volume—is absent. The proposal bets the entire company on a single, unproven leap. The risk is not just missing targets; it is bankruptcy caused by over-extension of supply chains and inability to convert deposits into actual delivered units.

The pre-mortem is clear: one year later, the factory is stalled, batteries are scarce, depositors are angry, and the cash from deposits has been burned on tooling that cannot yet produce sellable cars. There is no kill criterion defined for stopping the bleed if early production metrics fail. Without a proven path to volume or non-refundable commitment, this is a gamble, not a plan.

DECISION: NO-GO
