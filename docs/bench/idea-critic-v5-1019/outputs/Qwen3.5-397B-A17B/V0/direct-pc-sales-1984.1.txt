The claim is that a direct-to-consumer, low-cost IBM-compatible computer assembler can succeed in 1984 by incorporating and scaling purely on revenue, bypassing retail and service networks. The steelman: customers prioritize price and specification over physical inspection, and direct sales eliminate margin-killing intermediaries, allowing undercutting of established brands despite the lack of a physical presence.

However, critical failure modes exist. First, the "try before buy" objection is not merely psychological; in 1984, high-ticket electronics purchases without tactile verification or immediate local support are anomalous. Trust is the primary currency, and a new entity lacks the brand equity to overcome this friction solely via price. Second, the supply chain risk is existential. Established makers have priority access to components and favorable credit terms; a cash-constrained startup relying on revenue growth may face stockouts during demand spikes, halting production exactly when momentum is needed. Third, the absence of a service network creates a liability loop: early failures (inevitable in assembly) require shipping units back, creating long downtimes that destroy reputation before it forms.

The "do nothing" alternative (remaining a small unincorporated entity) limits liability but caps growth. A better way might involve a hybrid model: partnering with existing repair shops for local service fulfillment rather than building a proprietary network, or securing a line of credit against future orders to smooth supply chain volatility. The proposal assumes revenue growth will naturally solve credit and parts availability, but suppliers often require proof of stability before extending terms, creating a catch-22.

The pre-mortem reveals a likely collapse within 18 months due to a component shortage during a sales surge, compounded by a wave of returns that the single-channel logistics cannot handle. The cost of delay is low; waiting to secure a service partner or credit line before aggressive scaling reduces the risk of catastrophic reputation damage. Without a concrete plan to mitigate the service gap and supply credit, the "revenue-only" growth strategy is too fragile. The risks of brand distrust and operational fragility outweigh the margin benefits of the direct model in this specific historical context.

DECISION: NO-GO
