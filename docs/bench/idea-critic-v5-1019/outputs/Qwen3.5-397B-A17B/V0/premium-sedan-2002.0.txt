The claim asserts that a mass-market manufacturer can successfully launch a global luxury sedan under its existing brand name, supported by a dedicated factory and premium specifications, to compete directly with established luxury rivals.

**Steelman:** The vehicle's superior engineering and materials will objectively overcome brand perception barriers, allowing the manufacturer to capture premium market share immediately upon launch.

**Attack:** This proposal ignores the fundamental driver of the luxury segment: status signaling via brand heritage. In 2002, luxury buyers purchase the "badge" as much as the metal. A mass-market logo signals affordability, not exclusivity; placing it on a high-price item creates cognitive dissonance that repels the target demographic. Furthermore, building a dedicated factory for low-volume production destroys unit economics before the first car is sold. The risk of brand dilution is severe: if the luxury attempt fails, it may tarnish the core mass-market reputation for value. The "do nothing" or "create a sub-brand" alternatives offer significantly lower financial risk and higher probability of acceptance by preserving brand separation.

**Better Way:** Establish a distinct sub-brand or acquire an existing luxury marque to isolate the premium identity from the mass-market parent. This avoids the "cheap" perception while leveraging existing engineering. Alternatively, defer the dedicated factory and utilize flexible manufacturing to validate demand before committing capital.

**Kill Criteria:** The proposal lacks a stop condition. It should be halted if pre-order conversion from the mass-market base is below 2% or if brand sentiment tracking shows a net negative shift in the core segment within six months of announcement.

**Verdict:** The strategy fails the "fit" test against luxury market dynamics where image dictates price elasticity. Launching a high-cost, low-volume product under a volume brand without a separate identity is a structural failure waiting to happen. The dedicated factory commitment amplifies this risk into a potential existential financial threat.

DECISION: NO-GO
