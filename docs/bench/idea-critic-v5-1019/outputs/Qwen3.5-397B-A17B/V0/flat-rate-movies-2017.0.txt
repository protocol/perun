The claim that slashing the price to $10/month while paying theaters full ticket price can be sustained by future data revenue fails the basic unit economics test. At $10, a member seeing just two films a month likely exceeds the subscription revenue, given average ticket prices. The proposal relies on "heavy users" driving growth, yet these same users generate the deepest losses per capita under this model.

The "better way" analysis reveals a fatal flaw: the strategy bets on unproven revenue streams (data, studio deals) to subsidize a core product that loses money on every additional use. This is not a growth hack; it is a liquidity trap. The new owner's cash would be burned rapidly to acquire customers who actively drain capital with every visit. Theater chains refusing cooperation is a secondary risk; the primary failure is internal mathematical impossibility without massive, indefinite subsidization.

The pre-mortem is clear: the company runs out of cash before data monetization matures because the contribution margin per user is deeply negative. The "door" is one-way; once prices are cut and expectations set, raising them later will churn the base. The cost of delay is low compared to the cost of executing a broken model.

There is no evidence provided that data revenue can cover the gap between $10 subscriptions and full-price ticket costs. The assumption that studios will pay for data generated by a subsidized user base is speculative and unverified. The proposal asks to scale a loss-making unit before proving a path to profitability.

To proceed would be to ignore the immediate cash bleed in favor of a hypothetical future. The kill criteria cannot even be set because the baseline premise (that volume solves the margin issue) is inverted; here, volume accelerates failure.

DECISION: NO-GO
