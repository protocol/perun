The claim is that installing copy-protection software without clear consent will effectively reduce piracy while maintaining customer trust. The steelman: this is a necessary, immediate defense to stop revenue bleeding from casual copying, assuming customers prioritize access over control of their machines.

However, the proposal fails the "better-way" and "skeptic" tests catastrophically.
1. **Incentive Misalignment**: Treating paying customers as thieves by installing hidden software destroys brand loyalty. The backlash risk is not theoretical; it guarantees negative press and boycotts, likely reducing sales more than piracy does.
2. **Security Reality**: Software that installs silently and resists removal is, by definition, malware. Security researchers will expose vulnerabilities, turning the protection into a vector for actual attacks on users' computers. The company becomes liable for damaging customer infrastructure.
3. **Reversibility**: Once shipped, millions of discs are in the wild. If the software causes system instability or is flagged as a virus, a recall is logistically impossible, and a patch requires admitting the software is dangerous.
4. **Alternatives**: The "do nothing" option preserves trust. A "better way" is offering value-added digital services (early access, bonuses) that copying cannot replicate, rather than fighting a technical war on user hardware.

The pre-mortem is clear: The software triggers antivirus alerts, crashes specific PC configurations, and leads to a class-action lawsuit regarding unauthorized installation. The cost of delay (waiting a week) is negligible compared to the reputational nuclear winter of shipping "rootkit-like" behavior. The proposal lacks any kill criteria or consent mechanism, relying on the false assumption that users will accept invisible control over their devices.

This approach shifts the battle from "music vs. copiers" to "company vs. customers' computer security," a fight the company cannot win. The risk of permanent brand damage and legal liability vastly outweighs the potential recovery of lost sales from casual copying.

DECISION: NO-GO
