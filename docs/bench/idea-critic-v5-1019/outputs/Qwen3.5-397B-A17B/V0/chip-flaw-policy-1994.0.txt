The proposal fails the steelman test: it assumes users can verify a rare, complex mathematical error in their own workflows, which is technically implausible for the vast majority. By shifting the burden of proof to the customer, the company effectively denies relief while admitting the defect exists.

The "better way" analysis reveals a critical flaw in the cost-benefit logic. While a universal recall incurs a massive immediate financial cost, the reputational damage of appearing to hide a defect or gaslight customers about "thousands of years" of safety is irreversible and likely far more expensive long-term. In the information age (even in 1994), news of such flaws spreads faster than a user can encounter them naturally. The risk is not the error rate; it is the perception of dishonesty.

The pre-mortem is clear: if this policy stands, the narrative will shift from "a rare bug" to "a company that refuses to stand by its product." Competitors will exploit this hesitation. The "do nothing" or "limited replacement" options fail because they ignore the psychological contract of trust in hardware. A two-way door (financial loss) is being traded for a one-way door (brand destruction).

The strongest attack survived is the financial magnitude of a full recall; however, this attack collapses when weighed against the existential threat to the brand. The kill criterion here is simple: if the defect is acknowledged internally, it must be acknowledged externally with a solution that requires no effort from the user. Asking users to prove they are affected is a hostile friction point that guarantees public backlash.

The evidence provided states the error is real and the company knows it. The proposal to limit replacements relies on an unverified assumption that users can self-diagnose rare floating-point errors. This assumption is false. Therefore, the proposal creates a high-probability failure mode where the company loses both money (eventually) and trust (immediately).

DECISION: NO-GO
