The proposal attempts to monetize scarcity in a market rapidly shifting toward ownership and convenience. The steelman argues that a low-entry-cost disc with micro-transactions unlocks impulse rentals without subscription commitment. However, the attack reveals fatal structural flaws.

First, the friction is prohibitive. Requiring a specialized player that costs *more* than standard units creates an immediate barrier to entry, while the mandatory phone-line call for every extension introduces latency and privacy concerns unacceptable to consumers. Second, the value proposition is inverted. Customers already dislike paying repeated fees for temporary access (the "rental fatigue" of the era); this model institutionalizes that hatred by making the disc useless without constant payment. Third, the coordination risk is extreme. Securing agreement from both studios and electronics manufacturers for a proprietary, restrictive format in 1998 ignores the industry's momentum toward DVD standardization and eventual digital distribution.

The "better way" analysis shows superior alternatives exist: standard DVD rentals (lower hardware cost, no phone line) or emerging subscription models that remove per-view friction. The "do nothing" option avoids sinking capital into a format likely to be orphaned by competitors.

The pre-mortem is clear: six months post-launch, adoption is near zero because consumers refuse to buy expensive hardware for a format that treats them as revenue streams rather than owners. The cost of delay is negligible compared to the cost of launching a failed standard. The kill criteria are obvious: if hardware partners hesitate or if consumer testing shows resistance to the phone-call requirement, the project must stop. Since the proposal admits these risks without mitigation, and the fundamental mechanics oppose market trends, the claim fails the viability test.

This is a solution in search of a problem, adding complexity where the market demands simplicity. The reliance on unproven behavioral changes (accepting higher hardware costs for restricted media) makes this a speculative gamble with low reversibility once manufacturing begins.

DECISION: NO-GO
