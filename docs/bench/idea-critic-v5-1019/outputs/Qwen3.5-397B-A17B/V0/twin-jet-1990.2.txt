The proposal relies on a single order for 34 aircraft to justify the "very large cost" of an all-new aircraft program. Historically, launch customers for wide-body jets commit to far higher volumes to amortize billions in development; 34 units likely fail to reach break-even, creating immediate financial insolvency risk.

The regulatory hurdle is critical. Twin-engine jets were historically restricted from long overwater routes. Launching before this approval is granted is a fatal gamble; if regulators delay or deny ETOPS (Extended-range Twin-engine Operational Performance Standards) certification, the aircraft loses its primary market advantage over existing tri-jets and four-engine rivals. The proposal treats this unknown as a future formality rather than a current showstopper.

Designing with eight airlines simultaneously invites "feature creep." Conflicting requirements from multiple carriers will inflate complexity, delay entry-into-service, and increase unit cost. With a rival already building new models, speed to market is essential; a design-by-committee approach guarantees the competitor will capture market share first.

The "do nothing" alternative is superior. The manufacturer already possesses two successful wide-body models. Bridging the gap with a derivative or re-engined existing airframe would lower development costs, utilize proven regulatory certifications, and reach the market faster. The proposed all-new design adds unacceptable technical, regulatory, and financial risk without a corresponding order book large enough to secure the investment. The single launch order is insufficient collateral for the exposure described.

DECISION: NO-GO
