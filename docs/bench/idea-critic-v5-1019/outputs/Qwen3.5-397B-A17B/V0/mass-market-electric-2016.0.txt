The claim is that a niche manufacturer can instantly pivot to mass-market production of a cheaper vehicle using refundable deposits to fund scaling. The steelman: pre-orders validate demand and provide cash flow to bridge the gap between low-volume history and high-volume ambition.

However, the failure hypothesis is severe. The company has no proven capability in mass production; scaling from dozens to tens of thousands of units introduces exponential complexity in supply chain, quality control, and assembly line logistics that capital alone cannot solve. Battery supply scaling is a global bottleneck, not a switch this vendor can flip. Refundable deposits create a liability, not revenue; if production slips—as it likely will given the inexperience—refunds trigger a cash crunch, halting the project entirely. The lower price point eliminates the margin buffer needed to absorb the inevitable inefficiencies of a new, unproven high-volume line.

Alternatives include maintaining the low-volume, high-margin strategy to build operational maturity before attempting a cheaper model, or securing non-refundable financing to de-risk the cash flow before promising delivery. The "do nothing" option preserves current solvency.

This is a one-way door with catastrophic downside: failure here likely bankrupts the firm due to refund obligations and wasted capacity investment. The cost of delay is low compared to the cost of a botched launch. The premise that deposits equal guaranteed sales is unverified and dangerous; history shows high cancellation rates when delivery dates slide. The assumption that battery supply can be secured instantly contradicts known industry constraints.

Without a verified path to mass production, secured battery contracts, or non-refundable capital to survive delays, the risk of insolvency outweighs the potential market share gain. The proposal relies on optimistic assumptions about execution speed and customer retention that the company's track record does not support.

DECISION: NO-GO
