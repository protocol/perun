The claim asserts that users will pay a premium for short-form, mobile-only professional content despite the ubiquity of free alternatives. The steelman posits that a gap exists for "prestige" short narratives that social platforms cannot replicate due to quality constraints.

However, the skepticism here is fatal. The proposal ignores the dominant market reality: free, algorithmically optimized short video is already entrenched. Asking users to pay for a constraint (mobile-only) that competitors offer for free with infinite variety is an inverted incentive. The "mobile-only" restriction is not a feature; it is a severe limitation that excludes the primary living-room consumption mode for paid streaming, directly contradicting user behavior where premium content is expected on large screens.

The "better way" analysis reveals a glaring alternative: license existing high-quality short content or launch with TV support immediately. Building a walled garden for phone-only viewing in 2020, when casting and multi-screen ecosystems are standard, creates an artificial ceiling on adoption. The high budget ($1.75B) amplifies this risk; the breakeven point requires massive subscription density that the "phone-only" friction will likely prevent.

The pre-mortem is clear: The service launches, users try it, realize they cannot watch on their TVs, and churn immediately to free social apps or established streamers. The cost of delay is negligible compared to the cost of launching a fundamentally misaligned product. The kill criteria are missing, but the premise itself fails the "fit" test against established viewing habits. The assumption that "professional" alone justifies a paywall for short content, while removing TV access, is unsupported by the 2018 market landscape.

This is a classic case of solving a problem that doesn't exist (lack of high-end short video) while creating a new one (platform restriction). The risks outweigh the unproven value proposition.

DECISION: NO-GO
