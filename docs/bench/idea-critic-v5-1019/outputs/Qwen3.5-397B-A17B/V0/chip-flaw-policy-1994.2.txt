The proposal fails the steelman test because it assumes users can verify a flaw that occurs once in thousands of years, which is practically impossible for most. This creates an immediate conflict: the condition for replacement (proving impact) cannot be met by the very users the policy claims to protect.

The "do nothing" alternative is safer than this half-measure because it avoids establishing a precedent of conditional honesty that appears deceptive. A "replace all" strategy, while costly, eliminates the reputational risk of being seen as hiding a defect. The current plan invites speculation that the company knows more than it admits, fueling the very discussion it hopes to quell.

The pre-mortem is clear: if a single high-profile user demonstrates a catastrophic failure caused by this chip after being denied a replacement, the narrative shifts from "rare glitch" to "corporate negligence." The cost of delay in fixing this perception exceeds the immediate hardware replacement cost. The proposal relies on the assumption that technical rarity equals psychological irrelevance, which is false in a crisis of trust.

The strongest attack survived is the cost argument; however, the reputational damage from a botched rollout likely dwarfs the replacement sum. The plan lacks a kill criterion for when public sentiment turns, leaving the company reactive rather than proactive. Without a mechanism to verify user impact, the policy is functionally a denial of service for legitimate claims.

The dissent ledger notes that offering replacements only upon proof of error is a logical trap. The remaining risk is a total loss of brand equity in the scientific and engineering sectors. The leading metric to watch is media sentiment, but waiting for it to sour is too late. The proposal must be rejected in favor of a universal replacement or a transparent, no-questions-asked exchange program to restore confidence immediately.
