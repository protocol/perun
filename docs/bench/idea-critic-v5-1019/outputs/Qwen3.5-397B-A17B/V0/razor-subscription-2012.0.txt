The claim is that a low-budget humorous video and simple website can disrupt dominant razor brands via subscription in 2012. Steelman: Direct-to-consumer economics allow significantly lower prices than incumbents burdened by retail markups and massive ad spend, while humor bypasses traditional advertising costs to acquire customers efficiently.

However, critical failure modes exist. First, supplier dependency is fatal; if the sole supplier cuts off access or raises prices under incumbent pressure, the business halts immediately with no inventory control. Second, customer behavior risk: shaving is habitual, and convincing users to trust a mailed blade over established brands requires overcoming high switching inertia, which a single video may fail to achieve. Third, retaliation risk: dominants can temporarily slash prices or flood channels with litigation to crush a capital-poor entrant before it scales.

Better ways include securing multiple suppliers before launch to mitigate supply chain hold-up, or validating demand through pre-orders before building the full site. The "do nothing" option avoids capital loss but cedes the low-price segment. The cost of delay allows competitors to lock in more subscribers, but rushing without supply redundancy invites immediate collapse.

The strongest attack survived is the marketing hypothesis: while a single video might fail, the 2012 digital landscape allowed viral outliers to succeed where traditional ads would bankrupt a startup. The humor angle specifically targets the absurdity of overpriced blades, creating a narrative incumbents cannot easily copy without mocking their own premium pricing. Yet, without verified supply contracts and a clearer path to recurring retention beyond the first shipment, the operational risk outweighs the marketing potential. The proposal lacks a contingency for supplier failure or a verified mechanism to ensure repeat shipments, which are the actual economic engine, not the initial sale.

DECISION: NO-GO
