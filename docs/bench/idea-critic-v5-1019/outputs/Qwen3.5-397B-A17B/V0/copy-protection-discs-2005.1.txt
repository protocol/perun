The claim is that installing copy-protection software without clear consent will reduce piracy enough to offset reputational and security risks. The steelman: if the software effectively blocks casual copying and installs seamlessly, it protects immediate revenue streams from mass duplication.

However, the attack reveals fatal flaws in this approach. First, the "intrusive" nature described—installing without clear consent—violates the fundamental trust between a vendor and a customer. In the 2005 landscape, users are increasingly aware of spyware and rootkits; software that hides its installation mechanism will be flagged by security tools and vilified by the press. Second, the risk of "weaknesses" is not theoretical; any software that intercepts system calls to block copying creates a new attack surface. If hackers bypass the protection (which they inevitably will), they will likely exploit the underlying vulnerability to compromise the user's PC, turning a sales protection tool into a vector for malware.

The "better way" analysis shows that "doing nothing" or using standard, transparent DRM (clearly labeled on the box) preserves brand equity. The proposed path treats all customers as thieves, alienating the loyal base while failing to stop determined pirates. The pre-mortem is clear: six months post-launch, a major security blog exposes the software as a rootkit that cannot be uninstalled, leading to class-action lawsuits, a massive recall, and permanent brand damage that far exceeds lost sales from copying.

The kill criteria are missing because the proposal lacks a safe rollback plan or a threshold for acceptable backlash. The cost of delay (waiting for a transparent solution) is low compared to the catastrophic risk of a security scandal. The evidence from similar early-2000s attempts suggests that opaque installation methods trigger immediate community backlash and technical neutralization.

This proposal fails the "do no harm" principle of software distribution. The reputational destruction and legal liability outweigh the potential recovery of lost sales. The method of installation is fundamentally flawed.

DECISION: NO-GO
