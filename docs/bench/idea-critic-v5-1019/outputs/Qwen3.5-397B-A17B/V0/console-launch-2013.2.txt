The proposal attempts to correct past failures by prioritizing developer ease, consumer ownership rights (used games/offline), and aggressive underpricing. The steelman is that these three pillars will rapidly rebuild market share lost to a rival with a head start in online services.

However, the attack reveals a fatal financial contradiction. Launching below a rival's price while accepting reduced margins per unit creates a dependency on massive volume to sustain operations. This volume is threatened by the admitted lack of first-party software at launch and a contracting consumer market for dedicated hardware. Without a flagship title to drive hardware adoption, the lower price point may simply accelerate cash burn without securing loyalty, especially when the rival's entrenched online network offers sticky value that price alone cannot easily displace.

The "better way" analysis suggests that competing solely on price and hardware openness ignores the rival's core strength: services. A "do nothing" or "wait" approach might preserve capital, but the "do nothing" option guarantees continued erosion. The critical flaw is the absence of a funded plan to bridge the software gap. Relying on third-party developers to fill the launch window void is speculative, given the rival's existing install base.

Furthermore, the pre-mortem indicates a high probability of failure if the hardware subsidy drains resources needed for marketing or acquiring exclusive content. The cost of delay is high, but the cost of a poorly funded launch is existential. The proposal lacks a specific kill criterion regarding cash reserves or a minimum pre-order threshold to validate the volume assumption before full-scale manufacturing.

While the consumer-friendly stance on used games is a strong differentiator, it is insufficient to overcome the financial fragility of selling hardware at a loss without a guaranteed software pipeline. The risk of bleeding cash on every unit sold, without the software ecosystem to recoup losses via licensing, is too great given the current market contraction.

DECISION: NO-GO
