**Steelman:** Rapidly dominate urban on-demand logistics by subsidizing user acquisition with free, fast delivery, betting that scale will eventually lower unit economics below subsidy costs.

**Attack:** The unit economics are structurally negative. Delivering low-margin items (snacks, books) with free shipping and no minimum order guarantees a loss on every transaction. The cost of employing direct staff and maintaining vehicles in major cities creates massive fixed overhead before variable revenue is realized. Unlike asset-light models, this approach scales losses linearly with expansion; entering more cities faster accelerates cash burn without a path to profitability. The assumption that venture capital can indefinitely subsidize a behavior (ordering small items frequently) that destroys value per unit is unproven and historically fragile. There is no "two-way door" here; once capital is spent on hiring and city infrastructure, it cannot be recovered if the subsidy model fails to convert users to profitable behavior.

**Better Way:** Start with a high minimum order value to ensure positive contribution margin per delivery. Use independent contractors instead of direct employees to reduce fixed costs and legal liability. Limit expansion to a single city to prove unit economics before raising significant capital.

**Kill Criteria:** If the cost per delivery exceeds the gross margin of the average order after 10,000 deliveries in the pilot city, the model is broken. If customer retention drops below 20% once subsidies are reduced, the demand is artificial.

**Verdict:** The proposal demands immediate, massive capital expenditure on a model where every sale loses money. It ignores the fundamental constraint that logistics costs for small items exceed their value. Expanding quickly multiplies these losses. The risks of insolvency are immediate and certain under the proposed terms.

DECISION: NO-GO
