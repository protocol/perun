The claim is that a cheap, remote-less HDMI dongle controlled by personal devices will succeed where previous dedicated TV devices failed. The steelman: this minimizes hardware cost and complexity by leveraging existing user devices as controllers, lowering the barrier to entry for both the company and consumers.

However, critical flaws exist. First, the "no remote" assumption ignores the living room context: users often want to browse content on the TV screen itself, not just cast from a phone. Without an on-screen menu or remote, the device may be unusable for discovery, confusing buyers and increasing support costs. Second, the "few apps at launch" risk is existential; a streaming device without content is a paperweight. Established competitors have large catalogs, making this value proposition weak. Third, the "almost no margin" pricing leaves no room for error in manufacturing, support, or marketing, turning any small hurdle into a financial loss.

Better ways exist: include a basic remote and simple on-screen UI to ensure standalone usability, even if it raises the price slightly. Alternatively, partner with existing TV manufacturers to embed the software directly, avoiding hardware risks entirely. The "do nothing" option avoids sinking capital into a low-margin, high-risk hardware play in a crowded market.

The pre-mortem: The device launches, users cannot navigate without their phones, app support lags, and returns surge due to confusion. The kill criterion is clear: if user testing shows >20% failure rate in basic content playback without phone assistance, the project must stop. The leading metric is "daily active users per unit sold" within 30 days of launch; if this is below 40%, the product is failing.

Given the high risk of user confusion, the lack of a content moat, and the razor-thin margin offering no buffer for failure, the proposal as stated is too fragile. It requires revision to address usability and content strategy before approval.

DECISION: NO-GO
