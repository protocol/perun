The claim is that a proprietary, phone-dependent disc format with recurring fees will succeed in the 1998 market despite higher hardware costs and studio fragmentation. The steelman: this model captures long-tail revenue from repeat viewings and locks out used-disc resale, creating a sustainable ecosystem if adoption hits critical mass early.

However, the attack reveals fatal flaws in the current context. First, the "better way" of existing VHS rentals and emerging DVD purchases offers immediate compatibility and no extra hardware cost; customers already accept rental windows without needing specialized players. Second, the requirement for studios and electronics makers to align on a new, incompatible format introduces massive coordination risk; history shows format wars (e.g., Beta vs. VHS) stall markets until one clear winner emerges, and this proposal creates a third, fragmented option. Third, the "phone line" dependency for verification is a friction point that increases failure rates and customer support costs compared to physical media norms.

The pre-mortem is clear: the format fails because consumers refuse to buy expensive, single-purpose players for a service model they can approximate cheaper via existing rental stores. The "repeated viewing fee" is perceived as a penalty rather than a value add, especially when the disc physically exists in the user's home. The cost of delay is low; waiting for DVD infrastructure to mature or for digital distribution to become viable is safer than betting on a hybrid analog-digital bridge that satisfies neither camp.

The strongest attack survived is the potential for high-margin recurring revenue, but this relies on adoption levels that the high hardware barrier prevents. There is no kill criterion that saves this; the fundamental value proposition (pay more for less flexibility) contradicts consumer behavior in 1998. The risk of stranding customers with unusable discs if the service folds is too high.

DECISION: NO-GO
