The proposal to launch a luxury SUV under a sports-car brand in 2002 faces immediate existential threats that the current plan fails to neutralize. The primary failure mode is brand dilution: loyal enthusiasts, the core revenue base for a small-range maker, may perceive a heavy, off-road capable vehicle as a betrayal of the brand's performance heritage. Unlike established luxury makers with diversified portfolios, a niche sports-car maker lacks the brand equity buffer to absorb this identity shock. If the core following abandons the marque, the high fixed costs of developing a large vehicle on a shared platform could bankrupt the company before the SUV finds a new audience.

The "co-development" angle mitigates some engineering risk but introduces dependency risk; the brand becomes a junior partner in its own product definition. Furthermore, entering a segment dominated by strong, established players with a single, high-priced model is a classic "me-too" trap. The proposal argues for "powerful engine and off-road ability," yet these are table stakes in 2002, not differentiators. There is no stated mechanism to translate the sports-car DNA into the SUV experience (e.g., handling dynamics superior to rivals) that would justify the premium to skeptics.

The financial exposure is asymmetric: high first-year costs against an unproven market fit within the existing customer base. The plan lacks a "go-if" condition, such as securing pre-orders from current owners or a guaranteed buy-back clause from the larger partner if sales miss targets. Without a concrete kill criterion or a phased rollout to test brand elasticity, this is an all-in gamble on a strategic pivot that contradicts the company's historical north star. The risk of destroying the brand's unique value proposition outweighs the potential revenue expansion.

DECISION: NO-GO
