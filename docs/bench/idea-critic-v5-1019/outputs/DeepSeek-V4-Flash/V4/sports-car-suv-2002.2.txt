First call: GO. The enthusiast core is small; the real money is in new buyers.

Steelman: A shared-platform luxury SUV lets the brand reach affluent families who would never buy a two-seat sports car, funding future sports models without diluting the core line — Porsche’s later Cayenne proved this exact logic.

Better ways: Do nothing (stagnation, small niche). Sell a badge-engineered SUV without engineering input (cheap, but kills credibility). A smaller, less expensive crossover (lower risk, but less margin and less distinct).

Fit: The brand’s north star is survival through profit; a high-margin SUV serves that. Risk to the sports image is real but manageable if the SUV is genuinely fast and capable.

Pre-mortem: It launches heavy, soft, and overpriced; enthusiasts mock it; it sells poorly; the brand is damaged. One-way door: high first-year investment, but the platform is shared, so exit costs are limited. Delay cost: competitors consolidate the segment now.

Evidence: All figures are assumed — no market data, no cost numbers, no brand-equity metrics are given. The proposal states “loyal customers may see betrayal” and “established makers are strong,” but these are risks, not proven blockers.
