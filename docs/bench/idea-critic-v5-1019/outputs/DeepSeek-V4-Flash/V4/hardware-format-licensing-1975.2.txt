First call: GO. The proposal bets on quality and lead, but the rival’s open licensing and longer tapes directly attack the core assumptions of demand and ecosystem.

Steelman: Proprietary control preserves margins and quality leadership, while an early lead and superior picture can anchor a premium niche before the rival matures.

Attack: The stated facts already undermine the claim. The rival’s many licensees will flood players, drop prices, and attract title producers—network effects you cannot match with few partners. One-hour tapes fail the primary use case: a full film or sports event. Fewer makers means fewer pre-recorded titles, shrinking consumer value. The rival’s path to becoming the standard is not speculative; it is the direct consequence of the facts given. Your own evidence shows the target users will defect to longer tapes and more titles, regardless of picture quality.

Better ways: License broadly to match the rival’s spread, or double down on a two-hour tape before launch. Do nothing is worse—you lose the lead.

Kill criteria: If, within 12 months, the rival signs more than twice your licensees or any top-10 title is exclusive to its format, stop the proprietary strategy and pivot to open licensing.

Blocker: The proposal’s own facts—one-hour limit, few partners, fewer titles—remove its route to winning the standard. Risks are not the issue; the stated negatives are already true.

DECISION: NO-GO
