First call: GO — the company is profitable, and a premium design-led product can expand margin and brand without cannibalizing the tower.

Steelman: This is a deliberate design statement—a silent, minimalist desktop that trades expandability for aesthetics and quiet, targeting buyers who value form and simplicity over tinkering, and who are willing to pay a premium.

Attack: The core risk is demand. The proposal states buyers who want to upgrade will reject it, and it costs more than a faster tower or the popular all-in-one. That means the target buyer must be someone who wants a desktop, not an all-in-one, doesn't upgrade, and pays more for less function. That is a narrow niche. The manufacturing flaw risk is a cost execution issue—manageable with engineering. The pricing risk is a positioning issue—premium can work if the design story is strong.

However, nothing in the stated facts proves the niche is large enough to justify the product, nor that the premium price will be accepted. The company is profitable, so it can absorb a flop, but the proposal offers no evidence of demand, no market test, and no fallback if the niche is too small.

Better ways: Do nothing (keep selling the tower and all-in-one), or launch a limited, higher-margin variant with a clear design story and a small production run to test demand before committing to full manufacturing.

Kill criteria: If a limited run does not sell through at the premium price within a quarter, stop.

Blockers: None — no stated fact proves it fails; the risks are unproven.
Risks: Narrow demand, manufacturing flaws, premium price rejection.

DECISION: GO
