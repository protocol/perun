First call: NO-GO. The core economics do not work on the stated facts.

Steelman: Rapid membership growth at a low price creates scale, data value, and negotiating leverage with studios and theaters, eventually making the model profitable.

The proposal pays theaters full ticket price for every film seen. At roughly $10 per month for one film a day, a member seeing even two films a month costs the company more than the fee. Heavy users—who are the most likely to join—will cost far more than they pay. The stated revenue from data and studio deals is unproven and speculative, not a contracted or demonstrated stream.

The alternatives are not considered: a higher-priced tier that limits usage, a per-film surcharge, or a cap on monthly viewings. Doing nothing preserves cash. The proposal's own numbers show the fee cannot cover the ticket cost for any meaningful usage level. This is not a risk; it is a certainty stated in the proposal itself.

The pre-mortem is clear: the company grows membership, pays full ticket prices for heavy users, the new revenue streams fail to materialize, and the subsidy runs out. The one-way door is spending real cash on an unproven model with no kill criterion tied to a measurable metric within a bounded window.

The kill criteria are absent: no cap on the subsidy, no date to reassess, no metric that would trigger a stop. Without these, the plan fails as proposed. The evidence in hand contradicts the core claim that growth alone leads to profitability.

Blockers: the fee does not cover the cost of tickets for any but the lightest users; the new revenue streams are unproven; no cap or exit metric is named. Risks: theater refusals, data value not realized.

DECISION: NO-GO
