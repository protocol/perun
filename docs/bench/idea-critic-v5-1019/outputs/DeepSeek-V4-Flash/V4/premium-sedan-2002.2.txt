**First call: NO-GO.** The stated facts already show the target buyers value the badge, and the plan offers no credible path to overcome that.

**Steelman:** A technically superior, world-class sedan at parity pricing could force its way into the premium segment on merit, leveraging the parent’s scale for quality and cost.

**Options:** Do nothing (maintains profitable mass-market position, no downside). Build a smaller, regional pilot under a sub-brand (tests demand at lower risk). The proposal as stated—dedicated factory, global launch, premium price, mass-market badge—is the most expensive and least reversible option.

**Fit:** Entering premium is plausible, but the brand’s mass-market identity directly contradicts the stated buyer psychology.

**Pre-mortem:** The sedan launches, receives good reviews, but sells poorly. Luxury buyers—who explicitly “pay for the badge”—see a cheap brand name on a very expensive car. Low volumes fail to cover the dedicated factory’s fixed costs, dragging down the parent’s margins. The brand is damaged in its core market by appearing to overreach.

**Evidence:** The proposal itself lists the risks: buyers pay for the badge; the brand has no luxury image; low-volume production is expensive. These are stated as facts, not speculation. The plan offers no counter-evidence—no market study showing badge resistance is weak, no plan to build a sub-brand, no pilot.

**Blocker:** The proposal’s own stated facts—that buyers pay for the badge and the brand lacks a luxury image—already show the target users are unlikely to want this product. A cheaper alternative (sub-brand, regional pilot) reaches the same upside at far lower risk and cost.

**Kill criteria:** None proposed.

**Decision:** The plan fails as proposed. Entering premium may be right, but not this way.

DECISION: NO-GO
