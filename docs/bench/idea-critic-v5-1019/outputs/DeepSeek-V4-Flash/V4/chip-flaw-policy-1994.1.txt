First call: NO-GO. The proposal’s own logic collapses under the stated facts.

The core claim is that the flaw is “not serious” because the error is rare. But the proposal simultaneously admits the problem is “widely discussed” and that users “cannot easily show” they are affected. If users cannot prove impact, your offer of replacement “only to those affected” is effectively no offer at all. That is not a mitigation; it is a refusal dressed as a policy. The stated facts already show the target users cannot use the proposed path, so the plan fails as proposed.

The cost of replacement is large, but the cost of the alternative is larger and irreversible. A chip maker selling a processor with a known arithmetic defect, while publicly minimizing it and requiring proof from customers, will be perceived as hiding a defect. The reputation risk is not speculative; it is the direct and foreseeable outcome of the stated policy. The pre-mortem writes itself: the bug becomes public, the company’s response looks evasive, and trust in the entire product line—not just this chip—erodes.

A two-way door exists: replace chips for all who ask, cap the program, and absorb the cost. That is expensive but bounded and reversible. The proposed policy is cheap but permanently damaging. The cheaper alternative does not reach the same upside; it destroys it.

The correct call is to replace for all who ask, state the facts plainly, and take the short-term hit to preserve long-term trust. The proposal’s own evidence—rare error, but unprovable impact and wide discussion—makes the restrictive policy untenable.

DECISION: NO-GO
