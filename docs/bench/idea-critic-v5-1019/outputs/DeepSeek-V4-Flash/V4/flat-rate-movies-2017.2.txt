First call: NO-GO. The core economics are inverted at the stated price.

The steelman: a $10 monthly fee for one film a day could attract massive membership, and the resulting data and studio/theater deals would eventually cover the per-ticket subsidy, turning a cash-burning subscriber base into a profitable platform.

Against do nothing: the current small, high-price base is sustainable but stagnant. Against alternatives: a moderate price cut with a per-member cap or a two-tier plan (limited films vs. unlimited) would test demand and deal appetite at far lower cash burn.

The stated facts break the claim. At $10/month, a member seeing just two films (average U.S. ticket ~$9) costs the company $18 in ticket payments alone—an $8 monthly loss before any overhead. A heavy user seeing ten films costs $90 against $10 in revenue. The proposal names no cap, no deal terms, no data monetization timeline, and no evidence theaters will accept discounted bulk arrangements. The revenue streams are entirely unproven, and the risk section concedes the two largest cost drivers (full ticket price, heavy users) are unmitigated.

This is a one-way door for cash: the subsidy scales with membership, so success (rapid growth) accelerates losses. Without a pilot, a per-member cap, or a pre-negotiated theater discount, the plan fails its own arithmetic.

A cheaper, staged alternative—launch at $15 with a two-film cap, negotiate a 20% discount with two theater chains, and measure data-deal interest before scaling—reaches the same upside with a fraction of the burn. The proposal does not address this.

DECISION: NO-GO
