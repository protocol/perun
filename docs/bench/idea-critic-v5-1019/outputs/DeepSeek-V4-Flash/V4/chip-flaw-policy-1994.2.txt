First call: GO. The flaw is rare, and a targeted replacement is defensible if handled transparently.

Steelman: The flaw is cosmically rare in practice, so a full recall is disproportionate. Offering replacement only to demonstrably affected users is financially rational and technically honest, provided the company is open about the issue.

Attack: The core risk is not the math but the perception of concealment. If users cannot easily prove impact, the policy looks like a dodge, and the "widely discussed" context means trust is already fragile. A targeted program without a clear, public explanation could turn a minor defect into a reputational crisis.

Better ways: A full replacement is overkill. A better path: publicly disclose the flaw, explain its rarity in plain terms, and offer free replacement to any user who requests it, without requiring proof. This costs more than the proposed policy but far less than a blanket recall, and it converts a potential scandal into a demonstration of candor.

Kill criteria: If media coverage or customer complaints spike beyond a set threshold within 90 days, or if the "proof" requirement becomes the dominant story, the policy fails. The leading metric is the ratio of requests to replacements and the tone of public discussion.

Decision: The proposal as written is too narrow. Requiring proof invites distrust and is impractical. The safer, nearly as cheap route is full disclosure plus replacement on request, no proof needed. That preserves the cost benefit while neutralizing the concealment charge.

DECISION: NO-GO
