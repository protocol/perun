First call: GO, but the stated risks are decisive. The proposal asks to bet an entire format war on picture quality and a brief lead, while the rival is already doing the one thing that wins standards battles: broad licensing that drives player volume, lower prices, and title availability. A proprietary, few-partner strategy concedes the ecosystem before the fight starts. One-hour tapes are a hard functional ceiling against a longer-tape rival for films and sports, and the proposal offers no counter — no roadmap to longer tapes, no content deals, no pricing response. The early lead erodes exactly as the rival’s licensed base scales. The failure mode is not speculative; it is the stated structure of the market. The correct move is to open licensing broadly now, matching the rival’s spread, while pushing tape length and securing pre-recorded titles. Keeping it proprietary maximizes short‑term margin per unit but sacrifices the standard, which is the only durable asset. The proposal’s own evidence — rival licensing to many, longer tapes, title risk — already contains the negative that removes its route to winning. No amount of picture quality compensates for being the Betamax of 1975. Approving as proposed locks in a losing trajectory.

DECISION: NO-GO
