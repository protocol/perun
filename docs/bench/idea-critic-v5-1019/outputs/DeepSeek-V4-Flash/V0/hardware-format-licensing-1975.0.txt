DECISION: NO-GO

The proposal rests on the belief that superior picture quality and a head start will overcome the rival’s open licensing strategy. That is a weak foundation. The rival’s approach—many makers, lower prices, more titles—directly attacks the two things that determine consumer adoption in a format war: availability of content and affordability of hardware. A one-hour tape is a hard functional limit; it cannot capture a full film or a typical sports broadcast. That is not a minor drawback, it is a disqualifier for the primary use case of the medium. Fewer partners mean fewer players in stores and fewer pre-recorded cassettes on shelves. Retailers stock what sells, and they will stock the format with more machines and more titles. Early lead is real, but it erodes quickly once the rival’s network effect compounds. The risk that the rival becomes the standard is not a distant possibility; it is the most likely outcome given the structural advantages. The proposal offers no counter to that network effect, no plan to expand licensing, no response to the tape-length gap. It assumes quality wins, but in a standards battle, ecosystem breadth beats technical edge. Approving this as proposed means accepting a likely loss of the market.
