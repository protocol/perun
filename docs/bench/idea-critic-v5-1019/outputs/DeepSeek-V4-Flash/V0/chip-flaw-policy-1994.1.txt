DECISION: NO-GO

The proposal underestimates the reputational and legal risk of a selective replacement policy. In 1994, with the problem already widely discussed, asking users to “prove” they are affected creates a perception of concealment and shifts the burden unfairly. This will likely amplify negative press, invite class-action lawsuits, and erode trust far more than the cost of a full replacement. The “rare error” framing, while statistically true, ignores that even a single wrong result in financial, scientific, or engineering calculations can cause severe downstream harm. Offering replacements only to those who can demonstrate impact is impractical—most users cannot verify such rare failures—and will be seen as an admission of guilt without offering a clean solution. A full, transparent recall, though expensive, preserves credibility and limits legal exposure. The company should replace all chips, publicly acknowledge the flaw, and commit to rigorous testing for future revisions.
