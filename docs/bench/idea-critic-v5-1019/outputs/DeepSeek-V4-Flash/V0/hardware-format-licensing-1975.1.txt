DECISION: NO-GO

The proposal’s core bet—that proprietary control plus superior picture quality will overcome a rival’s open licensing—fails on basic market dynamics. The rival’s strategy of spreading players across many manufacturers will drive down hardware costs and expand distribution, creating a larger installed base. That base, not picture quality, determines which format attracts pre-recorded titles and becomes the standard. Your one-hour tape is a hard limitation: it cannot capture a full film or a full sports event, which are the two highest-value use cases for home recording. The rival’s longer tape directly solves this. Fewer partners mean fewer retail shelves, fewer titles, and higher prices—a self-reinforcing decline. An early lead is irrelevant if the ecosystem you build is smaller and more expensive. The risk that the rival becomes the standard is not speculative; it is the probable outcome of their model. You are betting on a superior artifact in a market that rewards network effects. That bet is structurally wrong. The proposal offers no counter to the rival’s cost and scale advantage, and no path to convert your quality edge into market share before the rival’s base locks in the standard. Approving this now locks you into a losing war of attrition.
