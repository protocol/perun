DECISION: NO-GO

The steelman: a small, high-quality burrito chain could become a premium fast-casual niche, with the option to scale later at low cost, diversifying beyond the core burger model.

But the claim fails on evidence and fit. The chain is 16 units against a giant’s thousands; no data shows unit economics, same-store sales, or that the premium price covers higher labor and slower throughput. Scaling a made-to-order model with skilled workers contradicts the giant’s core competency of speed and consistency. The minority stake offers no control to fix operational drift, and the “right to invest more later” is a one-way door only if the chain proves itself—but nothing here proves it will.

The better way: do nothing now, or run a small, time-boxed pilot in one or two markets with a clear kill metric (e.g., store-level margin below X% or customer wait over Y minutes within 12 months). That beats paying for 16 unproven units.

The pre-mortem: management gets distracted chasing a premium niche, the chain’s higher costs never scale, and the giant’s core brand is diluted by association with slower, pricier food. The cost of delay is low—this is not a scarce asset—so waiting a quarter to test one store loses nothing.

Kill criteria: if a pilot cannot hit a 15% store-level margin or a 5-minute average order time within 12 months, stop and do not invest further.

The strongest attack survived: that the option to invest later is cheap and reversible. It fails because the initial minority stake is still an irreversible capital commitment with no operational control, and no evidence supports the chain’s profitability.

Remaining risk: if the chain is secretly highly profitable, waiting loses a year—but that is unproven and unlikely given the labor and price constraints.
