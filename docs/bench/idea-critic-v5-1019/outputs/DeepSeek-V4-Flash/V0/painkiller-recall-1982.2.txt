DECISION: NO-GO

The proposal’s core failure is that it treats a localized contamination event as a systemic product defect. The facts state the poisoning occurred *after* leaving the factory and that the company’s own plants appear unaffected. A nationwide withdrawal therefore punishes the entire market for a criminal act outside the company’s control, converting a contained crisis into a self-inflicted brand catastrophe.

The risks cited are not abstract—they are the predictable, near-certain consequences of the proposed action. A very large recall cost for a possibly local problem means the company pays maximum price for minimum risk reduction. Competitors will indeed take the shelf space, and a nationwide halt signals danger to consumers who never saw a poisoned bottle, permanently associating the brand with harm. The later relaunch, even in tamper-resistant packaging, cannot undo the message that the product itself was unsafe.

The proposal’s own logic contradicts its goal. If the threat is a single unknown person in one metro area, the correct scope is that area—pull those bottles, cooperate with law enforcement, communicate transparently about the specific, external nature of the threat, and offer tamper-resistant packaging as an industry-leading response, not a retreat. A nationwide withdrawal does not catch the poisoner; it only surrenders market leadership.

The better way is targeted, not total. Contain the known risk, preserve the brand’s integrity, and let the facts—not fear—define the response. The proposal as written is an overreaction that guarantees the very outcomes it fears.
