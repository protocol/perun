DECISION: NO-GO

The proposal asks the company to commit to a massive, irreversible scaling effort based on refundable deposits and an unproven production ramp. The core risk is not demand, but execution: the company has no track record at the required volume, and the battery supply chain is a hard external dependency. A miss on either turns the lower-margin sedan into a cash-burning liability, not a growth story.

The refundable deposit structure is a red flag: it signals customer hesitation and converts to sales only if the company delivers on time and at price. With little margin, any cost overrun or production delay erodes the business case entirely.

The two-vehicle, low-volume history means the company lacks the institutional knowledge—tooling, supplier management, quality control, workforce training—to jump to far higher volumes in one year. The proposal offers no plan to de-risk that ramp, no contingency for battery shortfalls, and no kill criterion tied to deposits converting or production hitting milestones.

The better path is to prove the model at a smaller, controlled scale first: launch the sedan in a limited market or with a modest production target, validate the battery supply and manufacturing process, and then expand. This preserves the brand's credibility, limits financial exposure, and gives the company a chance to learn before betting the firm.

The idea is directionally sound, but the timing and scale are reckless. Approving it as proposed invites a failure that could sink the company.
