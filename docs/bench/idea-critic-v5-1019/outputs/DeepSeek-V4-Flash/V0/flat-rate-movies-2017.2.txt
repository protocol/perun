DECISION: NO-GO

The proposal’s core economics are fatally mispriced. At $10/month with one film per day, a member seeing just two films in a month costs the company roughly $20–$30 in full-price tickets, while revenue is only $10. Even modest usage guarantees a gross loss per subscriber. The plan’s stated future revenues—data monetization and studio/theater deals—are speculative and unproven, with no evidence they can close the gap. The risk that heavy users (the most engaged segment, likely drawn to a $10 all-you-can-watch offer) will consume far more than they pay is not a tail risk but the expected outcome.

Subsidizing this with owner cash merely converts cash burn into a larger, faster cash burn. Growth without a path to unit profitability is not a strategy; it is a donation. The theater chains’ refusal to cooperate is a real, immediate blocker: without their participation, the service cannot fulfill its core promise, and their leverage to demand even better terms will grow as the service’s dependence on them increases.

The proposal fails the basic test: it cannot survive the arithmetic of its own pricing. The correct move is to revise the model—raise the price, cap usage, or secure per-ticket discounts from theaters before scaling—or do not proceed.
