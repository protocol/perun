DECISION: NO-GO

The proposal’s core appeal—cheap, phone-controlled, open—fails against the stated history: prior TV device attempts sold poorly, signaling weak consumer pull for the company’s content on the big screen. A dongle with no remote or menu demands buyers already comfortable casting from a phone, a behavior not yet mainstream in 2013. The “open kit” is speculative; few apps at launch means the device’s value is almost entirely the buyer’s own phone content, which they already have on the phone.

Margin is near zero, so volume must be enormous to matter. That volume is unlikely without a killer use case or a large existing app ecosystem—both absent. Established streaming boxes already offer robust catalogs and simple remotes; the dongle’s only advantage is price, which is undercut by its poor usability and thin software.

The strongest version of the claim is “cheap + phone control removes friction and cost,” but that ignores the real friction: buyers must learn to cast, and they lose the familiar lean-back TV experience. The failure scenario is concrete: early adopters buy, find little to watch beyond their own files, and return or abandon it, killing retail momentum and developer interest before the kit matures.

Doing nothing costs little; the company can wait, watch phone-casting adoption grow, and then launch with better app support and a clearer use case. Revising later is far cheaper than launching now into a crowded, low-margin market with a confusing product. The cost of delay is minimal; the cost of a failed launch is brand damage and wasted engineering.

No kill criterion here can save it—the product’s core premise is the risk. Approve only when the ecosystem and user behavior are proven.
