The proposal’s core bet is that buyers already own a smart device, so the dongle simply mirrors or casts content, eliminating the cost and complexity of a remote and on-screen UI. The earlier poor sales of a television device are not directly comparable, as that was likely a full box with its own interface, not a phone-dependent accessory. The open kit addresses the app gap by letting third parties build support without the company’s own engineering bottleneck.

However, the risks are severe. No remote or menu means the buyer must understand the phone-as-control paradigm immediately—a friction that mainstream TV buyers historically reject. The near-zero margin means no retail or marketing budget, so the product will rely on word-of-mouth and early adopters. Established boxes with huge catalogs will be cheaper to buy for many consumers who already know how they work. The lack of launch apps means the first experience could be a blank screen unless the phone app is flawless.

The proposal does not specify pricing, exact launch app list, or how the company will educate buyers on the phone-control model. It also does not address what happens when a guest wants to watch TV but has no phone paired. The open kit is promising but unproven in generating content quickly enough to counter the incumbents.

Given the history of poor TV device sales, the minimal margin, and the high risk of buyer confusion without a remote, this is not ready for immediate approval. It needs a clearer go-to-market plan, a fallback for non-phone users, and at least a few must-have apps confirmed. The idea has merit, but the execution as proposed is too fragile.

DECISION: NO-GO
