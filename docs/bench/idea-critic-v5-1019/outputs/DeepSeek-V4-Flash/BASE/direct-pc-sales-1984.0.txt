The proposal leverages a lean, asset-light model in a market where IBM-compatible hardware is rapidly commoditizing. Direct sales cut channel costs, enabling aggressive pricing that established brands with retail overhead cannot easily match. In 1984, the PC market is still young, with many first-time buyers more concerned with price and specifications than with in-store experience, especially since the product is standardized. The service risk is mitigated by the modularity of parts—customers can swap components, and a mail-order model can ship replacements cheaply. Credit from suppliers is a cash-flow challenge, but incorporation limits personal liability, and growth from revenue forces disciplined scaling. The absence of stores is not a weakness if marketing targets early adopters and businesses already comfortable with mail-order catalogs. Established brands’ channels are slow and expensive; they cannot pivot quickly to direct sales without alienating their resellers. The proposal does not require external capital, avoiding dilution, and the direct feedback loop from buyers sharpens product configuration. The main risk—customer hesitation without hands-on trials—is addressable through generous return policies and detailed spec sheets, which cost little. The market is expanding fast enough that a niche, price-led entrant can gain traction before giants react. No evidence suggests the founder lacks technical or operational competence. The timing is optimal: IBM’s dominance has legitimized the standard, but brand loyalty is not yet entrenched among budget buyers. Approving now captures first-mover advantage in the direct, no-frills segment.
