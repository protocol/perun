The proposal leverages a clear cost advantage and a lean model in a market where established players are slow and expensive. Direct order removes retail markup and inventory overhead, allowing aggressive pricing that undercuts brands—a compelling value proposition for price-sensitive buyers, especially students and hobbyists.  

The risks are real but manageable. Buyers wanting to try before buying are a subset, not the majority, given the price gap. The lack of a service network can be offset by modular, standardized parts—customers can swap components themselves, and a mail-in or phone-support model suffices for this niche. Established brands’ channels are a weakness, not a strength, in this emerging market segment; their overhead forces higher prices, and they are not agile.  

Supplier credit is the only genuine hurdle, but incorporation limits personal liability, and small initial orders—paid cash on delivery—build a track record. Revenue can be reinvested to grow inventory gradually, avoiding debt. The market is early: IBM-compatibles are becoming a standard, and direct order is proven in other industries (e.g., mail-order electronics).  

The founder’s student status suggests low personal overhead and high technical competence. The proposal is simple, scalable, and does not require external capital upfront. The risks raised are speculative, not fatal, and can be tested with a small pilot run. Waiting or revising would forfeit first-mover advantage in a price-sensitive niche.
