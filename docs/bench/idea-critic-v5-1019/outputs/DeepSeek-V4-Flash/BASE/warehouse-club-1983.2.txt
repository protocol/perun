The proposal bets on a membership-fee model to drive profit while undercutting a larger rival on price. The risks are real but not fatal. The fee creates loyalty and upfront cash flow, which offsets thin margins. The competitor’s size is a weakness: it has higher overhead and less flexibility to match aggressive pricing. The no-frills format keeps costs low, and bulk buying appeals to price-sensitive households and small businesses. The first location will test willingness to pay, but the fee also filters out casual shoppers, reducing per-customer service costs. Scaling fast is risky, but starting with a few warehouses in dense, low-income areas can build a base before expanding. The earlier competitor may have more sites, but it likely carries a broader assortment and higher prices—leaving a clear niche for a leaner operator. The concern about large sites is manageable via industrial zones and long-term leases. Thin margins demand discipline, but the membership revenue provides a buffer that the rival lacks. Customers who buy in bulk already exist; the fee is a hurdle, but the savings will justify it if prices are consistently 10–20% lower. The plan is not without execution risk, but the core logic is sound: monetize the shopping trip itself, not just the goods. The market is big enough for two players, and the first mover advantage of the rival is offset by their cost structure. Proceed now, but start with one or two additional locations, not a rapid rollout, and watch early renewal rates. The model has a clear path to profitability if the fee is priced right and the assortment is tight.
