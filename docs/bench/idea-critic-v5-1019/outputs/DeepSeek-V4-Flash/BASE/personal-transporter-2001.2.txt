The proposal’s core value is replacing short car trips, but the listed risks are fatal at this stage. At $5,000, it undercuts a car but vastly overprices a bicycle or scooter for the same utilitarian need. The learning curve for balance creates a barrier for casual adoption, not a trivial one—it demands practice and falls. Legal ambiguity on sidewalks and roads means deployment could be blocked in major cities, making “sell widely” unexecutable. Zero cargo or passenger capacity further narrows its use case to solo, light errands—exactly where walking or a cheap bike already wins. The inventor’s technology may be sound, but the market timing is wrong: infrastructure, regulation, and consumer familiarity are absent. Launching now would burn capital on a niche gadget, not a transport revolution. The better path is to pilot with fleet operators (e.g., campus or tourism rentals) to build usage data, refine safety, and lobby for rules—then sell to consumers once price drops and legality is clear. Approving as proposed risks a high-profile failure that poisons future attempts. Hold for revision: target a commercial pilot first, lower price later, and solve regulatory ambiguity before mass consumer sales. The proposal’s vision is right, but its execution plan is premature.
