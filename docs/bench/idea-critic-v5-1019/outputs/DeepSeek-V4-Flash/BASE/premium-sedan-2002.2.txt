The proposal hinges on a fundamental brand-equity gap. The carmaker’s strength is scale and affordability, not prestige. Launching a very large, expensive sedan under its own name directly confronts the core driver of luxury purchases: exclusivity and status signaling. The risks listed are not minor—they are structural. Premium buyers are not comparing specifications; they are buying a social identity. A mass-market badge, no matter how excellent the engineering, fails that test.

The dedicated factory compounds the risk. At low initial volume, fixed costs will be crushing, likely forcing either a high price that further deters buyers or a lower price that erodes margin and brand positioning. The advanced suspension and top-quality materials are table stakes in this segment, not differentiators. The established rivals have decades of heritage, service networks, and a loyal, wealthy clientele. Entering with a single model, no matter how good, is a frontal assault with insufficient ammunition.

The proposal also ignores the timing context (2002) and the alternative of acquiring or partnering with an existing premium brand, which would grant instant credibility. Building from zero luxury reputation is a multi-decade, capital-intensive gamble with a high probability of failure, as the buyers’ perception is the product. The risks raised are not objections to manage; they are validations of a flawed strategy. The company should leverage its mass-market strength to dominate its current segment, not dilute its identity in a quixotic pursuit of a market it cannot authentically own. The plan as proposed is a costly distraction with poor odds.

DECISION: NO-GO
