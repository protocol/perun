The proposal hinges on a volume-versus-margin bet. Currently, a small, high-fee base limits losses but also limits data scale and negotiating power. Cutting the fee to ~$10 for daily access will attract heavy users immediately, and each visit costs the company the full ticket price—likely $10–$15. Thus, any member seeing more than one film per month is a net drain. The projected offset from data sales and studio/theater deals is speculative; no contracts or pricing models are cited. Theaters refusing to cooperate would cap supply and push demand to fewer screens, worsening per-ticket economics. The new owner’s cash can fund the subsidy, but there is no exit path: if data revenue fails to materialize, the cash burn scales with membership, not with profit. The only way to break even is to renegotiate ticket prices or earn per-member revenue above the average ticket cost—neither is guaranteed. The risk is not that the model fails to attract users; it will. The risk is that every new user increases the deficit, and the unproven streams are the sole basis for future profitability. Without evidence of theater willingness to discount, studio data-sharing agreements, or a cap on usage, the proposal is a cash-drain with a hope, not a plan. A smaller pilot with usage limits or a tiered price tied to actual average cost would be prudent, but the proposal as written offers no such guardrails. The executive team should not approve a model that structurally loses more money as it grows, based on revenue sources that do not yet exist. The facts show no proof of demand for the data, no theater partnerships, and no cost-control mechanism. That is a bet on luck, not strategy.  
DECISION: NO-GO
