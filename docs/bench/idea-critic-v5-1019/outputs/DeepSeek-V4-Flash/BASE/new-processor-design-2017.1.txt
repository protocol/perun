The proposal bets on a core-count and price advantage to offset a weak prior architecture. The market context favors disruption: the rival is dominant, but desktop demand is mature, and software parallelism is steadily improving, though not universal. The company’s scale is a genuine constraint—its last all-new design failed, and financial reserves are thin. A second miss would be existential.

However, the proposal’s specific terms are not reckless. Many cores at lower prices is a classic challenger play that can force the rival to respond, but the rival’s response (price cuts or faster chips) is likely and would compress margins further. The software risk is real but not fatal: even if some workloads don’t scale, multi-core helps multitasking and content creation, which are growing desktop segments.

The key question is timing and execution. The proposal is for 2017, and the rival’s next-gen is unknown. If the new architecture is genuinely all-new (not a tweak), it carries high engineering risk, but the company has no choice but to leapfrog—incremental improvements won’t close the gap. The financial “no room for a second miss” is the strongest counterargument, but it also means the company cannot afford to stay passive.

Given the facts, the proposal is coherent: it targets a clear weakness (price/cores) and leverages a fresh start. The risks are known and mitigated by aggressive pricing and a distinct value proposition. Waiting for perfect software support or a weaker rival would cede the market entirely. The company’s small size allows agility, and a bold, well-priced product is its best shot.
