The core product is novel and addresses a real urban gap: short trips that are too far to walk, too inefficient for a car. The inventor has a working prototype, which is a significant de-risking step.  

However, the proposal fails on multiple immediate fronts. The $5,000 price point is not just above a bicycle—it is roughly the cost of a reliable used car, which offers weather protection, cargo capacity, and passenger seating. The learning curve for balance is a real adoption barrier, not a minor inconvenience; it limits the target market to early adopters with patience and disposable income.  

Regulatory uncertainty is the fatal flaw. Without sidewalk or road legality, the device cannot be used legally in most cities, making "sell it widely" a fantasy. The lack of cargo or second-person capacity further narrows its utility to a single, solo commuter with no errands—a tiny niche.  

The timing (2001) also matters: urban infrastructure, bike lanes, and shared mobility norms were not yet developed, and the public had no reference point for such a device. The proposal offers no plan for regulatory engagement, no pilot city, no leasing or fleet model to offset price, and no safety certification. It is a great invention, but a poor market launch as proposed.  

The right move is to refine the go-to-market: start with a limited, controlled deployment (e.g., corporate campuses or gated communities), work with local regulators, and consider a rental or subscription model to lower upfront cost. Approving a mass consumer launch now would burn capital and damage the brand before the ecosystem exists.  

DECISION: NO-GO
