The proposal bets on a novel, time-limited disc model. The core economics are attractive: low disc cost, high-margin repeat fees, and a differentiated experience. However, the execution hinges on three fragile pillars. First, studio and manufacturer alignment for a new format is a multi-year, high-stakes negotiation; without their support, content and hardware availability collapse. Second, the player’s higher price and phone-line requirement create a steep adoption barrier, especially when standard players and discs are cheaper and simpler. Third, the 48-hour viewing window with repeat fees directly conflicts with established consumer expectations of ownership and unlimited playback—this is a behavioral shift, not a minor feature.

The partners are not named, and no commitment from studios or hardware makers is cited. The retailer’s own stores provide distribution, but that alone cannot overcome the format war risk or the value proposition gap. The “dislike paying for repeated viewing” is not a minor annoyance; it is the core monetization mechanism, and the proposal offers no mitigation (e.g., free first extension, rental-only option, or bundling with hardware). The phone-line requirement also assumes broadband or landline ubiquity that may not exist in 1998, creating technical failure points.

Given the lack of confirmed partner agreements, the premium hardware cost with no clear subsidy, and a revenue model that penalizes the customer’s most natural behavior (rewatching), the proposal is not ready for immediate launch. It needs revised pricing, partner commitments, and a clearer path to consumer acceptance. The timing is early, but the structural flaws are not timing issues—they are design issues.

DECISION: NO-GO
