The proposal weighs a low technical failure rate against a high public-relations risk. The error, while rare, is real and affects a core function—floating-point division—in a product marketed for precision. The plan to require proof of impact is problematic: most users cannot practically demonstrate their specific workloads trigger the flaw, so this condition would either frustrate legitimate claimants or invite distrust.  

The timing is critical. The problem is already widely discussed, meaning the company does not control the narrative. Refusing to offer replacements to all who ask, while publicly calling the flaw “not serious,” will likely be perceived as minimization, not engineering judgment. The cost of a full replacement is large, but the cost of a damaged reputation—especially for a chip maker whose brand depends on reliability—could be far larger in lost future sales and trust.  

A partial remedy (only for proven affected users) may seem fiscally prudent, but it fails operationally (proof is impractical) and ethically (it shifts burden to the customer for a manufacturer’s defect). The better path is to acknowledge the flaw openly, offer replacement to all who request it, and accept the short-term financial hit to preserve long-term credibility. The proposal as written, with its restrictive condition, is likely to backfire.  

Therefore, the current proposal should not be approved as is. It requires a more generous and transparent approach before implementation.  

DECISION: NO-GO
