The proposal targets a real revenue loss, but the risks are not hypothetical—they are predictable and severe. In 2005, the market is already sensitive to malware-like behavior; auto-installing software without clear consent will trigger consumer backlash, negative press, and potential class-action lawsuits. Hard-to-remove software will be labeled as spyware, damaging the label’s brand and trust. Security researchers will almost certainly find flaws, leading to public exploits that undermine the protection entirely and invite regulatory scrutiny. Customers who legally bought discs will feel punished, driving them to alternatives—including piracy—which is the opposite of the goal. The technology itself is also brittle: it only works on the specific playback environment, and any workaround (e.g., using a different player) voids the protection. The company’s core asset is its catalog and artist relationships; this move risks those for a short-term, unproven gain. The proposal lacks a clear consent mechanism, removal path, and incident response plan. It treats a systemic market shift as a technical problem, when the real issue is business model adaptation (e.g., digital distribution, licensing). Approving now would launch a product likely to fail publicly, harming sales and reputation. A better path is to pilot a voluntary, transparent system with opt-in, or focus on legal and educational approaches. The executive team should not greenlight a high-risk, low-trust scheme that invites regulatory and consumer hostility.  
DECISION: NO-GO
