The proposal targets a real problem: declining sales from computer-based copying. However, the execution is flawed for 2005. The core issue is that the protection mechanism relies on installing software on the buyer’s machine without robust, informed consent. This creates two immediate, compounding risks.

First, the negative customer reaction is not speculative—it is predictable. Treating paying customers as potential criminals, especially with hidden or hard-to-remove software, will generate public backlash, bad press, and likely boycotts. This directly harms the very sales the proposal aims to protect.

Second, the security risk is severe. Any software that installs itself and is difficult to remove becomes a prime target for malware authors and security researchers. Once a vulnerability is found—and it will be—the company faces a massive public relations and legal liability crisis, far exceeding the revenue saved from reduced copying.

The proposal also fails to account for the technical landscape: many users will simply avoid playing these discs on computers, or share the discs with those who have non-vulnerable machines. The protection is partial at best, but the downside is total—brand damage, trust erosion, and potential lawsuits.

A better approach would be to offer value-added digital content or flexible licensing that makes copying less attractive, rather than punishing the user. The current proposal is a high-risk, low-reward gamble that prioritizes short-term control over long-term customer trust and security.

DECISION: NO-GO
