The proposal hinges on a dramatic leap in scale, pricing, and production capability, all within a single year. The company’s track record is minimal—two low-volume, high-cost vehicles—which provides no evidence of supply-chain management, assembly-line efficiency, or cost control at the required level. The battery supply scale-up is a critical dependency with no stated secured contracts or capacity. Refundable deposits are a useful demand signal but not revenue; they can be reversed if production slips, which is likely given the inexperience. The low margin leaves no buffer for the inevitable cost overruns, quality issues, or production delays that plague any new high-volume launch, especially for a company that has never done it. The timeline is unrealistic: unveiling, taking deposits, and starting production in the next year at far higher volumes than ever built is a recipe for missing targets, burning cash, and damaging brand credibility. While the ambition is bold, the execution risk is extreme, and the financial buffer is thin. The executive team should not commit resources to a plan that depends on unproven capabilities and optimistic assumptions. The correct action is to reject this as proposed, as it requires fundamental revision—either a slower production ramp, a secured battery supply, or a higher price point to protect margins. Approving now would be irresponsible.  

DECISION: NO-GO
