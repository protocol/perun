The proposal asks for a massive, irreversible commitment based on unverified claims. The core asset—the blood-testing device—has no published, peer-reviewed evidence. The company refuses to disclose its methods, meaning the chain cannot independently validate accuracy, reliability, or safety. This is not a minor due-diligence gap; it is a fundamental absence of proof for a medical device that will guide patient decisions.

The chain’s lack of medical expertise compounds the risk. Even if the technology worked, the chain would be responsible for clinical oversight, handling adverse events, and interpreting results—none of which it is equipped to do. The exclusivity clause locks the chain into a single, unproven partner, eliminating any fallback if the device fails or regulators intervene.

Regulatory risk is not speculative. Health authorities routinely require transparency and clinical validation for diagnostic tools. A device that withholds its methodology is likely to face rejection, forcing the chain to shutter hundreds of clinics and absorb a sunk cost of $350 million, plus reputational damage from offering unreliable tests.

The proposal’s appeal lies in speed and cost, but those advantages are meaningless without accuracy. There is no evidence the founder can deliver on the promise, and the structure of the deal—secrecy, exclusivity, massive upfront spend—protects the vendor, not the chain or its customers. The responsible path is to decline now, or require published studies and method disclosure before any further discussion. Given the scale, the unknown unknowns are disqualifying.  

DECISION: NO-GO
