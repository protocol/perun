The core conflict is brand equity versus product substance. The proposal bets that engineering and materials can overcome a nameplate associated with economy and volume. However, the premium segment is defined by exclusivity, heritage, and perceived status—not just specifications. A dedicated factory at low volume creates high fixed costs, while pricing at parity with established rivals invites direct comparison where the brand will lose on intangibles. The large engine and advanced suspension are table stakes, not differentiators. The target buyer is not seeking value; they are seeking identity. A mass-market badge on a very large sedan signals a discount or a mistake, not a desired purchase. The risk of cannibalizing the core brand’s image is also real: if the sedan fails, it drags down the mass-market perception of quality; if it succeeds, it may confuse the brand’s positioning. The 2002 context—pre-digital, badge-heavy, status-driven—makes this even more perilous. There is no evidence the company has a plan to build a luxury sub-brand, change dealer experience, or create a separate service culture. The proposal is a product plan, not a brand strategy. It will likely achieve low volume with high losses, and the residual value of the sedan will be poor, further damaging resale perception. The only path would be a separate brand and a decade of investment, but that is not what is proposed. Therefore, the current plan should not proceed as given. The executive team should reject this specific factory-and-launch approach, even if the long-term ambition is valid. The facts presented do not support a successful entry.  

DECISION: NO-GO
