The proposal asks the company to leap from niche, low-volume production to mass manufacturing within a year, despite zero proven scale capability. The deposit model creates a false signal of demand—refundable money is not revenue, and cancellations are likely once delivery delays or quality issues surface. The low margin leaves no buffer for the inevitable cost overruns in tooling, battery procurement, and rework. Battery supply scaling is not a matter of will; it requires secured contracts, logistics, and validated cell quality, none of which are evidenced. The company’s engineering and production teams have never managed a supply chain of this complexity, nor the quality control needed for a cheaper vehicle where defects are less tolerable. Unveiling now would overpromise to a public that will judge the brand on delivery, not hype. A cheaper sedan could dilute the premium positioning without the operational capacity to back it. The risks are not merely financial—they are existential. Production delays, cash burn, and refund requests could sink the firm. The only rational path is to first pilot a medium-volume vehicle, prove the supply chain, and then scale. But the proposal demands immediate commitment to a volume tenfold higher than any prior output, with no pilot phase. That is reckless. Approving it now would prioritize marketing over manufacturing reality, and the consequences would be severe. The correct action is to reject this specific timeline and scope, forcing a more measured plan.  

DECISION: NO-GO
