The core tension is brand equity versus market expansion. The enthusiast base is the company’s lifeblood, and a high-priced, heavy SUV directly contradicts the agility and purity they prize. The risk of alienating them is not speculative—it is the primary revenue source. The co-development with a larger group reduces engineering risk but not brand perception risk. Established luxury SUV competitors already own the credibility for "powerful and off-road" in that segment; entering late with a badge that says "sports car" invites skepticism, not conquest sales.

The high first-year cost is a given, but the deeper problem is strategic fit. A small range means every model must reinforce the core identity. An SUV, even a good one, dilutes that identity in the minds of both loyalists and potential buyers who will question what the brand stands for. The "betrayal" narrative is powerful and hard to reverse. While the financial upside might be tempting, the downside is a permanent weakening of the brand’s reason for being. The proposal offers no mitigation for brand damage, only product specs. That is insufficient.

The timing (2002) also matters—luxury SUVs are mature, not emergent. The market does not need another me-too entry from a brand with no truck heritage. The better path is to deepen the sports-car line or develop a lighter, faster coupe-SUV that retains driving purity, but that is not this proposal. As written, the costs, competitive headwinds, and brand erosion outweigh the speculative profit. Approving now would be a bet against the company’s core asset.

DECISION: NO-GO
