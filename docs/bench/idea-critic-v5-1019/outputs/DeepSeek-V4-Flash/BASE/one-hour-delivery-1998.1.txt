The proposal’s core economics are unsound. Free delivery with no minimum order means every small transaction loses money on fulfillment alone, before product cost. Direct employment of delivery staff in multiple cities creates a fixed-cost burden that scales linearly with volume, with no path to profitability unless order density becomes extreme—unlikely when customers are incentivized to use the service only for tiny, low-margin purchases. The 1998 context is critical: no smartphone GPS, no real-time routing, no gig-economy flexibility. Managing a large, city-based fleet of staff with vehicles in an era of paper maps and pagers will be operationally chaotic and costly. Expansion to more cities before solving unit economics multiplies losses. The risk that demand is purely subsidy-driven is high—once prices rise or delivery fees appear, usage will collapse. There is no evidence of a moat: competitors can copy the model, but the model itself cannot sustain margins. The team is young, with no stated operational or logistics experience, and the plan relies on venture funding to burn through cash without a clear break-even trigger. The only defensible path would be to test in one city with a minimum order, paid delivery, and a narrow product set—but that is not what is proposed. The proposal as written is a classic high-burn, low-margin, scale-too-fast trap. Approving it now would waste capital and management time.  
DECISION: NO-GO
