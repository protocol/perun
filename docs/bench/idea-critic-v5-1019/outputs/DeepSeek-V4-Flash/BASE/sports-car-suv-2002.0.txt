The proposal asks a niche sports-car brand to enter a crowded, capital-intensive luxury SUV segment in 2002, using a shared platform from a larger group. The core tension is brand dilution versus revenue diversification.  

Loyal enthusiasts are the brand’s foundation. Introducing a heavy, high-riding SUV under the same badge risks alienating this core, as the emotional purchase driver is precision and lightness, not utility. The proposed high price and powerful engine may attract new buyers, but the off-road claim conflicts with the brand’s on-road purity. Established rivals already own the “luxury SUV” mental space, with deeper dealer networks and proven reliability—a single co-developed model cannot easily unseat them.  

The first-year cost is high, and shared platforms often reduce differentiation, making the vehicle feel like a rebadged group product rather than a true sports-car expression. If the SUV fails to meet sales targets, it could drain resources from the core sports-car line, which is the brand’s lifeblood. The risk is not just financial but existential: a misstep here could permanently tarnish the marque’s identity, and loyal customers may not return.  

While diversification is tempting, the timing (2002, pre-SUV boom in this segment) and the brand’s small scale make this a leap too far. A better path would be a limited, halo SUV with a different sub-brand, or a partnership that keeps the sports-car name off the vehicle. As proposed, the risk/reward is skewed against the brand’s core equity.  

DECISION: NO-GO
