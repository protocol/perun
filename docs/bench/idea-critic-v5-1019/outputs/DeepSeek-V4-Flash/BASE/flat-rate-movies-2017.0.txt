The proposal hinges on a deliberate trade: subsidize heavy usage now to buy scale, then monetize that scale later. The core math is unfavorable at the outset—every ticket costs full price, while the monthly fee is capped. Heavy users will indeed be loss leaders. The unproven data and studio/theater deals are speculative, not contractual. Theaters have no incentive to share margin, and could block access, strangling the value proposition.

However, the new owner’s cash removes the immediate solvency constraint. Rapid membership growth at $10/month can generate a large, engaged user base—a tangible asset that studios and theaters may eventually pay to reach, especially if the service drives incremental attendance during off-peak hours. The risk of theater refusal is real, but the proposal can pivot: if chains refuse, the service can negotiate directly with independent cinemas or offer differential pricing to members. The heavy-user cost is a known, bounded loss, not an unknown one. The unproven revenue streams are a bet, but the cash buffer funds the experiment.

The question is not whether the model is profitable today—it is not—but whether the owner can tolerate the burn rate long enough to convert scale into leverage. The proposal’s success depends on execution, not on the absence of risk. Given the explicit cash subsidization and the clear path to negotiated deals once membership is large, the risk is acceptable as a deliberate strategic investment. The alternative—waiting for proven revenue—would forfeit the first-mover advantage. The proposal is internally consistent and funded. Approve now.
