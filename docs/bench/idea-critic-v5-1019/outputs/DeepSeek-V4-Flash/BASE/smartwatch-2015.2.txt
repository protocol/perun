The proposal asks to enter a market with proven modest demand, not a clear failure. The maker’s existing phone ecosystem provides a unique integration advantage—no other watch pairs as seamlessly with its own hardware. The multi-material, multi-price strategy hedges against the “no need” risk by targeting both luxury and fitness segments. The one-day battery is a real drawback, but early adopters accepted similar limits in phones; the app store creates a sticky ecosystem that competitors lack. The “second screen” objection assumes buyers are satisfied with pulling out a phone, yet health tracking on the wrist is a distinct, growing use case. Risks are real but not disqualifying: poor sales of others’ watches reflect their weak software and clunky design, not a saturated category. The maker’s brand loyalty and retail reach can convert a modest market into a strong niche, and a 2015 launch is early enough to define the category before rivals improve. The watch’s dependence on the phone is a feature for existing users, not a barrier. Battery life can improve in later versions; first-gen buyers accept trade-offs for novelty and integration. The proposal does not overreach—it offers several price points, so downside is limited if premium versions lag. The executive team should approve now, as waiting would cede mindshare and app developer attention to competitors. The risks are manageable through iterative software updates and marketing focused on health outcomes, not “second screen” messaging. No fatal flaw exists in the business case, and the maker’s strengths directly counter each raised risk. Approve as proposed, without delay.
