The proposal targets a genuine urban gap: short trips where cars are overkill and walking is slow. The inventor’s device offers novelty and efficiency, but the 2001 context is decisive. At $5,000, it is a luxury toy, not a mass-market mobility solution—priced above most bicycles and used cars, with no cargo or passenger capacity. The learning curve and legal ambiguity on sidewalks and roads create adoption friction that a single launch cannot overcome. Cities have no infrastructure, rules, or social norms for this vehicle; early adopters would face tickets, collisions, or ridicule. The inventor’s prototype may be brilliant, but “sell it widely” ignores that distribution, service, insurance, and regulatory approval are absent. The price alone kills the “replace walking” premise—walking is free. Moreover, the target user (urban commuter) likely lacks storage, charging, and tolerance for a steep learning curve in traffic. The proposal is a product idea, not a business plan. It lacks a pilot city, a regulatory strategy, a financing model, or a clear path from novelty to necessity. Timing is also poor: the early 2000s saw no shared mobility or battery cost curves that would make this viable at scale. The right move is to refine the product, test in a controlled environment, partner with a city for legal carve-outs, and iterate on price and use cases (e.g., campus or warehouse logistics) before a consumer launch. Approving now would burn capital and credibility.  

DECISION: NO-GO
