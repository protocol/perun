The proposal hinges on a 1998 market where e-commerce is nascent, and the unit economics are deeply flawed. Free delivery on a $3 coffee or a $5 snack, with no minimum, guarantees a negative contribution margin on a large share of orders. Direct employment of delivery staff in each city creates fixed labor costs that do not scale with fluctuating demand, and vehicle costs add further capital intensity. The plan to rapidly expand cities multiplies these losses before any proven repeatable model exists.

The risk that customers only use the service when prices are subsidized is critical—it suggests zero pricing power and no loyalty beyond the subsidy. The founders’ youth and energy do not offset the absence of a path to profitability. The proposal lacks any mention of charging for delivery, setting a minimum order, or using courier partners to share variable costs. It also ignores the operational complexity of perishable goods (coffee, snacks) versus durable ones (videos, books) in a single, fast delivery network.

While the vision of convenience is attractive, the execution plan as written will burn through venture capital without reaching a sustainable unit economy. A smaller pilot in one city, with paid delivery or minimums, could test real demand, but that is not what is proposed. The current model guarantees losses on every transaction and every city added. Approving it now would be irresponsible.

DECISION: NO-GO
