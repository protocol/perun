The proposal asks the executive team to approve a minority stake in a 16-unit, higher-priced, made-to-order burrito chain, with an option to increase investment later, while allowing independent operation. The core question is not whether the concept is good, but whether this specific structure fits the company’s strategic and operational reality in 1998.

Key facts: The chain is tiny, its food is slower and requires more skilled labor, and its price point is above the company’s core menu. The risks are real: scaling a made-to-order model is notoriously difficult, and the operational DNA is fundamentally different from fast-food assembly. Management attention is a finite resource, and a minority stake with no control creates a situation where the company absorbs financial risk without the ability to fix operational issues. The “right to invest more later” is attractive, but it’s an option, not a current obligation.

However, the independent operation mitigates management distraction, and the higher price point targets a different customer segment, potentially offering a strategic learning opportunity about premium formats. The tiny scale means the downside is limited. The option to increase investment later provides a cheap way to test a new market without full commitment. The burrito chain’s success would not depend on the giant’s core competencies, but on its own leadership, which the proposal respects.

The decisive factor is the low absolute cost of a minority stake in 16 units. This is a small, controlled experiment. If it fails, the loss is modest. If it succeeds, the option to invest more is valuable. The risks raised are real but are exactly why the stake is minority and the chain runs independently. Holding or revising would lose the first-mover advantage in a potentially growing segment. The proposal is prudent, limited, and reversible. Approve as proposed.

DECISION: GO
