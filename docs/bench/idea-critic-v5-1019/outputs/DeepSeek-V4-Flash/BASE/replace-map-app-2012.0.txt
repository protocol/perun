The proposal trades immediate user trust for long-term strategic control. In 2012, the rival’s mapping app is the market standard for reliability and data completeness. The new app, built from third-party data, lacks equivalent coverage and accuracy. Replacing a working default with an inferior product risks user backlash, negative press, and erosion of the phone’s core utility—navigation is a daily, essential function.

The benefits—turn-by-turn, 3D, voice—are already present in the rival’s app, so differentiation is minimal. The real gain is data ownership and ecosystem leverage, but that payoff is speculative and years away. The immediate cost is concrete: users lose a familiar, functional tool; businesses may be mislisted; and the rival becomes a competitor rather than a partner. There is no fallback plan if the new maps fail performance reviews during the fall launch window.

The proposal does not address a staged rollout, opt-in for existing users, or a hybrid approach (keeping the rival’s app available as a manual download while defaulting to the new one). A forced swap without user choice converts a convenience into a liability. The executive team should prioritize user experience over territorial control, especially when the alternative is a known, working product.

Given the data gaps, error risks, and likely user anger, the proposed immediate replacement is not acceptable as written. The idea has merit, but it needs a softer transition, improved data validation, and a clear rollback mechanism before going live.

DECISION: NO-GO
