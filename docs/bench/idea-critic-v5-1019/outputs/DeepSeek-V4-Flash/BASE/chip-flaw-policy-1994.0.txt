The proposal hinges on a probabilistic risk assessment versus public perception. The technical claim—that the error is astronomically rare in typical use—is plausible, but the operational flaw is the “show that your work is affected” condition. That requirement is impractical: most users cannot diagnose a floating-point edge case, and asking them to do so shifts burden onto customers, breeding distrust. The risk of widespread discussion is real, as 1994 media and online forums amplify technical stories quickly. A partial replacement policy invites accusations of a cover-up, especially if any high-profile failure emerges (e.g., in scientific or financial computation). The cost of full replacement is large, but the cost of reputation damage and potential class-action or regulatory scrutiny could exceed it. However, the proposal does not address a proactive communication strategy—it only states the flaw is “not serious” without offering transparent testing tools, a clear explanation of the error’s conditions, or a simple return path for any concerned user. Without those, the “show affected” bar will be seen as a stalling tactic. The company should instead offer a free replacement to any user who requests it, while simultaneously publishing a technical whitepaper and a diagnostic utility. That would cost more upfront but preserve trust and avoid the appearance of hiding a defect. The current proposal, as written, is too defensive and impractical to execute fairly. It should be revised to a full-replacement offer with transparent disclosure, or held until such a plan is ready. Given the information provided, approving as-is would likely worsen the reputational risk.  
DECISION: NO-GO
