The proposal targets a real revenue loss, but the execution risks are severe. In 2005, consumer trust in digital media is fragile, and the described software—silent installation, difficult removal—directly invites backlash. The security-researcher risk is not hypothetical; any widely deployed protection will be probed, and a public exploit would trigger a PR disaster. Customers already resent DRM; framing them as thieves by default will accelerate piracy, not reduce it. The label would own the harm: damaged brand loyalty, potential class-action suits, and regulatory scrutiny. The proposal offers no mitigation plan for consent, uninstall, or vulnerability response. There is no evidence the copy-protection method is robust enough to justify the intrusion. The upside (reduced copying) is speculative, while the downside (customer revolt, security breach) is immediate and concrete. The executive team should not approve a rollout that risks its entire consumer base for a partial, temporary technological fix. A better path would involve legal education, tiered pricing, or partnerships with hardware makers—but those are not in this proposal. As written, the costs outweigh the benefits.  
DECISION: NO-GO
