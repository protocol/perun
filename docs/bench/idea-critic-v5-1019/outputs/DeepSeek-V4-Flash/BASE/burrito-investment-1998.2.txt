The proposal offers a minority stake with an option to increase later, while preserving the chain’s independent operations. The core question is whether this fits the fast-food giant’s strategic and operational reality in 1998.

The risks are real: scaling from 16 units is unproven, the operational model (made-to-order, higher skill, slower service) is fundamentally different from the giant’s standardized, speed-driven system. The price point sits above the core menu, creating brand adjacency confusion. Management distraction is a legitimate concern, given the giant’s own scale and competitive pressures.

However, the deal structure is deliberately low-commitment: a minority stake, no operational control, and an option—not an obligation—to invest more later. This allows the giant to learn about a premium, handmade segment without betting the company. The chain’s independence means the giant’s culture and processes won’t be imposed, avoiding the classic failure of forcing a small, artisanal concept into a corporate mold. The higher price and slower service are not flaws if the chain targets a different customer—one willing to pay for quality and freshness.

The main risk is not the chain’s failure, but the opportunity cost of distraction. Yet, with a minority stake and hands-off mandate, the giant can monitor performance with minimal internal bandwidth. If the concept proves scalable, the option to increase investment becomes valuable; if not, the loss is limited to the initial stake. The proposal does not ask for operational integration or brand rebranding, so the core business remains untouched.

Given the low financial exposure, the strategic learning value, and the preserved optionality, the proposal is acceptable as presented.

DECISION: GO
