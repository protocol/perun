The core model bets on volume overcoming unit economics, but the math is brutal. A $3 coffee delivered free costs $8 in labor and vehicle wear. No margin on small orders means every delivery is a loss unless average basket size rises dramatically—which the proposal does not address. Direct employment of delivery staff in multiple cities creates fixed costs that scale linearly with losses, not revenue. The 1998 timing adds risk: no smartphones, no GPS routing for consumers, no real-time tracking—dispatch inefficiency alone could double delivery times. The “expand quickly” plan amplifies operational chaos before proving a single profitable city. The risk that customers only use the service when subsidized is fatal; once subsidies end, demand vanishes, and you’re left with idle staff and leased vehicles. There is no mention of partnerships with existing coffee shops or bookstores to share costs, no tiered pricing, no minimum basket threshold, no surge or peak-hour adjustments. The proposal is a classic growth-at-all-costs trap dressed as convenience. It might work in a dense, wealthy micro-market with premium pricing and tight logistics—but not as proposed. The executive team should reject this as-is; the founders need to test a single neighborhood, charge a nominal fee or set a $10 minimum, and use gig workers or courier services to avoid fixed employment costs. Without those changes, this burns capital with no path to break-even.  

DECISION: NO-GO
