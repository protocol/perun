The proposal underestimates the public perception risk. In 1994, with the internet already amplifying technical debates, a “not serious” stance plus a narrow replacement policy will read as evasion. Users cannot “prove” impact without deep expertise, so the offer is effectively hollow—fueling outrage and media scrutiny. The cost of full replacement is large, but the cost of a damaged reputation, lost enterprise trust, and potential lawsuits is larger and longer-term. A partial fix invites endless qualification disputes, while a full recall, though expensive, is a one-time, decisive action that demonstrates accountability. The flaw’s rarity does not matter once perception shifts to “they hid it.” The company’s own analysis is credible internally, but it cannot be communicated convincingly to a skeptical public. Offering chips only to those who can show impact will be seen as a trap, not a solution. The wiser path is to replace all chips promptly, absorb the cost, and frame it as a commitment to quality—turning a crisis into a trust-building moment. Any delay or conditionality will prolong the story and deepen suspicion. The proposal as written is strategically weak: it prioritizes short-term savings over long-term brand equity, and its risk assessment ignores the asymmetric downside of appearing dishonest. Approving it now would likely trigger a backlash that makes a full recall inevitable later, but with added reputational damage. Therefore, do not proceed as proposed.
