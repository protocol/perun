The proposal bets on technical superiority and a head start, but the market dynamics are stacked against it. A one-hour capacity is a critical functional limit for movies and sports, which are the core drivers of home video demand. Consumers will not accept a format that cannot capture a full feature film.

The rival’s strategy of open licensing to many makers will flood the market with cheaper, widely available players. More players mean more consumer adoption, which in turn attracts more content producers to release titles on that format. This creates a self-reinforcing cycle: more hardware, more software, more users. Proprietary control with only a few partners cannot match this network effect.

Even with superior picture quality, the format war is won on content breadth and playback time, not just clarity. The early lead is real but temporary—the rival is already preparing a competing launch. Without broad industry support, pre-recorded titles will remain scarce, and retailers will favor the format with higher sales volume. The risk of becoming a niche product, like a Betamax in a VHS world, is not speculative; it is the most likely outcome.

Licensing to a few partners does not solve the capacity or title availability problem. It only delays the inevitable. The proposal should be rejected because it prioritizes control over market survival. The only viable path would be to either increase tape length to at least two hours or open the format aggressively, but the proposal does neither. As written, it will lose the standard war.

DECISION: NO-GO
