The proposal hinges on a high-risk bet: subsidize a per-ticket cost that exceeds the monthly fee, betting that rapid membership growth will unlock unproven data and studio/theater deals before cash runs out. The current model—small membership, higher price—already proves the unit economics are negative. Cutting the price tenfold worsens every ticket’s loss, and heavy users (one film/day) guarantee severe adverse selection. Theaters paying full price means no discount leverage, and their refusal to cooperate kills the core product. The new revenue streams are speculative, with no evidence of demand or contract terms. The owner’s cash provides a runway, but no timeline or milestone is given for when subsidies end. Without data on churn, acquisition cost, or theater willingness, the plan is a blind cash burn. The only real asset is the existing small member base, which could be tested for willingness to accept a lower price with reduced perks—but that is not what is proposed. The proposal as written asks for approval to scale a proven loss-making model before validating any path to profitability. The risks are not mitigations; they are the business model’s core flaws. No plan addresses how to renegotiate theater terms or prove data value before cash depletes. Therefore, the proposal cannot be approved as is—it requires a revised plan with a pilot, unit cost caps, and theater agreements in hand.

DECISION: NO-GO
