The proposal leverages a proven membership-warehouse model, but the timing and competitive position are weak. The larger competitor already has more locations, established supplier relationships, and deeper cash reserves. Entering in 1983 against that incumbent means the new retailer starts with a scale disadvantage, higher relative fixed costs, and no brand recognition to drive membership sign-ups.

The core risk—that shoppers may not pay a fee to shop at an unknown warehouse—is substantial. The incumbent can undercut prices during a price war, given its cash buffer, while the new entrant’s thin margins offer no cushion. Bulk-only purchasing also limits the customer base to households or small businesses with storage space and upfront cash, which is a narrower segment than the competitor already serves.

Without capital or a unique advantage (location, product mix, or operational efficiency beyond what is standard), the plan relies on hope rather than a defensible strategy. The earlier competitor’s head start in real estate and customer habits is a moat that cannot be crossed by simply opening more warehouses. The proposal does not address how to differentiate or survive the first two years of losses, which are inevitable given membership acquisition costs and low margins.

The idea is sound in isolation, but the execution timing and market entry are flawed. A better approach would be to acquire an existing smaller player or partner with a regional chain, but that is not in the proposal. As presented, the risk of failure is too high relative to the expected return. The executive team should not commit resources to this now.

DECISION: NO-GO
