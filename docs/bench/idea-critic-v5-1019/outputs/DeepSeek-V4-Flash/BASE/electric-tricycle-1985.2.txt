The proposal targets a niche in 1985 Britain: a low-cost, licence-free personal vehicle. The price point under £400 is accessible, and the appliance maker’s assembly capability suggests scale is feasible. Mail order bypasses dealer networks, which is clever for a novel product.  

However, the risks are not minor—they are fundamental. A 15 mph top speed and short range make it impractical for most commutes, not just a comfort issue. No weather protection in Britain’s climate severely narrows the usable season, undermining “large numbers.” Safety concerns about low visibility among traffic are legitimate and could trigger negative press or regulatory scrutiny, damaging the brand before launch.  

The January unveiling is poorly timed: cold, wet, dark months showcase the worst aspects. The optional extras model (e.g., weather protection) suggests the base product is incomplete, which may frustrate early adopters. The entrepreneur’s background is in home computers, not vehicles—this is a different manufacturing, safety, and liability landscape. The appliance maker’s role is assembly only, not design or distribution expertise.  

There is no evidence of market research, a pilot test, or a contingency plan for low sales. The proposal is bold but premature. A winter launch with an unproven, safety-questionable vehicle sold sight-unseen via mail order is a high-risk gamble with likely reputational damage if it fails. The core product needs refinement, better timing, and a clearer safety story before scaling.  

DECISION: NO-GO
