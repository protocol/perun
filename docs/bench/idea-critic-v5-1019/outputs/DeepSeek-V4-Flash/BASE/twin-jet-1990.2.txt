The proposal asks for a major financial commitment based on a single customer order. The aircraft’s key selling point—twin-engine overwater range—depends on regulatory approval that does not yet exist. If that approval is delayed or denied, the entire value proposition collapses, and the 34-aircraft order may be void or renegotiated.  

The design process with eight airlines sounds collaborative but introduces complexity: conflicting requirements, late changes, and schedule slips are likely, inflating an already very large development cost. The rival’s new models create a competitive race where being first matters, but being wrong is catastrophic.  

The single order is not a market validation; it is a pilot customer, not a diversified demand signal. History shows that wide-body programs need multiple anchor orders and proven technical readiness before launch. The cost is so large that a failure would threaten the company’s entire wide-body portfolio.  

The prudent path is to wait for at least a second major airline order, secure provisional regulatory approval, and lock down the final configuration with airlines before committing billions. Launching now, on one order and an unproven regulatory path, is a gamble with unacceptable downside.  

The proposal should be revised to include those preconditions, not approved as is.  

DECISION: NO-GO
