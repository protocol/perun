The proposal leverages a low-cost physical disc but couples it with a time-limited playback and a required phone connection for a special player. The core appeal is cheap, immediate access to movies without a purchase commitment, but the model hinges on a proprietary ecosystem.

Key execution barriers are severe. First, success requires simultaneous adoption by movie studios, consumer electronics manufacturers, and retailers—a coordination problem that historically takes years and massive marketing spend, especially against an entrenched standard. Second, the player’s higher price and phone-line requirement directly contradict consumer expectations of convenience and portability for home video. Third, the 48-hour window with a fee for extension creates a psychological penalty: customers pay repeatedly for the same content, which conflicts with the ownership mindset of buying a disc. While the disc cost is low, the total cost of entry (player + discs + extension fees) is likely higher than renting a tape or buying a standard disc, especially for repeat viewing.

The partners’ incentives are misaligned: studios fear cannibalizing sell-through revenue, and electronics makers fear a niche format that risks becoming a beta vs. VHS-style failure. Without a clear path to mass adoption, the venture would burn cash on unsold inventory and failed partnerships. The proposal offers no mitigation for the chicken-and-egg problem of content and hardware, nor a fallback if phone-line costs or consumer backlash emerge. Given the lack of any evidence of studio commitments or proven consumer demand, this is premature.

DECISION: NO-GO
