The proposal leverages a passionate brand to enter a high-margin, growing segment, but the timing and execution carry severe structural flaws. The loyal enthusiast base is the core asset; a large, heavy, high-priced SUV directly contradicts the brand’s lightweight, agile, driver-focused identity. While co-development reduces engineering risk and shares costs, the first-year financial burden remains significant, and the competitive field—established luxury SUVs with proven off-road credibility—is already crowded.

The off-road claim is particularly weak: the brand has zero credibility in that domain, and buyers in this segment prioritize comfort, tech, and badge prestige over genuine capability. The powerful engine may attract some, but it does not offset the perceived betrayal. The proposal’s success hinges on converting existing fans, yet most cannot afford a high-priced model, and new luxury SUV buyers are unlikely to switch from entrenched rivals based on a sports-car badge alone. Brand dilution is a real, quantifiable risk: a misstep here could permanently alienate the core customer, eroding future sports-car sales.

The executive team should not approve this as proposed. The concept is not inherently wrong—a lower-priced, smaller, more sporty crossover could work later—but this specific large, heavy, off-road-focused SUV at a high price is a strategic mismatch. It forces the brand into a fight it cannot win on its own terms, with a high chance of damaging the very loyalty that makes the company valuable. The proposal needs fundamental repositioning, a different target segment, and a clearer path to profitability before any commitment. Approving now would be a costly gamble on a weak premise.  

DECISION: NO-GO
