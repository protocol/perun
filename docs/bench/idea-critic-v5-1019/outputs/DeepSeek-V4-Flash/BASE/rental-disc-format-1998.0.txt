The proposal bets on a novel ownership model—cheap physical media with time-limited playback—rather than selling discs outright. In 1998, this clashes with entrenched consumer expectations: buyers own what they purchase. The 48-hour window and per-view fees will likely be perceived as a tax, especially since standard discs are already affordable and playable everywhere. The special player’s higher cost and phone-line requirement add friction, not value, for most households.

The format’s success hinges on two external parties—studios and competing hardware makers—adopting it. History shows such coalitions are slow and fragile, especially when the incumbent standard (DVD) is gaining momentum with lower cost and broader compatibility. The retailer’s core strength is shelf space and pricing, not pioneering proprietary hardware. Without network effects or a killer exclusive, early adopters will be niche, and studios will hedge, limiting content.

The only real upside is disc cost (a few dollars) reducing inventory risk, but that is offset by player cost and consumer resentment. The 48-hour model might appeal to renters who dislike return trips, yet they already have VHS and DVD rental via existing players. Asking them to buy a new, pricier machine for a marginal convenience is a hard sell.

Given the timing—just as DVD is standardizing—this proposal fights the market’s direction. It requires massive education and partnership alignment that a single retailer cannot drive alone. The risk of stranded inventory and negative brand association is high. There is no compelling urgency; waiting to see if the format gains third-party traction is wiser.

DECISION: NO-GO
