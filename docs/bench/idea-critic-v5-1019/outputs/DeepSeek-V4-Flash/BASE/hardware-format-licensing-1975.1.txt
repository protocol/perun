The proposal bets on technical superiority and an early lead, but the risks are decisive. A one-hour tape is a hard consumer limit—it cannot hold a typical movie or a full ballgame, which is a daily-use case, not a niche. The rival’s strategy of wide licensing will flood the market with cheaper players, driving adoption through price and availability. Fewer partners for your format means fewer retail shelves, fewer rental titles, and a self-reinforcing decline as content makers and consumers both favor the more common standard. Even if your picture quality is visibly better, most buyers choose based on library size and cost per hour, not a side-by-side comparison. The early lead is real but short—the rival can catch up in months, and once their tapes are in more homes, your proprietary edge becomes a liability. Holding the format close protects margins but sacrifices the network effect that wins formats. The proposal’s own risk list confirms the most likely outcome: you become the Betamax footnote. There is no path here to dominant standard without opening the format now, and the proposal explicitly rejects that. Approving as written guarantees a slow, expensive loss. The only rational move is to reject this version and force a rethink—either license widely immediately or abandon the format for a longer-playing alternative. No revision is possible within the proposal’s own constraints.  

DECISION: NO-GO
