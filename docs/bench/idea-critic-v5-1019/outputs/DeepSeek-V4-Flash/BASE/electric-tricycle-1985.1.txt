The proposal targets a niche in 1985 Britain: a low-cost, licence-free personal vehicle. The £400 price point undercuts cars and motorbikes, appealing to commuters and those unable to drive. The appliance maker’s assembly capacity solves manufacturing scale. Mail order avoids retail overhead and reaches nationwide buyers quickly.  

However, the product’s core limitations are severe for the UK market. A 15 mph top speed is dangerous on roads with faster traffic, and the low seating position increases collision risk—safety groups will likely campaign against it, damaging public trust. The short range and lack of weather protection make it impractical for most of the year, especially in winter rain and cold. These factors suggest demand will be seasonal and limited to mild, short urban trips—not the “large numbers” envisioned.  

The January launch is poorly timed: cold, wet weather will showcase its worst features, generating negative early reviews. Mail order magnifies this, as buyers cannot test the vehicle before purchase, leading to high returns and complaints. The optional extras model complicates the value proposition, as the base version may feel inadequate, pushing the true cost higher.  

The entrepreneur’s enthusiasm is clear, but the evidence points to a product that fails core usability and safety expectations for the target market. A launch now would burn capital and reputation. Better to delay, redesign for higher speed and basic weather protection, or reposition as a leisure vehicle for private grounds—not public roads. As proposed, the risk of failure outweighs the potential reward.  

DECISION: NO-GO
