The proposal targets a real behavioral shift—mobile viewing—but the economics and timing are misaligned. In 2018, free short-form content on YouTube, Instagram, and emerging TikTok already conditioned audiences to expect zero cost for sub-10-minute videos. Charging a monthly fee for the same format, even with higher production values, faces a steep psychological hurdle. The lack of TV compatibility at launch further fragments the value proposition, as most subscription video services succeed by being the default on the largest screen in the home. Mobile-only, at this stage, limits reach and retention.

The $1.75B raise is aggressive. Commissioning high-budget shows from known producers means significant upfront costs, and with only two countries at launch, the subscriber base is too narrow to recoup that spend quickly. The ad-supported tier complicates revenue—ads on short mobile content typically yield lower CPMs, and viewers are prone to churn if ads interrupt a tight narrative. Breakeven would require either massive global scale (not planned) or a price point that undercuts perceived value.

The founders’ credentials are strong, but the market evidence in 2018 suggests this is a solution searching for a problem. The risk of cannibalization by free platforms is high, and the capital burn before proving product-market fit is dangerous. A smaller, cheaper pilot in one market with TV compatibility and a clear differentiation from free content would be wiser. As proposed, the scale and constraints make failure likely, and the executive team should not commit this capital now.

DECISION: NO-GO
