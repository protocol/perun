The proposal offers a minority stake in a small, premium-priced burrito chain with an option to increase investment later. The core risks are real: scalability is unproven, operations require higher skill and slower service, and the price point sits above the giant’s core menu. Management distraction is also a legitimate concern for a large organization.

However, the structure mitigates the biggest downside: a minority stake with an option to invest more later means the giant limits initial capital at risk while preserving upside if the concept proves itself. The chain runs independently, so operational complexity does not fall on the giant’s existing systems. The higher price and made-to-order model target a different customer segment, not a direct replacement for fast food—this could be a strategic hedge, not a diversion, if managed with clear boundaries.

The year is 1998. Fast-casual dining is nascent, but consumer trends toward fresher, customizable food are visible. The chain’s small size is a weakness, but also a controlled experiment. The giant can learn from its performance without committing full resources. The right to invest more later is a valuable option—if it fails, losses are capped; if it succeeds, the giant can scale its involvement on better terms.

The risks raised are valid but not fatal. The key is discipline: no operational interference, clear financial targets, and a defined review period. The proposal as written allows for that. Approving now, with the independent operation and limited stake, is a prudent, reversible move.
