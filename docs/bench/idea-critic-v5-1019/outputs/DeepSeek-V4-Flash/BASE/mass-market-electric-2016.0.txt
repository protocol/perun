The proposal hinges on converting pre-orders into delivered vehicles at unprecedented scale within one year, with thin margins and a supply chain that has never been tested. The core facts are stark: the company has sold only two models in small numbers, meaning no proven manufacturing ramp, no established supplier network at required volume, and no track record of cost control. The cheaper sedan’s price point leaves minimal margin, so any production hiccup, battery shortage, or quality issue would erase profit entirely. Refundable deposits are not binding revenue; they are interest-free loans that can vanish if delays or spec changes disappoint buyers. The timeline is aggressive—unveiling in 2016 and producing at far higher volumes in 2017 assumes flawless execution in engineering, tooling, and logistics, which the company has never demonstrated. While the proposal could generate massive buzz and brand expansion, the operational risk is existential. A failure to deliver on time or at cost would not only burn through deposits but also destroy credibility with both customers and investors, potentially bankrupting the firm. The upside is speculative; the downside is concrete and severe. The executive team should not approve this as proposed. It requires a staged approach: first prove production at moderate volume, secure binding battery contracts, and test the supply chain before taking mass deposits. The current plan bets the company on an unproven leap. Therefore, the proposal is not viable in its present form.  
DECISION: NO-GO
