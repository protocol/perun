The proposal relies on a lean, direct-sales model in a market dominated by established brands with strong distribution and service networks. The risks are real: customer trust without a physical presence is uncertain, and the lack of a service network could deter buyers who fear post-purchase problems. However, the core advantage is price—selling below established brands by eliminating retail markup and reseller margins. This resonates with a segment of buyers who are technically savvy and willing to order by phone or mail, especially hobbyists and small businesses seeking cost efficiency. The direct-order approach also keeps inventory lean and cash flow tight, reducing the need for heavy credit. The credit risk on parts is a genuine operational hurdle, but it can be mitigated by starting small, paying suppliers promptly, and building a track record. The absence of stores is not fatal if the company offers a clear return policy and a simple, phone-based support line; early adopters in 1984 are often tolerant of such trade-offs for a better price. The established brands are slow and expensive; this model can undercut them significantly. The key is to grow gradually, reinvesting profits, and avoid overexpansion before demand is proven. There is no evidence of a fatal flaw. The risks are manageable with disciplined execution, and the pricing advantage provides a strong, defensible entry point. The proposal should proceed as outlined, with the understanding that growth will be conservative and customer feedback will guide any adjustments.
