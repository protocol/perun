DECISION: NO-GO

The proposal’s core bet—that proprietary control and superior picture quality will overcome an open, widely licensed rival—fails on its own stated facts. The rival’s strategy of licensing to many makers directly addresses the two biggest market drivers: player availability and price. More makers mean more retail presence, more competition on hardware cost, and a larger installed base, which attracts content producers. The proposal’s own risk list concedes this: fewer manufacturers likely means fewer pre-recorded titles, and a one-hour tape cannot capture a full film or sports event, a functional gap the rival’s longer tape fills. Early lead and picture quality are real but temporary advantages; they do not offset the network effects the rival is building. The standard will be set by whoever achieves the broadest ecosystem, not the best spec. Betting the company’s format on exclusivity when the competitor is already pursuing the winning play is a one-way door with no credible path to the upside. Approve now and you lock in a marginal product against a superior market strategy. The correct move is to revise: open the format, accelerate licensing, and extend tape length before launch. That is not approval as proposed.
