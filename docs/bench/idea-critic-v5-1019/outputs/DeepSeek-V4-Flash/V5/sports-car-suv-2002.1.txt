First call: GO. The brand’s loyalty and halo can transfer to a high-margin SUV if positioned as an extension of performance DNA, not a dilution.

Steelman: The crossover taps a proven, lucrative segment using shared costs, while the sports brand’s engineering credibility justifies a premium price and attracts new buyers without alienating core fans.

Attack: The core risk is brand betrayal. Enthusiasts buy the marque for purity; an SUV contradicts that. If they defect, the halo collapses and the new model loses its pricing power. But loyalists rarely leave over a new variant—they buy the sports cars they already love. The SUV expands the tent, and off-road ability plus a powerful engine aligns with the brand’s performance ethos. Cost is high but shared platform mitigates it. Heavy vehicle? Buyers expect mass in this class; the sports image survives because the sports cars remain unchanged. Established rivals are strong, but none own this brand’s specific enthusiast cachet.

Strongest attack survived: “Brand betrayal kills the core franchise.” It fails because no evidence shows existing customers stop buying the sports cars; the SUV is additive, not substitutive.

Kill criteria: first-year sales below internal forecast, or measurable enthusiast defection (e.g., loyalty metrics drop) within 12 months—then pivot marketing or trim scope. Leading metric: pre-orders from current owners vs. new buyers.

Risks: execution, market timing, brand stretch—all manageable and reversible via model positioning.

DECISION: GO
