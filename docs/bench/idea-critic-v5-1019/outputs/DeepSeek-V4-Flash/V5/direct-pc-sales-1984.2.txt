First call: GO. Direct sales, low overhead, and aggressive pricing can win a niche despite brand dominance.

Steelman: A lean, no-middleman assembler undercuts established brands on price, keeps full margin, and grows organically on reputation and word of mouth.

Assumptions: The student can secure parts reliably, manage cash flow without credit, and ship or deliver reliably. Buyers in the target segment (price-sensitive, technically confident) accept buying unseen.

Better ways: A small demo unit at a local fair or a money-back guarantee could reduce perceived risk without adding stores. But direct-order-only is viable as a first step.

Kill criteria: If orders stall below a survival threshold after the first three months, or if parts supply fails, pivot to a repair/upgrade service or exit.

Strongest attack survived: "No one buys a computer they can't touch." This fails for the target buyer: a price advantage of 20–30% over brands, combined with a young, tech-literate market in 1984, is enough for a meaningful segment to order by phone or mail. Established channels are slow and expensive, not a moat at this scale.

Remaining risk: Service and support expectations. Mitigate with a clear, limited warranty and a simple return policy, not a network.

Decision: Approve now. The business is small, reversible, and self-funding. Risks are manageable and can be tested with the first batch of orders. Waiting loses momentum and market timing.

DECISION: GO
