First call: NO-GO. The proposal’s core claim—that the flaw is not serious—is contradicted by its own risk that users cannot easily show they are affected. If affected users cannot prove it, the offer is hollow and the stated mitigation fails its stated purpose. The company’s own analysis (once in millennia) is an assumed average, not a guarantee for specific workloads; a user doing millions of divisions daily could hit it far sooner. The proposal’s evidence does not establish that no real harm occurs, only that it is rare on average.

The better way is to offer replacement chips to all who request one, without proof. This is cheaper than a full recall but avoids the reputational trap of appearing to hide a defect or demanding impossible proof. The cost of a broad but voluntary replacement is bounded; the cost of a scandal is not. The risk of “everyone asks” is a cost, not a blocker—it does not break the plan’s numbers, it just makes them larger.

The pre-mortem: the company denies the flaw’s seriousness, users cannot prove impact, press coverage grows, and the company looks evasive. That is a one-way door for reputation. Waiting a week to decide costs little; acting now with a restrictive policy risks a permanent stain.

The proposal’s own risk list already shows the kill criterion failing: if users cannot show they are affected, the offer is useless. That is not a may—it is stated as a risk the plan does not answer.

DECISION: NO-GO
