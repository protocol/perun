The proposal is a classic subsidize-to-scale bet: low price drives membership, future monetization via data and studio/theater deals offsets ticket costs. The core risk is arithmetic—at $10/month, a member seeing even two films costs the company ~$20+ in ticket reimbursements. The business model relies on unproven revenue streams and assumes theaters will accept a partner that undercuts their own economics.

Heavy users are a structural problem: the more a member uses the service, the deeper the loss per subscriber. There is no cap, no per-film margin, and no evidence that data or deals will materialize at scale. Theater cooperation is uncertain—chains may see this as cannibalizing full-price customers.

The owner has cash, which buys time, but time alone does not fix a unit-economics flaw. The proposal offers no pilot, no usage cap, no renegotiation trigger with theaters, and no data showing members will watch infrequently enough to make $10 viable. The upside depends on two speculative revenue streams and a partner ecosystem that has not agreed to anything.

The idea is bold but the plan as written burns cash on a structurally loss-making core with no demonstrated path to profitability. A cheaper alternative—smaller pilot, usage limits, or per-film revenue share—would test the thesis without betting the whole subsidy. The proposal does not beat do nothing on the facts given.

DECISION: NO-GO
