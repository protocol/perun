**First call:** NO-GO — the proposal’s core mechanism (unconsented, hard-to-remove software) creates a direct conflict with the stated customer base, whose cooperation is essential.

**Steelman:** The protection is invisible to the honest buyer, stops casual copying, and preserves revenue without raising prices.

**Attack:** The software installs on the buyer’s machine without clear consent and is hard to remove. This is not a risk; it is a stated feature. The buyer is the victim of the very product they paid for. The record company’s upside depends on those buyers continuing to purchase discs. The proposal’s own evidence—that customers may object to being treated as thieves—undermines the core claim that they will keep buying. A security flaw is not hypothetical; it is inevitable given the stated design, and it will surface publicly, destroying trust faster than casual copying ever did.

**Alternatives:** Do nothing (lose some revenue, keep customers). Cheaper: offer legal ripping with a small fee or watermarking. The proposal is a one-way door: once installed, removal is difficult, and the reputational damage is irreversible.

**Kill criterion:** None proposed. No pilot, no opt-out, no cap on the intrusion. The plan cannot be reversed without a PR disaster.

**Blocker:** The stated facts show the target users (disc buyers) are also the ones being surveilled and restricted. Their objection is not a future risk; it is the proposal’s own admission. A plan that treats its paying customers as adversaries, with no exit, fails on its own terms.

**Risks:** Security researchers, removal difficulty, backlash. These are not blockers; they are certainties given the design.

**Decision:** The proposal’s success depends on customer goodwill that its own mechanism destroys. No evidence shows buyers will accept this. The alternative of a small, reversible step (e.g., limited protection with clear consent) is cheaper and safer, but the proposal as written does not include it.

DECISION: NO-GO
