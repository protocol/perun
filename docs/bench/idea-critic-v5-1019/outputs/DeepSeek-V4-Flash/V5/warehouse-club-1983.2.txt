First call: GO. The membership model inverts retail economics—profit from fees, not goods—and the competitor’s size is an argument for differentiation, not capitulation.

Steelman: A membership-funded warehouse can underprice the incumbent on staples, win bulk buyers, and scale fast because fees create loyalty and predictable revenue.

Attacks tried:
- Would shoppers pay to shop? Failed—the fee signals exclusivity and locks in repeat trips; the incumbent’s lack of fees doesn’t guarantee loyalty.
- Is the competitor’s scale fatal? Failed—more locations mean higher overhead and less focus on bulk pricing; a leaner entrant can win on price and experience.
- Do thin margins kill errors? Failed—membership fees are the profit engine, so goods margins only need to cover variable costs, not fund growth.
- Are warehouse sites a blocker? Failed—1983 land is cheap outside metros; bulk demand from families and small businesses is proven.

Kill criteria: If membership sign-ups trail projections by 40% in the first six months, or if the incumbent matches prices and fees stay flat, pivot to a smaller format or drop the fee. Review quarterly.

Remaining risk: The incumbent could slash prices to bleed the newcomer, but its cost structure can’t sustain that without hurting its own margins.

Dissent ledger: Scale advantage and thin margins are real but not decisive; the fee model is the moat.

DECISION: GO
