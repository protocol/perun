First call: NO-GO. The stated facts show the program rests on a single, non-binding order and an unproven regulatory path for its core selling point.

Steelman: The airline input ensures the twin is what operators want, the 34-aircraft order proves demand, and the wide-body gap justifies the investment.

Attack: The regulatory approval for twin-engine long overwater flights is the central premise. It is not granted, and the proposal treats it as a risk, not a certainty. Without it, the aircraft cannot fly its intended routes, and the 34-order becomes contingent on an unknown. Development cost is very large, and the rival’s new models add competitive pressure. Designing with eight airlines invites scope creep, delaying a costly program.

The order from one airline is thin evidence of broad market demand. The gap between existing wide-bodies may be real, but launching now on a single customer and an unapproved regime is a bet, not a plan. The cheaper, reversible path is to wait for regulatory clarity and additional airline commitments before committing the very large cost.

Blockers: The proposal’s own facts show the core claim (twin-engine long-haul viability) depends on approval that is absent, and the sole order is insufficient to justify the spend. Risks: cost overruns, delays from airline input, rival competition.

Kill criterion: No regulatory approval, no second major order, no cost cap. The correct move is to hold the launch until those are met.

DECISION: NO-GO
