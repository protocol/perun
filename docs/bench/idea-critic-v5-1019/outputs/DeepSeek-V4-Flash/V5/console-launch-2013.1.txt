DECISION: NO-GO

**First call:** NO-GO. The proposal fails on its own stated facts: the maker is already late and expensive, and the plan offers no credible launch software, a thin margin model, and a head start for the rival in the one area (online) where the maker is weakest. A price cut alone does not fix a content gap.

**Strongest attack survived:** Cheaper hardware + easy dev tools could win back third-party support. But the maker’s own studios have little new software at launch, and the rival’s online lead is a stated strength. The proposal provides no counter to that lead, no marquee exclusive, and no plan to convert a low margin into ecosystem revenue. The upside depends entirely on third parties, yet the maker’s late, expensive prior generation has already eroded their trust.

**Blockers (facts in hand):**
1. **No launch software** — the maker’s own studios have little new at launch. A console with no must-have title does not move hardware, regardless of price.
2. **Rival’s online head start** — the proposal names it as a risk, but offers no mitigation. The rival can match a price cut and retain the online advantage.
3. **Margin math** — below-rival pricing on PC-like hardware (commodity components, thin margins) with no stated service revenue plan. The proposal’s own numbers do not show how this becomes profitable.

**Risks (non-blocking but real):** consumer spending shift, used-game cannibalization, execution risk on a new hardware platform.

**Better way considered:** a cheaper, smaller first step — a focused launch with one strong exclusive and a clear online service plan — but the proposal as written does not include it.

**Kill criteria absent:** no metric for when to stop, no cap on the investment, no date to reassess.

The plan as proposed does not beat do-nothing and its own evidence contradicts its core claim that price alone wins.
