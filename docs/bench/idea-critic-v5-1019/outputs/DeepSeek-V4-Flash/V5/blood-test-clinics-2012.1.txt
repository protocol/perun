First call: NO-GO. The proposal asks for a massive, irreversible commitment based on unverified technology and no internal capability to assess it.

The core claim—that the device works reliably—rests on unpublished, unshared methods. There is no evidence of clinical validation, regulatory approval, or third-party replication. The chain’s entire upside depends on this one unproven assumption.

The chain has zero medical expertise to evaluate the device, run clinics, or manage patient safety. Regulatory risk is not hypothetical; it is stated as a live concern. If regulators later question the tests, the chain bears reputational and legal exposure, not the startup.

The exclusivity clause locks the chain into a single, unverifiable supplier for a decade-scale investment. There is no pilot, no cap, no exit clause mentioned. The downside is catastrophic; the upside is speculative.

A cheaper, reversible alternative exists: run a small pilot in a few stores, with independent lab verification and a clear regulatory review, before committing hundreds of millions. That option reaches the same upside if the technology works, but limits loss if it does not. The proposal as written skips this entirely.

The stated risks are not minor—they are fundamental. Unpublished science, no in-house expertise, no method transparency, and regulatory uncertainty are not execution details; they are blockers. The plan fails on its own facts: it cannot demonstrate the core product works, and it has no path to verify it before committing.

A sound operator would not approve this. The correct call is to reject the proposal as presented and require evidence first.

DECISION: NO-GO
