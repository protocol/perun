First call: NO-GO. The stated mechanics—silent install, hard removal, treating paying customers as suspects—are a self-inflicted wound on the label’s core asset: trust.

Steelman: It reduces casual copying and preserves revenue from a shrinking physical format.

Against do nothing: sales decline continues, but no brand damage. Against cheaper alternatives: DRM-lite, licensing deals, or value-add bundles (bonus tracks, liner notes) achieve the same upside without the liability.

The proposal’s own facts contradict its core claim. It admits the software installs without clear consent and is hard to remove. That is not a risk; it is a stated negative. The target users—the buyers—are the ones being treated as thieves, and the plan offers no path to their acceptance. The security-researcher weakness is a certainty, not a maybe, and the proposal has no answer for it.

Blocker: the plan’s own evidence shows the route to its upside is blocked. Customers who object will return discs, leave negative reviews, and fuel the exact copying behavior the label wants to stop. The negative is stated as already true (intrusive install, hard removal), and the plan provides no mitigation.

Risks (not blocking, because the blocker already decides): legal backlash, technical failure, and a permanent PR scar.

Kill criterion if it were to proceed: a 5% or greater return rate or a measurable spike in negative press within 90 days, triggering immediate recall and removal tooling. But that is moot.

The cheaper, safer path is a pilot on one title with opt-in software and an easy uninstaller, measuring sales impact against the control. That does not reach the same upside as proposed, so it is not a blocker—it is the better way.

DECISION: NO-GO
