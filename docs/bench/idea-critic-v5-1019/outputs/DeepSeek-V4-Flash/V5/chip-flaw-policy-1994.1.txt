First call: NO-GO. The proposal’s core claim—that the flaw is not serious—is contradicted by its own evidence of public scrutiny and reputational risk. A typical-user frequency estimate does not neutralize a defect that undermines trust in fundamental arithmetic.

Steelman: The error is rare, most users are unaffected, and a targeted replacement avoids a massive, unnecessary cost while preserving resources for those truly impacted.

Attack: The plan fails because it asks users to prove a subtle, technical harm they cannot easily demonstrate, while the company’s stance appears defensive. In a widely discussed controversy, requiring proof shifts burden and risk onto customers, amplifying suspicion. The stated cost of full replacement is large, but the cost of a damaged reputation—loss of enterprise trust, design wins, and future sales—is larger and less reversible. The proposal’s own risk list confirms this: users cannot show impact, and reputation may suffer. These are not speculative; they are the plan’s stated conditions, and they undermine its route to the claimed upside (protecting reputation by limiting exposure). The alternative—offer replacement to all who ask, with a clear public statement—costs more now but preserves trust and avoids the appearance of concealment, which is the core asset at risk.

Better way: Full, transparent replacement for any requester, paired with a public acknowledgment of the flaw and its rarity. This is more expensive but directly addresses the reputational risk the proposal itself names.

Kill criteria: If public discussion escalates or if any major customer reports impact, the targeted plan fails immediately. The leading metric is media and customer inquiry volume; review in 30 days.

Blockers: The plan’s own facts show target users cannot easily prove impact, and reputational harm is a stated negative that the plan does not answer. Risks: cost, but that is a trade-off, not a blocker.

DECISION: NO-GO
