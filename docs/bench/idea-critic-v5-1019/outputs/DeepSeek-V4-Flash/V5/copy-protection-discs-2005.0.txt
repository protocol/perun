First call: NO-GO. The core claim—that installing hidden software on buyers’ machines will reduce copying—fails on its own stated facts: it treats every customer as a thief, invites a security backlash, and offers no path to acceptance.

The steelman: copy-protection limits casual ripping and preserves revenue in the short term. But the costs are not speculative: the proposal itself concedes the software is intrusive, hard to remove, and vulnerable to researchers. Those are not future risks; they are design flaws baked into the plan. A security bypass would be public within weeks, turning a revenue problem into a reputational and legal crisis.

Cheaper alternatives exist: watermarking to trace leaks, legal enforcement against large-scale pirates, or a legitimate download store that undercuts the effort of copying. Do-nothing is also better than shipping malware-like software to millions of customers.

The pre-mortem writes itself: a researcher publishes an exploit, the press calls it a rootkit, a class-action suit follows, and the label loses more goodwill than it ever gained in prevented sales. This is a one-way door to customer anger and regulatory scrutiny.

The proposal’s own evidence—that users will object to being treated as thieves—already removes its route to the upside. There is no measured pilot, no opt-in, no removal guarantee, and no kill criterion. The stated facts show the target users will reject it.

Blockers: the plan’s own admission of intrusiveness and non-removability contradicts its core claim of acceptable protection. Risks: security exploits and legal exposure, both certain in outcome if not in timing.

DECISION: NO-GO
