DECISION: NO-GO

The proposal underestimates the reputational and legal risk of a selective replacement policy. The stated "once in many thousands of years" figure is an average, not a guarantee for specific workloads; a user doing millions of divisions daily could hit errors far more often, and proving "affected" work is impractical, creating a class of angry, publicly vocal customers. The problem is already widely discussed, so the company will be judged not by the error's rarity but by its response. Offering replacements only to those who can prove impact looks like evasion, inviting accusations of hiding a defect, regulatory scrutiny, and a PR crisis that dwarfs the replacement cost. The cost of replacing all chips is a large but finite, one-time expense; the cost of a damaged brand and potential lawsuits is open-ended. Public trust, once lost, is not easily bought back. A full recall, while expensive, signals responsibility and decisiveness, converting a technical flaw into a demonstration of integrity. The proposal's "not serious" framing will be rejected by the public; the company's own analysis is not the standard by which it will be judged. The safer, more defensible path is to replace all chips, absorb the cost, and communicate transparently.
