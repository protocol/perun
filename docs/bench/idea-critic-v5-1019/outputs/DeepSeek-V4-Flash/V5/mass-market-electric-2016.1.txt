First call: GO. The proposal is bold but the upside is massive, and the risks are execution challenges, not fatal flaws.

Steelman: The company can leapfrog from niche to mass market by unveiling a compelling, affordable sedan, using deposits to gauge demand, and scaling production to meet it. This is the classic playbook for disrupting incumbents.

Attack: The core risk is not demand but execution — can they actually build at volume? Deposits are refundable, so they are a weak signal. Margin will be thin. Battery supply is a bottleneck.

But: Every mass-market entrant faces these. The company’s small history is a concern, but not a blocker — it has the engineering talent and brand pull to attract investment and talent. The deposits are a marketing tool, not a financial plan. The plan is to start production next year, which is aggressive but not impossible; if they fail, they can slow down. The downside is capped (refundable deposits, no massive sunk cost yet), the upside is transformational.

Do nothing: they stay a niche player, likely to be acquired or fail. Alternatives: build a more expensive car (fails to scale) or partner (loses control). The cheaper sedan is the best path to survival and dominance.

Kill criteria: if they cannot secure battery supply or hit production targets within a year, they should pause. But the proposal itself acknowledges these risks, and they are manageable.

Strongest attack survived: "They have never built at volume, so they will fail." Response: volume manufacturing is a learnable skill, and the company’s existing small-scale production is a foundation, not a disqualifier. The plan is a gamble, but a calculated one with a clear path and a manageable downside.

DECISION: GO
