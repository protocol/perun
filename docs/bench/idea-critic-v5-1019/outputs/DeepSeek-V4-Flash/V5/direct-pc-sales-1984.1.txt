DECISION: NO-GO

The proposal lacks a viable path to scale under its own stated model. Direct-order-only sales in 1984 face a decisive barrier: the target buyer’s demonstrated preference for hands-on evaluation before purchase. The proposal itself lists this risk, and no counter-evidence is offered—no pilot, no pre-orders, no market test showing customers will buy sight-unseen from an unknown brand. The established competitors’ sales channels and brands are not merely risks; they are structural facts that the plan does not answer with any differentiated advantage beyond price. A price advantage alone, without service network or store presence, does not overcome the trust deficit for a first-time, unbranded seller of assembled parts. The absence of a service network is not a future concern but a present negative: a computer that fails has no repair path, and the proposal names no plan to address it. Supplier credit terms are uncertain, but the more fundamental flaw is demand validation. The plan commits to incorporation and growth from revenue before proving that any meaningful number of customers will buy under these constraints. A cheaper, reversible first step—test orders through a small channel, secure a service arrangement, or verify willingness to buy remotely—is available and not considered. The proposal as written cannot reach its stated upside on the facts given. It should be revised to include a capped pilot and a kill criterion before incorporation.
