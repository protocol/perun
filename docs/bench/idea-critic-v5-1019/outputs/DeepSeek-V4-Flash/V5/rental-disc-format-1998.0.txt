First call: NO-GO. The proposal's core economics and user behavior run against stated facts: a pricier player, a limited-use disc, and a phone-line requirement in 1998 all stack friction against a cheaper, open alternative.

Steelman: A low-cost disc with a 48-hour window and pay-to-extend creates recurring revenue, and partner support could seed a captive format.

Attack: The disc is "a few dollars to buy" but only watchable for 48 hours, then pay again. Standard rental already offers similar or better value without a special, more expensive player or phone line. The special player is a sunk cost with no benefit to the customer—only to the retailer's lock-in. Studios and electronics makers must agree, but they have little incentive to back a proprietary, higher-cost system when the standard format is universal and cheaper for consumers. The phone-line requirement adds friction and failure points. Customers already dislike paying for repeated viewing; this model forces it.

Better way: Do nothing, or stick with standard disc rentals—cheaper, no new hardware, no partner risk.

Kill criterion: If partner agreements are not secured at launch, the format dies. No evidence they will commit.

Strongest attack survived: The recurring-fee model could work if the disc were far cheaper than rental and the player subsidized, but the proposal states the player costs more, and discs are only "a few dollars"—not compelling enough to overcome the hardware hurdle.

Risks: partner apathy, customer backlash, higher cost, incompatibility. All are real and unmitigated.

DECISION: NO-GO
