The strongest case for withdrawal is that a known poisoning of the product has already occurred, the vector is unknown, and every additional day on shelf is a day a customer could die — a cost that dwarfs any recall expense and cannot be reversed.

The strongest case against is that the problem may be local, so a nationwide withdrawal sacrifices revenue and market share for a threat that may not exist elsewhere.

The case against fails: "may be local" is not evidence of safety elsewhere. The vector is unknown, so the company cannot bound the geographic risk. Leaving the product on shelf elsewhere is a bet that a killer who has already struck once has not tampered with bottles in other cities — a bet with unbounded downside and no evidence supporting it.

The cost concern is real but misframed. The recall cost is large and certain. The cost of a second poisoning is catastrophic and, given the unknown vector, plausible. A certain large loss is preferable to a plausible catastrophic one, especially when the catastrophic loss also destroys the brand and invites regulation.

The market-share concern is also real but subordinate. Competitors may take shelf space during the withdrawal. But if a customer dies because the company kept shipping to protect market share, the brand is finished and the company faces existential liability. Market share is recoverable; a death from inaction is not.

The "signaling danger" concern is backwards. Withdrawing signals responsibility. Keeping the product on shelf after a known poisoning signals indifference — and leaves the company exposed if a second incident occurs.

The proposal is sound. The relaunch in tamper-resistant packaging is the correct follow-on, and it also positions the company as the industry leader on safety. The only adjustment I would note — not a blocker — is that the company should immediately coordinate with law enforcement and the FDA, so the withdrawal is part of a coordinated response rather than a unilateral act.

DECISION: GO
