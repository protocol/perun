**Steelman:** Copy-protection that auto-installs could meaningfully reduce casual ripping and recover lost sales at minimal per-unit cost.

**Strongest attack survived:** Customers may object to being treated as thieves. This is the sharpest objection because it strikes directly at the brand relationship and the proposal already names it — yet it survives only trivially, because the objection is acknowledged but unaddressed. The proposal treats "customers may object" as a manageable risk when it is in fact the core failure mode.

**Why this does not pass:**

1. **One-way door, badly.** Shipping millions of discs with hidden, hard-to-remove software is irreversible at scale. Once deployed, every disc in the channel carries the risk. The proposal raises the risks but offers no kill criterion, no rollback path, and no consent mechanism.

2. **The blast radius exceeds the problem.** Lost sales to copying is a revenue problem. Secretly installing rootkit-like software on customers' computers is a trust, legal, and security problem. The proposal concedes every one of these risks exists and proposes to proceed anyway.

3. **No alternative weighed.** The proposal jumps to the most aggressive option without comparing it against education campaigns, value-add content, pricing changes, or simpler copy-protection that discloses itself. "Do nothing" may even outperform a solution that triggers a public scandal.

4. **The pre-mortem writes itself.** Security researchers find the hidden software. They discover it creates vulnerabilities. The story goes public. Customers sue. Retailers pull the discs. The brand damage dwarfs the copying losses. Every step is predictable from the risks as stated.

The proposal names its own failure modes and offers no mitigation, no consent, no rollback, and no kill switch. Approving it as proposed would mean accepting an irreversible, multi-million-unit deployment of concealed software on customers' machines in exchange for uncertain revenue protection.

DECISION: NO-GO
