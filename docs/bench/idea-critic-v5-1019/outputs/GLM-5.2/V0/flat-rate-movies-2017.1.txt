The company pays theaters the full ticket price for every film a member sees. At roughly ten dollars a month for one film a day, a single heavy user costs the company about $300 to subsidize a $10 fee. The math is structurally negative on the core transaction, and the proposal depends on unproven revenue streams—data monetization, studio deals, theater partnerships—to cover a loss that scales linearly with the most engaged users. The customers who sign up first are the ones who see the most films.

Theater chains refusing to cooperate is not a tail risk; it is a likely outcome, because the company's business model requires theaters to accept a middleman inserting itself between them and their customers while simultaneously devaluing the per-visit ticket revenue. No studio or theater deal is named, no data revenue figure is given, and no mechanism is described for how these streams would close the per-member gap. The proposal is a one-way door on cash burn with no kill criterion stated: there is no membership threshold at which the data and deal revenues are tested against the subsidy cost before scaling further.

The strongest defensible version of this claim is that a large enough membership base creates a data and distribution platform whose value exceeds the per-member subsidy. That survives only if the revenue streams are proven before the subsidy scales. They are not. The proposal asks the owner to fund an uncapped negative-margin expansion on the promise of revenue streams that are unnamed in scale, unproven in market, and dependent on the cooperation of partners whose interests are directly opposed.

DECISION: NO-GO
