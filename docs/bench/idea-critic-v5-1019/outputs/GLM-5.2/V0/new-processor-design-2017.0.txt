**Steelman:** A core-rich, aggressively priced new architecture lets a smaller player break the rival's performance-per-dollar moat while it still has the capital to swing.

**Strongest attack survived:** "Finances leave no room for a second miss" is the killer — but the proposal is exactly *one* swing at the same architecture bet the company is built on, and the alternative (incremental refinement of a disappointing design) is a slower death that cedes the desktop market entirely.

**Why no-go now:**

1. **One-way door with no kill criteria.** An all-new architecture is a multi-year, near-irreversible commitment. The proposal states the company cannot survive a second miss, yet names no tripwire: no "if single-thread performance doesn't hit X by tape-out, cancel" or "if pre-orders below Y, cut the run." Betting the company without a pre-committed stop is reckless.

2. **Unaddressed software-adoption risk.** More cores only help if software scales. The proposal raises this risk but answers it with nothing — no ISV partnerships, no benchmarking commitment, no "launch first in the segment where core count already matters." Shipping cores software can't use repeats the last miss.

3. **No price-war floor.** "Lower prices than the rival" ignores that the rival has deeper pockets and can sustain losses longer. The proposal doesn't name the margin floor or the scenario in which the rival undercuts and the smaller company bleeds out.

4. **Alternative not weighed.** A focused, cheaper bet — fewer cores, higher clocks, one high-margin segment first — carries lower blast radius and tests the architecture before the company is all-in. The proposal doesn't reject this; it ignores it.

The bet isn't wrong. Shipping it *now, as proposed, without kill criteria, a software story, or a price-war floor* is wrong.

DECISION: NO-GO
