**Steelman:** A minority stake with follow-on rights caps downside at the stake price, preserves optionality on a high-growth segment the giant can't easily build internally, and lets experienced operators run it.

**Strongest attack survived:** "This won't scale because it needs skilled workers and slower throughput." This is the sharpest objection because it targets the core economic engine. But the proposal's structure answers it: a minority stake is a *test* of exactly that scaling question, the follow-on right means the giant only commits more capital *after* the scaling thesis is proven or disproven, and independent operation means the giant's own processes aren't disrupted while learning. The attack fails because the deal is designed as a low-cost experiment on the scaling question itself, not a bet that it's already solved.

**Assumptions:**
- The chain's unit economics work at 16 stores (assumed; no financials given).
- Follow-on right has no time pressure forcing a premature scaling decision.
- Independent governance is contractually enforceable, not aspirational.

**Better ways considered:**
- *Do nothing:* loses the learning and the segment; the giant's own menu can't stretch to this price point without brand dilution.
- *Acquire outright:* too much capital at risk for an unproven scaling model, and destroys the independent culture that makes it work.
- *License the concept:* no control, no learning, brand risk.

Minority stake with optionality beats all three: it's cheaper than acquisition, more controlled than licensing, and more informative than doing nothing.

**Remaining risk:** The scaling question is real and unresolved. But that's precisely what the option structure tests. The price of being wrong is the minority stake; the price of being right but absent is the entire segment.

DECISION: GO
