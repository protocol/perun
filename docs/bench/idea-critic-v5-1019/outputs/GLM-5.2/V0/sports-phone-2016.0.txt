**Steelman:** Shipping in August beats the rival's September unveil to market, capturing demand before the competitor's narrative dominates.

**Assumptions:**
1. The August ship date is achievable with the thinner body and larger battery — **unverified**. Thinner + larger battery is a direct mechanical conflict; the proposal asserts it is resolved without evidence.
2. The iris scanner is production-ready at global volume — **unverified**. A new biometric sensor has no cited yield or reliability data.
3. Early release beats the rival's September announcement — **plausible but assumed**. If the rival announces in early September, an August ship gives only weeks of lead.

**Better ways:**
1. **Hold the ship date, keep the features.** More testing time directly addresses every raised risk. Cost: ceding the pre-rival window.
2. **Ship in August, but drop the iris scanner.** Removes the highest-variance untested component while keeping the schedule advantage and the battery/water-resistance story.
3. **Do nothing; ship on the normal cadence.** Cheapest; the rival's phone existing does not mean this line loses share.

**Kill criteria (missing):** The proposal names risks but sets no threshold at which they trigger a delay or a feature cut. No yield floor for the iris scanner. No battery thermal-test pass criterion. No definition of "enough testing."

**Strongest attack survived:** "August is too early to validate the battery-in-thin-body and the new sensor at scale." This is a real failure mode — a thermal or sensor-yield defect at global volume would be costly — but the proposal does not cite evidence that it *cannot* be validated in the compressed window, nor does it set the kill criteria that would make the bet disciplined. The attack does not kill the idea; it kills the proposal *as written* because the risks are named but not gated.

**Remaining risk:** A global defect from an untested sensor or a thermally stressed battery, with no pre-committed stop condition.

DECISION: NO-GO
