The strongest case for the proposal: the flaw is statistically negligible for almost all users, a full replacement is a very large sum, and offering replacements to affected users is a measured, cost-contained response.

But this case fails on the company's own stated risks. The proposal treats "users can show they are affected" as a workable gate, yet the company itself raises that users *cannot* easily show this. A remedy that the beneficiary cannot trigger is not a remedy; it is a public-relations position dressed as support. The proposal also assumes the flaw stays a quiet technical matter, while the company simultaneously concedes it is already being widely discussed. Once the defect is public, "we will replace chips if you prove harm" reads as the company hiding a defect and shifting the burden of proof onto the customer—the exact reputational outcome the proposal warns against.

The cheaper and stronger alternative is a no-questions-asked replacement program for those who request it, paired with full disclosure of the flaw's rarity. This costs more than the proposed gate, but far less than replacing every chip, and it converts the reputational risk into a reputation for standing behind the product. The status quo—do nothing beyond the gated offer—leaves the company defending a position its own risk list already contradicts.

The pre-mortem is straightforward: the proposal ships, the press reports that affected users are being asked to prove harm, trust collapses, and the company ends up doing the full replacement anyway under worse terms. The kill criterion for proceeding as proposed would be evidence that affected users *can* in fact demonstrate harm cheaply; the company's own risks say they cannot.

Approving as proposed endorses a gate the company itself says is unworkable.

DECISION: NO-GO
