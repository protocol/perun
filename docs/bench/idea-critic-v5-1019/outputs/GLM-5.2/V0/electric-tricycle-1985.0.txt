**Steelman:** A £400, licence-exempt, mail-order tricycle from a household-name assembler could open a new personal-mobility market too cheap and too convenient for incumbents to bother with.

**Skeptic — assumptions**
1. "Sell it by mail order in large numbers" assumes demand exists at scale. Nothing here proves a single buyer at £400, let alone the volume a national launch needs. *Action:* run a small pre-order or regional test; measure conversion, not interest.
2. Pricing "just below £400" with "several parts sold as optional extras" assumes the base price is profitable without the extras. If the margin sits in the add-ons, the unit economics collapse when most buyers take the base. *Action:* state the gross margin on the bare tricycle versus the fully-optioned one.
3. The appliance maker "agreed to assemble" — but on what terms, volumes, and cancellation penalties? *Action:* confirm the assembly contract is binding and priced before committing to a January launch.

**Better-way**
A full national mail-order launch in January is the most expensive way to learn whether the product works. Cheaper path: a limited regional pilot, or a pre-order campaign, to measure real demand and returns before committing large-scale assembly and marketing spend.

**Kill-criteria**
- If January pre-orders or pilot sales fall below a set unit threshold within the first month, stop the national rollout.
- If the return/refund rate exceeds a set percentage, halt mail-order sales until the product is revised.

**Strongest attack survived**
The combination of 15 mph top speed, no weather protection, short range, and low seating among traffic means the product may be unusable or unsafe for enough of the target market that a large-scale launch destroys the brand before demand is proven. This attack failed only partially: the risks are real and specific, and nothing in the proposal shows they have been addressed or tested. A national January launch is a one-way door on reputation and cash before the core question — will people actually buy and keep this? — is answered.

**Remaining risk:** launching big before proving demand; the safety and usability objections are unresolved, not dismissed.

DECISION: NO-GO
