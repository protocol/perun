The strongest case for the proposal: the company cannot verify the scope of contamination, tampering is undetectable to consumers, and the downside of inaction—further poisonings—is catastrophic and unrecoverable. Withdrawing nationwide is expensive, but the cost of a second death is unbounded.

The sharpest attack: withdrawing nationwide treats a local event as a national crisis. The company's own plants are unaffected; the tampering occurred in one metropolitan area. A targeted regional recall would address the known threat at a fraction of the cost. Nationwide withdrawal signals danger in markets where no threat exists, potentially destroying the brand in cities that were never at risk.

Why that attack fails: the critical assumption is that the tampering is local. But the company does not know that. The perpetrator's method, access, and intent are unknown. If tampering is occurring in multiple distribution points and the company has only detected one, a regional recall leaves poisoned product on shelves elsewhere. The asymmetry is decisive: a regional recall that turns out to be insufficient results in more deaths, legal liability that dwarfs the recall cost, and permanent brand destruction. A nationwide recall that turns out to be excessive costs money but harms no one. When the cost of being wrong in one direction is death and in the other is money, you spend the money.

The proposal also includes the correct endgame: relaunch in tamper-resistant packaging. This converts a crisis response into a durable competitive advantage. The brand may lose short-term share, but tamper-resistant packaging becomes the new industry standard, and the company that establishes it first reclaims trust.

The kill criterion is implicit but sound: if no further incidents occur after the nationwide withdrawal and the relaunch succeeds, the decision was correct. If further incidents occur despite the withdrawal, the contamination was not distribution-chain tampering but something else, and the strategy must pivot.

DECISION: GO
