The strongest case for the deal: a supermarket entering diagnostics could capture a large, high-margin health market, and exclusivity forecloses competitors. That is the claim to attack.

The proposal asks the chain to commit $350m and its brand to a device whose accuracy is unverified in any published study, whose methods are secret, and whose regulatory standing is uncertain. The chain has no in-house medical expertise to evaluate the technology independently. Putting clinics into hundreds of stores on that basis would expose every customer to tests of unknown reliability and expose the chain to liability, reputational damage, and stranded capital if the device fails or regulators intervene.

The cheapest alternative is a small, time-boxed pilot: fit clinics into a handful of stores, condition any expansion on published validation studies, independent third-party accuracy testing against gold-standard labs, and a clear FDA clearance pathway. This preserves the option to scale while spending a fraction of $350m and generating real evidence before the full commitment.

The pre-mortem is stark: the deal fails badly if the device produces inaccurate results at scale, regulators halt the rollout, and the chain's brand is tied to a failed health-tech venture. Exclusivity does not mitigate that risk; it amplifies it by concentrating the bet. The kill criteria are themselves the missing evidence: no published studies, no independent validation, no regulatory clearance, no shared methods. None of the risks raised have been converted into testable conditions with thresholds and dates. Approving $350m now, before any of that evidence exists, is a one-way-door decision made too early.

The proposal should come back with a capped pilot, independent validation results, and a defined regulatory pathway. Until then, the full commitment is not justified.

DECISION: NO-GO
