**Steelman:** A minority stake with later-investment rights is a cheap call option on the fast-casual segment — capped downside, uncapped learning, no operational entanglement.

**Strongest attack survived:** "The chain's model is incompatible with the giant's (slower, skilled-labor, higher price), so the giant lacks the competence to evaluate or help it." This fails because the proposal explicitly lets the chain run independently — the giant isn't committing to operate this model, only to learn whether it scales before buying more. The incompatibility is an argument *for* a minority stake rather than a full acquisition.

**Skeptic — assumptions checked:**
- "May not scale" is asserted, not evidenced. But the structure — invest small now, right to invest more later — means the giant pays to *find out* whether it scales, rather than betting the farm.
- "Management attention diverted" is contradicted by "let the chain run independently." If the giant insists on operational control, this risk returns; as proposed, it is covered.
- The option value is real: in 1998, fast-casual is nascent. A minority stake is the lowest-cost way to watch the segment from inside rather than from a competitor's rear-view mirror.

**Kill criteria:** Set a 24-month review. If the chain has not demonstrated unit economics that scale (same-store sales growth and four-wall profitability across at least 20 locations), do not exercise the right to invest more. The initial minority stake is sunk-cost-protected by its small size.

**Remaining risk:** The chain's culture and the giant's may diverge enough that the "right to invest more" is worthless if founders refuse to dilute or sell. This is a negotiation term to verify, not a reason to decline the option.

DECISION: GO
