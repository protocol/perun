**Steelman:** A dominant regional maker can leverage scale economies, existing engineering depth, and dealer networks to cross-subsidize a low-volume halo car that lifts the entire brand's margin ceiling.

**Skeptic — assumptions that fail:**
1. *Badge is everything in luxury.* The proposal concedes buyers pay for the badge, then ignores it. Lexus spent 1989–2000 building a separate luxury identity; VW's Phaeton (this proposal) proved the point by failing.
2. *Worldwide launch at premium parity.* No luxury credentials exist outside the home region. Simultaneous global launch burns capital in markets where the brand is unknown or downmarket.
3. *Dedicated factory at low volume.* Capital intensity per unit is extreme. The math only works if volume far exceeds luxury-segment norms—which contradicts the premium positioning.

**Better-way:** Do nothing (preserve capital for the mass market where dominance exists) OR launch under a new sub-brand in the home region first, building credentials before a global bet.

**Pre-mortem:** The sedan launches worldwide at premium prices under a mass-market name. Luxury buyers, who buy badge and heritage, see a VW on a $70K car and walk to Mercedes. The dedicated factory runs below breakeven. The halo tarnishes the core brand. VW lost ~€1B+ on the Phaeton; the failure mode is already demonstrated by the same company.

**Kill criteria absent:** No volume threshold, no breakeven date, no brand-equity metric. The proposal commits to a dedicated factory and global launch with no stated stop condition.

**Strongest attack survived:** None. The central objection—that luxury buyers purchase badge equity, not engineering quality—is acknowledged in the risks and never addressed in the proposal. The 2002 VW Phaeton proceeded anyway and became a textbook case of brand-extension failure.

DECISION: NO-GO
