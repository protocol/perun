**Steelman:** A £400 licence-exempt tricycle, assembled by a major appliance maker and sold by mail order, could capture a new market segment at a price point that makes impulse purchase plausible.

**Strongest attack survived:** "15 mph top speed and no weather protection make it impractical for British buyers." This is a genuine product-limitation concern, but the proposal already names these as risks raised — they are known constraints, not hidden defects. The question is whether they are fatal, and the evidence given does not establish that: a slow, exposed, short-range vehicle is a niche product, not necessarily a non-starter. Sinclair's track record and the appliance-maker commitment are not in evidence either way.

**Why this is still NO-GO as proposed:**

1. **Kill criteria absent.** The proposal commits to a January launch and mail-order sales "in large numbers" but names no metric, threshold, or date at which to stop. A vehicle programme is capital-intensive; launching without a pre-committed kill criterion is a one-way bet dressed as a plan.

2. **No alternative priced.** "Launch in January" is set against nothing — not a delayed launch with weather-protection prototypes, not a regional pilot, not a smaller first batch. The cheapest path (pilot before national mail-order) is not considered.

3. **Safety objection unaddressed.** "Safety groups may object" is treated as a risk to note, not a scenario to plan for. A low-sitting vehicle in traffic is a regulatory and PR exposure; the proposal does not say how it would be handled.

4. **Seasonal timing.** Unveiling in January and selling by mail order into British winter is the worst window for a vehicle with no weather protection. The proposal does not acknowledge the calendar mismatch.

The idea is not necessarily wrong. The proposal as written is: it asks for commitment to a launch date and sales volume without a stop condition, a cheaper alternative, or a plan for the safety objection.

DECISION: NO-GO
