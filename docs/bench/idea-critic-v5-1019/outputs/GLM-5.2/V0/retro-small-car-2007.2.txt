**Steelman.** Retro styling on an existing platform lets a weak carmaker charge a premium without a clean-sheet program, if nostalgia still sells.

**Skeptic.** Every named risk is an *unsupported claim presented as fact*. "Styling may date quickly" — retro is already dated; that is the appeal. "Buyers may not pay more" — Mini proved they would. "Recent models sold poorly" — those cars lacked this hook. "Thin margins" — the whole thesis is premium trim options lifting margin. Each risk has a ready counter, so none is a deal-breaker on its own. But the proposal bundles *all four* and answers none with evidence.

**Better-way.** "Do nothing" is the strongest alternative: a weak-balance-sheet carmaker launching a low-margin small car into a segment where it has recently failed is a bet the company may not survive if it goes wrong. The cheaper path — a face-lifted version of the platform-mate at the same price — tests nostalgia demand without the premium-pricing risk.

**Kill criteria.** The proposal names no metric, no threshold, no stop date. For a company that cannot afford a miss, that is the sharpest objection: there is no pre-committed point at which to pull the plug.

**Pre-mortem.** The car launches at a premium, buyers cross-shop the cheaper platform-mate, volumes undershoot, and the thin-margin program drains cash the company did not have. That failure is plausible and unguarded.

**Decision.** The idea has real merit, but as proposed it ships without evidence against its own risks and without a kill switch. A weak-financed carmaker should not greenlight a bet that names its failure modes and then answers none.

DECISION: NO-GO
