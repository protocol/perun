**Steelman:** Retro styling on an existing platform can command a premium and emotional loyalty if the design is timeless and the execution is flawless.

**Skeptic — assumptions:**
- *Buyers will pay a premium over the platform-mate.* Assumed. Nothing here proves the brand has pricing power in this segment.
- *Retro styling will resonate.* A 1957 design language may read as nostalgic to enthusiasts and dated to mainstream buyers within 18 months.
- *Personalization options will lift margin.* Configurator complexity on a thin-margin small car can erode, not raise, per-unit profit if take rates are low.

**Better-way:**
- *Do nothing.* The company has weak finances and a track record of poor-selling models. Launching another small car repeats a known-bad pattern.
- *Cheaper path.* If the retro concept has merit, gauge demand with a design study or a limited build (under 5,000 units) before committing to full production and a broad trim matrix.

**Kill criteria:**
- Pre-orders below 15,000 in the first six months → cancel or reduce volume.
- Per-unit margin below target after three months of sales → cut the personalization matrix or raise price.
- Resale value drops more than 10% versus the platform-mate at 12 months → the styling is dating; wind down.

**Strongest attack survived:** "The company is financially weak and its recent models sold poorly, so it cannot afford another miss." This is the sharpest objection and it is **not** fully resolved — the proposal does not explain why this car reverses the pattern. It survives only insofar as reusing an existing platform limits capital exposure, but the premium-pricing and broad-personalization bets compound, not mitigate, the risk.

**Remaining risk:** The proposal asks a financially weak company to bet on premium pricing and configurator complexity in a segment known for thin margins, without evidence that buyers will pay more than for the platform-mate.

DECISION: NO-GO
