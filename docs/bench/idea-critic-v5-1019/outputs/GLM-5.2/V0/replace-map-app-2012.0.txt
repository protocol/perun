The strongest case for the proposal: a phone maker that cedes maps to a rival cedes a core platform surface and the location data that feeds future products. Control of maps is a strategic necessity; shipping imperfect is acceptable if the feedback loop starts now.

But the proposal as stated fails the pre-mortem. Assume it shipped and failed badly: users open Maps, find their town mislabeled, businesses missing, routes wrong; the press runs side-by-side comparisons; the CEO publicly apologizes and suggests alternatives — including the rival's web app. The failure mode is not "some data is wrong." It is a perception collapse where every individual error becomes a headline, and the working app users trusted is gone.

That is a one-way door. Removing the established app by default cannot be cleanly reversed mid-cycle; the reputational damage compounds. The kill criteria are absent: no threshold for "wrong enough to roll back," no pilot geography, no metric that would trigger reverting to the rival's app before the damage becomes permanent.

A cheaper path exists: ship the new maps as an opt-in default alongside the established app for one cycle, measure error rates and user satisfaction against the incumbent on a defined cohort, and flip to sole-default only if the new app clears a stated quality bar. This preserves the strategic goal — own the mapping surface — while making the transition reversible and evidence-gated.

The risks raised are real and acknowledged, but acknowledging risk is not mitigating it. Shipping a known-inferior replacement for a working app, at scale, with no rollback trigger, bets the brand on data quality that the proposal itself admits is incomplete.

DECISION: NO-GO
