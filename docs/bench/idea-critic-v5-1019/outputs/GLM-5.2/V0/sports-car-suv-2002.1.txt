**Steelman:** A loyal enthusiast brand can extend into a profitable, high-growth segment using group resources, capturing revenue and customers impossible to reach with a small sports-car range.

**Strongest attack survived:** "An SUV under a purist sports-car brand will alienate the core and dilute the image." This is the central risk. But the proposal has no evidence that loyalists leave rather than grumble, no data on segment growth versus sports cars, and no test of whether off-road ability adds or distracts. The attack is plausible and unresolved.

**Better ways — not considered:**
- *Do nothing:* keep the small range, protect the brand, avoid the high first-year cost. The baseline this must beat.
- *Different brand:* launch the crossover under a new or group sub-brand, protecting the sports-car identity. The proposal never weighs this.
- *Smaller first step:* a concept or limited run to test reception before committing the full high-priced launch.

**Fit:** The north star is presumably the sports-car brand's identity and survival. This proposal bets the brand on an untested segment with strong incumbents, high cost, and image risk — without a single piece of evidence that the bet pays.

**Pre-mortem:** It is year two. The crossover sold below forecast, loyalists publicly defected, the press calls it a heavy car with a sports badge, and the group partner wants a price cut. The first-year cost is sunk. The brand is weaker than before.

**Decision: NO-GO**

The proposal lacks evidence the segment grows, that loyalists stay, or that the sports image survives a heavy off-road vehicle. It ignores cheaper alternatives — a sub-brand, a concept test, or doing nothing. Approving as proposed commits high cost and brand risk on assumption alone. Revise with market data, a brand-dilution test, and a smaller first step before any go.
