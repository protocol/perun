Steelman: at ~$35, a phone-controlled dongle undercuts $99 boxes while reusing the installed base of Android phones and Chrome as the remote; the open kit turns the thin client into a platform others extend.

Cheaper alternative: keep casting inside phones and TVs via existing protocols — but that leaves no owned TV surface and no developer ecosystem. Do-nothing: the company already failed with a fuller device; another swing at that is the real waste.

Fit: the north star is getting the company's services onto the biggest screen in the home; a near-zero-margin dongle is a distribution play, not a hardware-profit play. That reframing dissolves the margin objection — the dongle is customer acquisition for services and ads.

Pre-mortem: it fails if buyers expect a standalone box and return it, or if the open kit never attracts apps beyond the company's own. Reversibility: a dongle is a two-way door — kill the SKU, keep the protocol. Cost of delay: another holiday season with no TV presence while Apple and Roku entrench.

Evidence: the proposal asserts "few apps at launch," but the company's own video and music services are the #1 reason a buyer picks this up; if those cast cleanly, the catalog gap is a launch note, not a blocker. "May confuse buyers" is a note, not a failure scenario: the concrete risk is returns, and the kill criterion below covers it.

Kill criteria: if return rate exceeds 8% in the first 60 days or third-party app count stays below 20 at the 6-month mark, cut price further or pivot to a bundled-remote SKU. Leading metric: activation rate within 7 days of purchase; review at 90 days.

The sharpest attack — "no remote and no menu will confuse buyers" — survives as a real risk but not a blocker: phone-as-remote is the product, and the return-rate tripwire catches the failure mode if it materializes.

DECISION: GO
