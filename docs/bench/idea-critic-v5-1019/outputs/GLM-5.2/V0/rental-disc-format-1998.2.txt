**Steelman.** The strongest defensible version of the claim: a self-destructing disc with a 48-hour window captures the rental market's economics (pay-per-use, no return trip, no late fees) while giving studios a new revenue stream and electronics makers a premium-priced player category — the Divx model, 1998.

**Assumptions.**
1. Studios will license content to a format that competes with their own DVD revenue. *No evidence given that any studio agreed.*
2. Electronics makers will build special players at a price customers accept. *No evidence any maker committed.*
3. Customers prefer a phone-line-dependent, higher-cost player over a standard DVD player. *The proposal concedes the discs cannot play on standard players and the players cost more.*
4. Customers dislike paying for repeated viewing — this is stated as a risk, then left unresolved. *No test, no pilot, no data.*

**Better ways.**
1. **Do nothing.** The retailer already sells electronics and media; it can ride DVD adoption with zero format risk.
2. **Support standard DVD.** A disc that plays on existing players removes the special-player adoption barrier entirely.
3. **Rental model.** The retailer already knows rental economics; a disc-based rental avoids a new format war.

**Kill criteria.**
- **Premortem.** The format already failed badly: studios balked at fragmenting the DVD market; electronics makers refused a second player SKU; customers, offered a cheaper standard DVD, ignored the phone-line player; the retailer ate the format investment. *This is close to the historical outcome.*
- **Door class.** One-way door. A format launch commits capital, partner relationships and brand to a path that cannot be quietly reversed.
- **Cost of delay.** Waiting a quarter loses first-mover advantage — but first-mover advantage in a format war is a liability if the format loses.

**Strongest attack survived.** "Studios and electronics makers must agree" — and the proposal names this risk but provides no evidence any partner agreed. Without at least one studio and one player manufacturer committed, the format is a bet on unspecified future consent. That is not a bet worth taking now.

**Remaining risk.** The retailer's brand is tied to a format that, if it fails, leaves customers with orphaned players and discs — a reputational cost the proposal does not price.

DECISION: NO-GO
