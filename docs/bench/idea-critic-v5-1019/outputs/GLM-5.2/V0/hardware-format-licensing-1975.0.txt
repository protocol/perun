The steelman is that a proprietary, tightly licensed format can protect margins, preserve quality control, and let first-mover advantage compound before the rival ships. That case survives only if network effects are weak and content availability is irrelevant.

It does not survive. The decisive factor in a consumer recording format is installed base: more licensed manufacturers means more decks on shelves, lower retail prices, and more pre-recorded titles. The proposal bets on picture quality and a one-hour tape against a rival already pursuing the opposite strategy—broad licensing and longer recording time. One hour cannot hold a feature film or a sports broadcast; that is not a marginal limitation but a category-defining one for a home VCR. Quality alone rarely wins a standards war once content and price align behind the alternative.

The pre-mortem is straightforward: the rival's format becomes the de facto standard, the proprietary format's few partners lose interest, pre-recorded catalogs dry up, and the early lead is stranded. The reversal cost of a closed format is high because by the time the network gap is visible, content partners have committed elsewhere.

The strongest attack survived: that a closed format could still win on superior technology and brand, as sometimes happens in consumer electronics. It fails here because the one-hour limitation removes the core use case and the rival is already solving for duration and reach.

The proposal commits the company to a one-way door—proprietary lock-in against a broadly licensed rival—on a product whose primary recording use is unserved by the tape length. Approving as proposed would forfeit the standards race before it is run.

DECISION: NO-GO
