The single order for 34 aircraft cannot, on its own, carry a program of this cost. The steelman is that a launch customer plus eight airlines co-designing signals real market pull and de-risks the specification; but that case rests on a twin-engine approval that has not been granted, and the proposal does not make the order, the cost or the approval conditional.

The strongest attack I can mount is this: the program launches on one airline's 34 units, at very large cost, while the very regulatory approval that lets a twin fly the routes the aircraft is built for remains ungranted. If the authorities decline or delay, the aircraft's central value proposition collapses and the cost is sunk. That attack survives because nothing in the proposal answers it.

The better way is not "do nothing" — the market is real and a rival is moving. The better way is to **wait for the regulatory decision**, or launch conditionally on it, while converting more of the eight airlines to firm orders. The cost of waiting is modest next to the cost of launching a wide-body program that cannot be operated as intended.

**Decision:** No-go as proposed. Re-enter with a conditional structure: launch contingent on regulatory approval, a larger firm-order base, and a defined cost ceiling.

**Kill criteria if relaunched:** if regulatory approval is not granted within the defined window, stop; if firm orders fall below a stated threshold before a milestone date, stop.

**Leading metric:** firm orders from airlines beyond the launch customer, reviewed at the regulatory-decision date.

**Remaining risk:** the rival captures market share during the wait; this is a two-way-door risk that the conditional structure addresses without the full downside of an unapproved launch.

DECISION: NO-GO
