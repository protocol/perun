The proposal asks buyers to pay established-premium prices for a mass-market badge. That is the central risk, and it is not addressed by a dedicated factory, a large engine, or good materials—those are table stakes in this class, not differentiators.

The steelman: scale and engineering depth let a dominant regional maker absorb early losses, and product excellence can shift perception over a generation. But the proposal's own risks name the failure mode: premium buyers pay for the badge. A mass-market brand carrying a luxury sedan is a one-way-door bet—committed factory, worldwide launch, low-volume cost penalty—against a brand-equity gap no single product closes. Volkswagen's later Phaeton is the cautionary parallel: engineering excellence, premium pricing, mass-market badge, commercial failure.

Cheaper alternatives exist. A sub-brand or joint venture tests luxury acceptance without burning the mass-market name. A regional-first launch validates pricing power before a worldwide commitment. "Do nothing" preserves capital for the core business where margins and brand fit are proven.

The kill criterion is missing. The proposal gives no metric, threshold, or date at which to stop. Without it, sunk-cost pressure on a dedicated factory makes retreat nearly impossible.

The brand-equity gap is the unresolved objection. No amount of suspension tuning or material quality makes buyers pay Mercedes prices for a mass-market name unless the badge already carries that permission—and by the proposal's own admission, it does not.

DECISION: NO-GO
