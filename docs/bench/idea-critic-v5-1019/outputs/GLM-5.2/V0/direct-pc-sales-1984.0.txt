The strongest version of this proposal is that direct-only assembly eliminates the retail markup and inventory risk that burden established brands, letting a student undercut them on price while reinvesting cash from each sale. The attack that should break it: buyers of a several-hundred-dollar durable want to touch it, test it, and know where to bring it if it breaks. A seller with no storefront, no service network, and no brand has no answer to that.

But the proposal does not need to answer it fully to be a sound bet. It needs to survive its first pilot. The test is narrow: can the student assemble a working machine, deliver it, have the buyer be satisfied, and repeat enough times that revenue covers parts and leaves margin? If the first ten buyers are happy, the model has legs; if they return units or demand refunds the model fails before scale matters. The credit constraint is real but binding only at growth, not at proof. The competitive response from established brands is slow precisely because their retail-channel margins make direct price-cutting structurally hard for them.

The one genuine gap: the proposal names no kill criterion. A sound first step deserves one. If, after thirty units sold, more than fifteen percent come back defective or unsatisfied, or if gross margin per unit cannot cover assembly time within sixty days, stop.

That gap is fixable in the pilot design without re-opening the strategy. The core claim—direct assembly at below-brand prices, funded from revenue—survives its sharpest objection because the objection concerns scale and trust, both of which the pilot itself tests before any irreversible commitment.

DECISION: GO
