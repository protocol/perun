The steelman: copy protection on shipped discs directly attacks the revenue loss from casual copying, and millions of discs is a fast distribution channel.

**Strongest attack survived — and why it failed to survive:**

The security-researcher risk is not a footnote; it is the proposal's center of gravity. Software that auto-installs from an audio disc, is hard to remove, and runs at system level is a high-value target. When a researcher inevitably finds a weakness, the company has handed every attacker a pre-installed foothold on millions of customer machines — and the "hard to remove" property now cuts against the company, not for it. The blast radius is not "bad press"; it is a class-action liability surface and potential statutory exposure, all to stop copying that determined users will route around anyway.

This attack is concrete and the proposal's own risk list names its components. Nothing in the proposal — no consent mechanism, no uninstall path, no security audit, no kill switch — addresses it. The risks are listed as considerations, not as design constraints with mitigations.

**The cheaper alternative:** do nothing on the disc side; pursue licensing, digital distribution, or legal remedies against large-scale copiers. These are slower and less satisfying but carry none of the asymmetric downside of installing hidden software on customer computers.

**Pre-mortem:** the discs ship; within months a researcher publishes a remote-exploit path through the copy-protection software; the company recalls millions of discs, faces lawsuits and regulator scrutiny, and the revenue saved is a fraction of the liability created.

The proposal conflates "copy protection" with "covert auto-installing software." The former is debatable; the latter, in 2005, is a bet that no researcher will look — and researchers always look.

DECISION: NO-GO
