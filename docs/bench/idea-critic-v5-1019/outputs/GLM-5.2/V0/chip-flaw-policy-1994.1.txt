**Steelman:** Once-in-many-thousands-of-years is, for almost any user, below the threshold where a recall is justified; gating replacements on demonstrated need conserves a very large sum while still offering a remedy.

**Attack — "users cannot easily show they are affected":** This is fatal to the gating mechanism. The entire cost-saving logic rests on *most* users not claiming a replacement. But if a user cannot prove they are affected, then the company cannot prove they are *not*. The gate is asymmetric: it blocks legitimate claims while providing no defensible test. The cost saving is therefore not real — it is an accounting fiction that converts a known defect into an unbounded liability.

**Attack — reputation:** The flaw is already public. "State that the flaw is not serious" is a public-relations claim about a technical defect that the company itself admits exists. The moment a user finds a reproducible wrong answer, the company's statement becomes a demonstrated falsehood. The reputational damage from "they hid it" is strictly greater than the damage from "they acknowledged and fixed it."

**Kill criterion (the pre-mortem):** Assume this plan shipped and failed badly. Why? A researcher publishes a minimal test case producing wrong division results. The press reports that the company knew and told users the flaw was not serious. The stock drops. The CEO is asked why the company did not simply offer replacements. There is no good answer, because the flaw is real, the statements were false, and the gating mechanism was designed to save money at the expense of users who could not prove harm.

**Better way:** Acknowledge the flaw publicly, offer no-questions replacements to anyone who asks, and absorb the cost. This is more expensive but it is the only option that survives contact with a reproducible test case and a hostile press.

DECISION: NO-GO
