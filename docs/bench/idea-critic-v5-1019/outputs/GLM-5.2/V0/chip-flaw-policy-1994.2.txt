**Steelman:** Replacing only demonstrably-affected chips saves a very large sum while sparing the vast majority of users, who would meet the error about once in many thousands of years.

**Strongest attack survived:** Users cannot easily *prove* they are affected, so the "show your work is affected" gate is effectively a refusal to replace — a reputational and liability exposure that dwarfs the replacement cost once the flaw is publicly discussed.

**Why it fails:** The attack is correct. The proposal's own risk list names the two failure modes — proof burden on users, and public perception of concealment — and the proposal resolves neither. A defect acknowledged but gated behind an impossible proof standard reads as evasion; the replacement cost, however large, is a one-time charge against a durable trust deficit.

**Assumptions:**
- The "once in many thousands of years" figure is the company's own; it has not been independently verified. **assumed**
- The flaw is rare but deterministic: affected workloads *will* produce wrong answers, not *might*. **assumed**

**Better ways:**
- Offer no-questions replacement to any requester; absorb the cost as a trust investment. Cheaper in reputation than the gated path.
- Publish the exact inputs that trigger the bug and a software workaround, so users can self-assess without proving harm to the company.

**Kill criteria:**
- If public discussion of the flaw increases buyer concern above a named threshold within a named window, switch from gated replacement to universal replacement.
- Leading metric to watch: ratio of replacement requests to units shipped, reviewed weekly for the first quarter.

**Remaining risk:** Even universal replacement may not fully restore trust once the initial gated response is perceived as concealment; the window for a clean response may already be closed.

DECISION: NO-GO
