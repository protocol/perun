**Steelman:** A $35 dongle with no remote removes the cost, complexity, and UI friction that killed earlier television attempts; the phone becomes the input device and the dongle becomes a dumb pipe, which is the cheapest possible entry into the living room.

**Strongest attack survived:** "No remote means buyers return it." The proposal reframes the absence as the design: the phone *is* the remote. This is a genuine bet, not a gap — it trades a familiar input method for lower cost and simpler setup. The attack fails because the alternative (including a remote) raises BOM cost and recreates the crowded-box problem that already defeated this company.

**Skeptic:** The margin objection is real but misframed. The goal is not dongle profit; it is getting a search company's services onto the largest screen in the house. A cheap device is a distribution play, not a hardware business. Few launch apps is a timing problem, not a design flaw — the open kit exists precisely to close it. Established competitors have more apps, but they also charge more and require their own remotes; the dongle's wedge is simplicity and price, not catalog depth.

**Better-way:** "Do nothing" leaves the company off television entirely while competitors cement their positions. "Build a full box" repeats the failed prior attempt at higher cost. The dongle is the cheapest reversible probe: small investment, fast feedback, and a clear kill signal if returns spike or developer adoption stays flat by the first quarter.

**Kill criteria:** If return rates exceed 10% within the first quarter, add a minimal remote or kill the product. If fewer than 20 third-party apps ship within six months, the open-kit thesis is failing — reassess.

The core bet — that people prefer using their phone as the remote and will accept fewer apps for a cheaper, simpler device — is a reasonable, reversible, low-cost probe into a strategically critical surface.

DECISION: GO
