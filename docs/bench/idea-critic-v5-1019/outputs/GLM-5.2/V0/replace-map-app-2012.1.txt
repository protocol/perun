The strongest case for the proposal: control of a core platform surface is strategically essential, and a mapping default that ships with the new OS cycle captures every new and upgraded user at once.

The strongest attack actually tried: the proposal bets the entire user base on a data layer the proposer itself admits is thinner and less accurate than the rival's, while simultaneously removing the working alternative. "Users may be annoyed" understates the risk — if place data is wrong, users lose trust in the phone itself, not just the maps app. The blast radius is the full installed base; the reversibility is low once the rival's contract ends and the default ships in the OS. The kill criterion — "are the maps good enough?" — is answered by the proposer's own risk list before launch: no.

That said, the one concrete failure scenario is clear: a user in a major city searches for a business, gets a wrong location, and the voice assistant routes them incorrectly. Nothing in the proposal names a data-accuracy threshold, a city-by-city validation gate, or a fallback to the rival app while the new one matures. The pre-mortem writes itself: the phone maker is remembered for launching a beautiful but broken maps app, and the CEO has to apologize publicly.

The better way is not "don't ship" — it is "don't bet everyone at once." Ship the new maps as a default on new phones only, keep the rival app available as a one-tap alternative for a defined transition window, and gate full rollout on a measured data-accuracy and user-satisfaction threshold across the top markets. This preserves the strategic goal — owning the maps surface — while making the blast radius survivable and the kill criterion observable.

DECISION: NO-GO
