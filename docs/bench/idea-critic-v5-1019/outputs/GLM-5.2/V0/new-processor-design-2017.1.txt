The strongest case for this proposal is that a smaller player cannot close a performance gap by incrementalism; a new architecture with more cores at lower prices is the only path to re-claiming the performance crown and winning back the desktop market. The alternative—another refresh of the disappointing earlier design—concedes the segment entirely.

But the attack that breaks the claim is this: the proposal's own risk list contains the company's existential constraint—"finances leave no room for a second miss"—yet the plan offers no kill criteria, no capped first step, and no fallback if software doesn't use the extra cores or the rival cuts prices. Betting the company on an all-new architecture, after the last all-new design underperformed, without a pre-committed stop condition or a time-boxed pilot with a named threshold, is a one-way-door bet presented as if it were routine.

The better-way alternative is not "do nothing." It is: build the new architecture, but launch a smaller, capped desktop SKU first—enough to prove the architecture performs, that software adoption is real, and that the price-core positioning wins reviews—before committing the full product line and the financial resources that cannot be replaced. This preserves the strategic bet while converting a one-way door into a two-way door.

The unresolved question: what single measurable outcome in the first launch window (review scores, unit volume, software-vendor commitments, margin) would tell us to scale up or stop? Without that named metric and threshold, the proposal asks for an irreversible, all-in commitment with no defined point to reverse course.

DECISION: NO-GO
