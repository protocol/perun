**Steelman:** First-mover advantage in time-limited physical media could capture rental revenue before broadband streaming threatens the model.

**Strongest attack survived:** The proposal requires unprecedented coordination across studios, electronics makers, and retailers to support a proprietary format with higher-cost players — and this multi-party alignment is the exact failure mode that killed most alternative disc formats in this era. It survives only because the potential upside of locking in a rental-revenue stream before competitors is real, but the attack reveals the proposal's fatal dependency.

**Assumptions:**
- Studios will license content to a restrictive, higher-cost format (unverified, contradicts their DVD revenue interests)
- Electronics makers will produce special players at competitive prices despite limited disc compatibility
- Customers will accept paying repeatedly for content they physically own

**Better ways:**
- Do nothing: standard DVD rental already exists and generates revenue without proprietary hardware
- Partner on standard DVD rental pricing tiers rather than inventing a new format

**Kill criteria:**
- If fewer than 3 major studios commit to licensing within 6 months, stop
- If special player cost cannot reach within 20% of standard players before launch, stop

**Remaining risk:** The entire value proposition depends on three independent parties (studios, electronics makers, customers) all accepting worse economics than the emerging standard DVD model offers them. The phone-line callback requirement adds infrastructure friction and privacy concerns. No evidence is presented that any party beyond the proposer has indicated willingness to participate. The format's technical restrictions (discs unplayable on standard players, repeated payment for viewing) directly conflict with consumer expectations shaped by the rental model the proposal claims to disrupt.

DECISION: NO-GO
