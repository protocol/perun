**Claim:** A fast-food giant should take a minority stake in a 16-restaurant burrito chain, with rights to invest more later, letting it run independently.

**Steelman:** The option to acquire later, before scale is proven or priced in, is worth more than the minority stake alone. Chipotle's 1998 reality: 16 stores, $20M+ revenue, 60%+ margins, Steve Ells. McDonald's invested $50M for ~33%, eventually worth $1.4B+.

**Strongest attack survived:** "Tiny chains rarely scale; burrito assembly needs skilled workers and is slower than fast food." True as a base rate, yet the model is replicable, unit economics are strong, and the minority stake plus later-invest right caps downside while preserving upside. The attack fails because the option structure already prices the uncertainty.

**Better ways considered:**
- Do nothing — zero risk, zero learning in fast-casual.
- Acquire outright now — overpays before scale is proven.
- Minority stake with optionality — cheapest learning with capped downside.

**Kill criteria:** If unit-level margins fall below 40% or store count fails to reach 30 within three years, exit or do not exercise the later-invest right.

**Remaining risk:** Management attention. Mitigated by independent operation; the giant provides capital and scale leverage, not operational control.

The structure is sound: minority position caps financial exposure, the later-invest right preserves acquisition optionality, and independent operation prevents cultural contamination and attention drain. The base-rate objection (small chains don't scale) is real but asymmetric — the downside is the minority stake amount, the upside is acquiring a proven scaler before competitors notice. Reversibility is high at this stage.

DECISION: GO
