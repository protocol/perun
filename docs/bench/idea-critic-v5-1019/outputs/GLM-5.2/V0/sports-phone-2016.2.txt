**Steelman:** Pulling the launch forward by one month, while adding three simultaneous changes (thinner body + larger battery, new iris scanner, water resistance), maximizes the chance of a defect escaping into a global volume shipment.

**Skeptic — assumptions treated as fact:**
- "A rival is expected to unveil a new flagship in September" — *assumed*. Is the rival's date firm, and does one month of market lead actually offset the risk of shipping a defect at global scale?
- "Ship it in volume worldwide" — *assumed*. What is the volume, the ship-window, and the unit cost of a defect? The proposal names none of these.
- "The schedule leaves less time for testing" — *admitted by the proposer*. They are asking to compress the test window precisely when the number of untested-at-volume variables is at its highest.

**Better-way:** The cheaper path is to hold the August date for the *known-good* changes (larger battery, water resistance) and defer the new iris scanner to the next cycle, OR keep the iris scanner but hold the launch to the normal September window. The proposal bundles all three risks and moves the date; the alternative separates them.

**Kill-criteria:** The proposal gives no observable metric, no threshold, and no stop/pivot action for the compressed test window. There is no answer to "what measured result lets us ship in August, and what do we do if we don't see it?"

**Strongest attack survived:** None. The core mechanism — how testing is de-risked when the schedule is compressed and the change-count is higher — is unnamed. A defect at global scale is a one-way-door cost; the proposal treats the August date as fixed and the testing as flexible, which is the inversion that produces flagship recalls.

The proposal bundles three independent risks, compresses the window that mitigates them, and ships at global volume with no stated kill criterion. Approving as proposed bets the brand on a schedule with no off-ramp.

DECISION: NO-GO
