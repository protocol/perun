**Steelman:** the maker's phone, OS, app ecosystem, retail stores, brand pull and health-sensor IP turn a modest category into a defensible wearable platform — and no rival can match that bundle.

**Origin:** executive proposal → `PASS_TO_USER` only if it survives.

**Skeptic.** Every named risk — one-day battery, phone-dependence, "second screen" doubt — is a real failure mode. But none is unaddressed: the phone pairing is the point (the watch is an accessory, not a standalone device), one-day battery is the known cost of a bright OLED and full-day use, and "buyers may not see a need" is exactly what a first-mover test measures. The sharper attack: Apple's own health-and-fitness narrative was thin in 2015 (Workout and Activity existed; the medical-research story was announced but unproven). If the watch is just "notifications on your wrist," it loses. The proposal does name health tracking and an app store, so the bet is not notifications-only — it is a platform. That survives.

**Better-way.** Cheapest alternative: ship a fitness band first, learn the sensors and the pairing, then graduate to a full watch. It is lower-risk and reversible. But it cedes the flagship wearable position, fragments the app-store story, and undercuts the brand. The full watch, done once, is the stronger play if the company can absorb a first-generation miss.

**Kill-criteria.** First window: six months of sell-through. Kill or pivot: sell-through below 60% of channel forecast at month 6, or app-store submission volume below 1,000 native apps by month 6. Leading metric: repeat-purchase rate of the sport band among existing watch owners at month 9.

**Remaining risk:** the watch is a first-generation product; first-generation Apple products have historically needed two to three iterations to reach their stride.

DECISION: GO
