The strongest version of the claim: direct-only distribution eliminates the reseller markup, letting a small assembler undercut branded IBM-compatibles on price while reinvesting revenue to fund growth — a model viable at the low end where buyers prioritize price over hand-holding.

**Skeptic.** The four named risks are all real and none is resolved by the proposal. "No service network" is the sharpest: a direct-buyer with a hardware fault has no local shop to return it to, and a student-run operation cannot field a national repair flow. The proposal answers none of these; it restates the model and asks to proceed. The growth-from-revenue assumption is also untested: if early units come back defective, revenue inverts to support cost.

**Better-way.** The cheapest alternative is a single-city pilot — assemble, sell direct, and measure returns and repeat-buy rate before incorporating. Incorporation is a fixed cost with no demonstrated payoff until the unit economics survive real customers. "Do nothing" loses little: the student can still build and sell informally while the pilot runs.

**Kill-criteria.** No stop condition is stated. A pilot needs one: if more than X% of units are returned or warranty-cost per unit exceeds Y within the first quarter, stop and reconsider the model. Without that, the proposal is a bet with no exit.

**Fit.** The north star is a price-competitive direct assembler that funds its own growth. The proposal moves toward it but does not prove the model survives contact with the four risks.

**Verdict.** The model is promising but the proposal is premature: it skips the cheap, reversible pilot that would test the four risks and establish unit economics, and it carries no kill criterion. Incorporate only after the pilot's returns and support costs clear a stated threshold.

DECISION: NO-GO
