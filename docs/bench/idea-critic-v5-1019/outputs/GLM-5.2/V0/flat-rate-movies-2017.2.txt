The proposal's core math does not close: the company pays full ticket price for each film while collecting only ~$10/month. A member seeing two films in a month at a ~$12 average ticket already puts the service underwater on that subscriber, before any overhead. The entire bet rests on unproven revenue streams—data monetization, studio deals, theater partnerships—subsidizing a known, structural per-user loss that scales linearly with the riskiest segment: heavy users who consume the most and pay the same flat fee.

The strongest case for the proposal is that subscription businesses tolerate initial per-unit losses in exchange for scale, network effects, and eventual pricing power. But that defense breaks down here: the marginal cost per user is not just uncertain, it is *uncapped*. There is no ceiling on how many films a heavy user sees, and the flat fee gives the company no lever to limit exposure. Unlike a gym membership where the profitable majority subsidizes the heavy minority, cinema attendance skews toward enthusiasts—the adverse selection problem is severe and the break-even point requires a mix of light users that has no evidence supporting it.

The kill criterion is missing entirely. The proposal names no membership-growth threshold, no average-revenue-per-user target, no monthly cash-burn ceiling, and no date by which the data/deal revenue must materialize before reversing course. Approving an uncapped-loss bet with no pre-committed stop condition is reckless regardless of the owner's cash position.

Theater-chain refusal is not a footnote; it is a potential single-point failure for the entire model, since the company depends on theaters accepting its payment terms at full price.

DECISION: NO-GO
