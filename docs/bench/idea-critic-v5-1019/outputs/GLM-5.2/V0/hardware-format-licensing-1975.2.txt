**Steelman:** Superior picture plus early lead wins the market, so staying proprietary protects margins and control.

**Skeptic:** The proposal assumes quality and lead time outweigh ecosystem size. The named risks — rival licensing broadly, one-hour tapes too short for films or sports, fewer manufacturers meaning fewer pre-recorded titles — each independently threaten adoption. No evidence is offered that picture quality alone drives consumer choice in 1975; "assumed."

**Better-way:** "Do nothing" is keep the format proprietary as stated — the default. A stronger alternative: license broadly now while the early lead exists, accepting lower per-unit margin for market share and a larger pre-recorded catalog. The proposal picks the lower-reversibility path (proprietary lock-in) without pricing the cost of losing the format war.

**Kill-criteria:** No stop condition is stated. A workable one: if rival licensed-player installed base exceeds ours within 12 months, or pre-recorded title count falls below rival's within 18 months, open licensing immediately. The proposal lacks any such trigger, so it cannot detect the failure mode it dismisses.

**Pre-mortem:** Assume it is 1980 and our format lost. Why: we kept one-hour tapes while consumers wanted to record a full movie or football match; we licensed to few partners, so our players stayed expensive and our catalog stayed thin; the rival's many licensees made it the de facto standard. Every one of these causes is visible in the 1975 risk list, and none is answered.

**Verdict:** The proposal acknowledges the exact risks that would kill it and offers no response beyond quality and lead. A format war is won by installed base, content availability and recording length, not by picture sharpness alone. Approving as proposed means betting on a one-way door with no kill criterion and no plan to open licensing if the early lead erodes.

DECISION: NO-GO
