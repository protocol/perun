The strongest version: dense urban demand plus direct control of the delivery fleet lets the company hit one-hour reliably, build a defensible logistics brand, and scale city-by-city before competitors notice.

The sharpest attack: free delivery of a $3 snack costs more in courier time than the snack earns, so every small order is a direct loss; the proposal's own risk list concedes this. The model only works if average order value is high enough to cover the courier cost — but "no minimum order" guarantees the unprofitable small-order segment exists and is the most attractive one to subsidized customers. Direct employment of couriers across many cities multiplies fixed cost and legal exposure precisely as the unit economics stay negative. Nothing in the proposal names the average order value needed, the courier cost per delivery, or the contribution margin required before free delivery stops bleeding cash.

The pre-mortem: the company scales to several cities, subsidized small orders dominate, contribution margin stays negative, the venture money burns through, and the company cannot raise again. The kill criterion — stop expanding when contribution margin per delivery is still negative after, say, six months in the first city — is not stated.

The proposal conflates "we can deliver" with "we can deliver profitably at zero minimum." The cheaper alternative — minimum order threshold or a delivery fee on small orders — directly fixes the unit-economics hole without abandoning the brand. The proposal rejects that by design.

Because the core unit economics are unproven and the expansion plan assumes they will resolve themselves, approving as proposed commits to negative-margin scaling before the first city demonstrates positive contribution. The risks raised are not answered; they are restated.

DECISION: NO-GO
