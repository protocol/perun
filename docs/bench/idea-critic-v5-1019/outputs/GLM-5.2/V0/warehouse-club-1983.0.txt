**Steelman:** Membership fees turn shoppers into stakeholders, creating predictable recurring revenue that subsidizes razor-thin merchandise margins — a structural advantage over retailers who must extract full margin from every transaction.

**Strongest attack survived:** "Shoppers won't pay a fee to shop." This is the existential bet, but it is *testable cheaply* at one location before scaling, and the membership model creates lock-in that pure discounters cannot replicate. The attack fails because the proposal doesn't require committing to expansion before the first warehouse proves the model.

**Why the stated risks don't block approval:**

- **"Shoppers may not pay a fee"** — this is the core hypothesis, not a hidden flaw. It is falsifiable at one location with a few thousand sign-ups.
- **"Competitor has more locations and cash"** — true, but the competitor's scale is its own model; this proposal competes on *structure* (fee-subsidized margins), not on matching footprint.
- **"Thin margins leave no room for errors"** — correct, and exactly why starting with *one* warehouse rather than a national rollout is the right first step.
- **"Large warehouses need big sites and bulk buyers"** — a siting and demand problem, again testable in one market.

**The decisive factor:** The proposal is a **two-way door** with a cheap first step. One warehouse either signs enough members in its first year or it doesn't. The membership revenue is measurable monthly. If sign-ups lag a clear threshold by month six, the model is abandoned before a second location opens. The cost of the test is one warehouse lease and one year of operations — small relative to the structural advantage if the hypothesis holds.

**Kill criterion:** Fewer than X paid memberships by month 6 → do not open a second location. (X = break-even membership count for the first warehouse's fixed costs.)

The risks are real but they are the *known* risks of a testable hypothesis, not reasons to defer a cheap, reversible experiment.

DECISION: GO
