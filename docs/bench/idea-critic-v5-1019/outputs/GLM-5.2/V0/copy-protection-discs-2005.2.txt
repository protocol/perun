The strongest case for this proposal is that copying is a real, measurable revenue leak and a technical barrier deployed at scale could slow it. But the attack that breaks it is simple: the proposal ships hidden software to the computers of paying customers — the exact people whose purchases the company cannot afford to lose.

Four problems make this untenable as proposed:

1. **Consent is not a footnote.** Software that installs silently when a disc is played is, in substance, a rootkit. In 2005, public exposure of this behavior would not be a risk; it would be a certainty, and the reputational damage would dwarf any copying prevented.

2. **The security surface is the product.** Millions of installations of hard-to-remove software create a monoculture target. When researchers find the vulnerability — and they will — the company is liable for every compromised machine.

3. **The math is inverted.** The proposal protects against non-customers (copiers) by degrading the experience of actual customers (buyers). A buyer whose computer is silently modified has every reason to become an ex-customer and a vocal one.

4. **No kill criterion is named.** If the backlash comes, what triggers a recall? The proposal offers no threshold, no date, no pivot. Shipping at this scale without a pre-committed stop condition is reckless.

The cheaper alternative the proposal never considers: invest in legal action against large-scale copiers, develop a legitimate digital storefront, and compete on convenience rather than fight customers with hidden software. The status quo — losing some sales to copying — is costly but survivable. The proposed path risks a consumer-rights scandal, class-action liability, and brand erosion that is not survivable.

DECISION: NO-GO
