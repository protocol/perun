The proposal faces severe, compounding risks that make approval as proposed untenable.

First, the unit economics are deeply strained. Raising roughly $1.75 billion to fund high-budget productions means each piece of content must perform extraordinarily well to justify its cost. Yet the format itself—short episodes designed exclusively for phones—limits the depth of storytelling and audience immersion that typically justifies premium budgets. You are spending as though each show is prestige television while restricting the canvas to bite-sized mobile clips.

Second, the competitive landscape is hostile. Free short-form video already dominates social platforms and commands enormous daily engagement. The proposal asks viewers to pay for something they can get elsewhere at no cost, with the added friction of a new app, a subscription fee, and no television support at launch. The value proposition for the consumer is weak: pay money to watch short, phone-only shows that compete against an endless free supply.

Third, the strategic sequencing is misaligned. Launching in only two countries restricts the addressable market at exactly the moment when massive production spend requires broad distribution to approach breakeven. Simultaneously, excluding television at launch removes a key revenue and discovery channel, further narrowing the path to recoupment.

The veteran film and technology leadership brings relevant experience, but experience does not offset a model where the cost structure, format constraints, and competitive pressures all push in the wrong direction. The core question—whether viewers will pay for premium short-form mobile content—remains unanswered, and the proposal stakes nearly two billion dollars on an unproven assumption.

Approving this as proposed would commit enormous capital to a venture with unclear demand, high fixed costs, limited initial distribution, and formidable free competition. The downside is catastrophic; the upside is speculative.

DECISION: NO-GO
