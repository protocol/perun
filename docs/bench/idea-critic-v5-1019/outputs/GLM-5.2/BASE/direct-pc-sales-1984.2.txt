The proposal targets a market with a clear and growing customer base. The core idea—selling direct to avoid the heavy costs of retail channels and passing those savings to buyers—is financially sound. It removes the middleman, lowers overhead, and allows for competitive pricing against established brands.

The risks raised are real but manageable. The concern that buyers want to try before buying is valid; however, demonstrations at trade shows or word-of-mouth can mitigate this. The lack of a service network is a more serious issue. Without stores, customers need reassurance that support exists. This could be addressed through mail-in repair, phone support, or partnerships with local technicians. The proposal should include a basic plan for after-sales service.

Supplier credit is a significant hurdle. As a new company without a track record, securing parts on credit will be difficult. The business must rely on cash flow from sales and possibly personal investment to fund initial inventory. This limits early scale but also keeps the company lean.

The established brands have large channels and strong recognition. Yet their overhead and retail markups create an opening for a low-cost direct seller. The student’s hands-on experience assembling computers is a key asset; it means the founder understands the product and can control quality and costs.

Overall, the business model is viable. The direct-to-customer approach reduces costs and prices. The main gaps are service and supplier credit. The proposal should be revised to include a clear after-sales support plan and a strategy for managing inventory with limited credit. The founder’s practical experience and the market’s growth potential justify further development of the plan.

DECISION: NO-GO
