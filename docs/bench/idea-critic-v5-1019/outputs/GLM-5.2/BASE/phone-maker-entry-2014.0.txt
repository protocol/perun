The proposal asks us to enter the most competitive hardware category with a device that is, at best, a clever companion to our store. The core problem is that a phone is not a shopping peripheral; it is the screen people live on. If buyers use their phone for messaging, maps, video and social apps, our device loses immediately because the leading phones have the app ecosystem and we do not. A premium price invites direct comparison to those leaders, and we cannot win that comparison on apps or on carrier availability with a single-carrier launch.

The 3D head-tracking effect is a novelty, not a purchase driver. The camera feature that identifies products for purchase is more interesting, but it assumes customers want a phone built around buying from us. That is a retailer’s hope, not a consumer need. Even the included year of membership is a retention tactic, not a reason to switch phones.

We also face a distribution trap. A single carrier limits reach and reinforces that this is a niche device. Combined with a premium price, it asks customers to pay top dollar for fewer apps, limited availability, and features that may not matter in daily use.

This is not a case of a strong product with fixable go-to-market issues. The product itself is mispositioned: it competes in a category where the dominant players win through ecosystem breadth and habitual app use, while we offer shopping integration and visual effects. The risks raised are not peripheral concerns; they are the strategy. Approving now would commit us to a premium launch against entrenched leaders, through a narrow channel, with no evidence that our differentiators drive phone purchases.

DECISION: NO-GO
