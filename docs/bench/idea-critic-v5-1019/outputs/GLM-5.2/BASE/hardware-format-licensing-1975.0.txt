The proposal underestimates the decisive factors in a consumer format war: ecosystem size, content availability, and recording duration. Picture quality and early lead are real but insufficient when the rival is pursuing broad licensing.

A one-hour tape is a severe functional limitation. Consumers buying recorders expect to capture full films and sports events. If the rival format offers longer recording times, it will align better with mainstream use cases and win consumer preference, even at slightly lower quality.

The proprietary, restricted licensing strategy creates a fatal vulnerability. Fewer manufacturers means fewer players on shelves, higher retail prices, slower adoption, and less incentive for content owners to release titles. The rival’s open licensing will flood the market with compatible hardware, drive prices down, and attract pre-recorded content. Once that cycle begins, it becomes self-reinforcing. Network effects, not technical superiority, determine which format becomes the standard.

The risks raised are not peripheral concerns; they are the central dynamics of this market. The proposal acknowledges these threats but offers no mechanism to counter them. Relying on picture quality ignores that consumers tolerated lower quality in other consumer formats when convenience and content availability were superior. Relying on early lead ignores that a lead only matters if it converts into an insurmountable installed base before the rival gains traction.

The proposal should be revised to open licensing broadly, accelerate installed-base growth, and address the recording-duration gap through faster tape speed or extended-length cassettes. Without those changes, the format risks being isolated in a premium niche while the rival achieves the scale necessary to become the de facto standard.

DECISION: NO-GO
