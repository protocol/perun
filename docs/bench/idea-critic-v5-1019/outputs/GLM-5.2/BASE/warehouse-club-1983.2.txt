The proposal faces a direct, unavoidable problem: it copies a format a larger competitor already operates. If the model works, the established rival has more locations, more cash, and earlier scale to defend or expand. If the model fails, the thin margins and upfront site costs make the losses severe.

The membership fee is the only structural advantage proposed, but it is double-edged. It can fund low prices and create recurring revenue, but it also raises the barrier to trial. Shoppers have no reason to pay to enter unless the savings are obvious and immediate. That requires a limited range, high turnover, and disciplined buying, none of which is guaranteed in a launch against an incumbent who already has supplier leverage.

The risks are not speculative; they are foundational. Big warehouses need big sites, and bulk buying needs customers willing and able to purchase in volume. In an unproven market, committing to multiple locations before validating membership uptake, basket size, and renewal rates would expose capital without evidence of repeat behavior. The competitor's existing footprint means shoppers already have a no-frills, bulk, membership option; this proposal does not articulate why they would switch or pay twice.

The concept has merit if execution is tight and expansion is cautious. But as proposed, opening more warehouses now, against a stronger incumbent, with thin margins and unproven willingness to pay, the downside is too large relative to the evidence. The right path is to prove the model in one or a few locations first: measure sign-ups, renewals, margins, and traffic before scaling. Approving broad expansion now would be a bet on format alone, and the format is not unique.

DECISION: NO-GO
