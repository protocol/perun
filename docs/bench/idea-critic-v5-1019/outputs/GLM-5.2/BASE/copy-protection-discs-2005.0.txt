The proposal asks us to secretly install software on millions of customer computers—our best customers, the people who actually buy discs—to stop copying by people who largely aren't buying discs.

Every risk raised is not speculative; each is near-certain. Software that installs without clear consent is, by definition, intrusive and will be perceived as such. Software that is hard to remove is a liability the moment a customer discovers it. Security researchers will find weaknesses—they always do—and when they do, we will have installed a vulnerability onto millions of machines. Customers will object to being treated as thieves, because they will have been treated as thieves.

The fundamental problem is that we are proposed to compromise the trust of paying customers to address a threat from non-customers. Even if the software works perfectly—preventing all ripping—it does nothing about peer-to-peer sharing of tracks obtained elsewhere. And it will not work perfectly; determined copiers will bypass it within days, rendering the entire exercise both harmful and futile.

The downside is asymmetric and severe. We gain a marginal, temporary friction on copying. We lose customer goodwill, invite class-action exposure, and hand critics a concrete example of a corporation secretly altering personal computers. The reputational damage from being caught secretly installing hard-to-remove software on millions of machines will vastly exceed any revenue protected.

This proposal treats our customers' computers as our infrastructure. They are not. We cannot install persistent, hidden software on machines we do not own, without consent, and then claim we were protecting content. The ethical, legal, and commercial risks all point the same direction: away from this approach.

DECISION: NO-GO
