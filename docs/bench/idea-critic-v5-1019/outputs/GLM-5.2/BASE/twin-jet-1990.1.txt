The proposal asks us to commit to a very large-cost, all-new aircraft program on the strength of a single airline's order for 34 units. That is insufficient demand validation for the capital at stake, especially when eight airlines were involved in design discussions but only one has committed. The gap between design participation and firm orders is a serious commercial risk.

The overwater twin-engine approval is not yet granted. This is not a minor regulatory footnote; it is a core assumption of the aircraft's value proposition. If approval is delayed, restricted, or denied, the aircraft's range and route economics could be compromised, undermining the business case. Launching before this regulatory pathway is clear exposes the program to a binary, uncontrollable risk.

Designing with airlines offers valuable input but also creates scope creep. Multiple airlines will have different requirements, and accommodating them can drive weight, cost, and schedule penalties. With development cost already described as large, there is little margin for the additional cost growth that airline-driven design changes could produce.

The competitive context heightens the urgency but also the danger. A rival building new models means market timing matters, but launching prematurely on weak order volume could lock us into a program that competitors undercut or out-time. The right response is not to rush but to secure stronger commitments, clarify the regulatory path, and control design inputs before committing major capital.

The risks are not fatal, but they are unresolved and material. Approving now would mean accepting large cost exposure, regulatory uncertainty, and thin order coverage simultaneously. That is not a defensible position for an executive team responsible for capital allocation and program risk management.

DECISION: NO-GO
