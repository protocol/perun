The proposal asks us to stake our last available resources on a bet where our core assumption—that more cores will translate to competitive desktop performance—is directly contradicted by one of the risks we identified: software may not use extra cores.

This is the fatal flaw. Our entire value proposition is offering "many more cores" at lower prices. However, desktop workloads in 2017 are heavily single-threaded or lightly threaded. If the software ecosystem cannot effectively utilize our core count advantage, our chips will deliver inferior real-world performance despite looking superior on paper. We would be competing on a metric that does not translate to user value.

The financial constraint seals the decision. We have "no room for a second miss," yet this proposal carries multiple unmitigated risks that could each independently cause failure. The rival can outspend us, out-price us, and out-engineer us. Our last architecture attempt underperformed, and there is no evidence presented that this new architecture will break that pattern. Launching now means betting the company's survival on unproven silicon against a dominant competitor with vastly more resources.

A more prudent path would be to validate the architecture in a lower-risk market segment first, build developer partnerships to ensure software optimization, and establish a financial reserve before attempting a full-scale desktop assault. We need a differentiated strategy that doesn't rely on our competitor's cooperation or software developers' adoption timeline.

DECISION: NO-GO
