This proposal forces a single interface onto all hardware configurations, ignoring the fundamental difference between touch and keyboard/mouse inputs. The risks raised are not minor edge cases; they represent the core revenue base. Corporate users generate significant, stable licensing revenue. Alienating them with a confusing, touch-first interface on non-touch hardware threatens immediate cash flow. High training and support costs make this transition expensive for businesses, many of which will simply delay upgrades or seek alternatives.

The proposal assumes that a unified interface across tablets and PCs is inherently valuable. However, users do not value uniformity for its own sake; they value efficiency and familiarity in their existing workflows. Removing the traditional start menu and offering no easy way back is an aggressive move that disregards user agency and the massive installed base of non-touch devices. It forces a binary choice: adapt or leave.

Furthermore, the success of a tablet app store depends on developer adoption. If desktop and corporate users reject the new interface, developers will see a fragmented, unattractive market. The app store will languish without applications, undermining the entire strategy. The proposal does not adequately address how to bootstrap the developer ecosystem, especially when the primary user base—desktop and corporate—may actively dislike the new paradigm.

The strategic goal of countering tablet market share gains is sound, but the execution proposed is reckless. It bets the entire PC user experience on an unproven touch-first paradigm, risking the dominant market position for uncertain gains in the tablet space. A more measured approach would offer the new interface as an option alongside the traditional desktop, allowing gradual adoption and preserving the loyalty of the core user base. This proposal asks us to sacrifice our strengths—desktop familiarity and corporate lock-in—to chase a market where we are already behind.

DECISION: NO-GO
