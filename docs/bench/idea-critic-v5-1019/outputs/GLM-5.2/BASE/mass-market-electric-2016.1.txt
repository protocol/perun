The proposal demands a leap from artisanal, low-volume production to mass manufacturing within roughly a year. That is not a linear scale-up; it is a change of industrial paradigm. The company’s entire operational DNA is built around making a handful of expensive cars. Asking it to suddenly produce at high volumes assumes that engineering talent alone can replace decades of manufacturing experience. It cannot.

The battery supply chain poses a similarly severe risk. Scaling cell production to match a mass-market sedan requires committed, multi-year offtake agreements with suppliers, new gigafactory capacity, and validated chemistries at scale. None of these can be reliably achieved in the proposed timeline. A bottleneck here means the entire program stalls while refundable deposits sit as liabilities.

On deposits: they are a useful signal of demand, but they are not sales. They are refundable. If production slips, if the price must rise, or if early reviews disappoint, depositors can withdraw en masse. The company would then be holding an expensive, half-built factory program with no guaranteed revenue floor.

On margin: a cheaper car at low volume and high cost is a recipe for sustained losses. The company would need to hit high-volume production quickly to spread fixed costs and reach a break-even point. Given the lack of manufacturing experience, that ramp will be slower and costlier than projected. Capital requirements will balloon.

The strategic logic—move downmarket, secure deposits, force the company to scale—is bold. But the execution risks are not merely elevated; they are existential. The timeline is too compressed, the supply chain too immature, and the margin for error too thin to approve as proposed.

DECISION: NO-GO
