The core issue is trust. The company’s own estimate—once in many thousands of years—may be technically defensible, but it is irrelevant if customers perceive the firm as evasive.

The proposal to replace chips only for users who can prove impact creates a trap. Floating-point errors are subtle and often non-deterministic. Most affected users cannot demonstrate harm, because the wrong answers look plausible. Asking them to prove it shifts the burden onto the customer and signals that the company values avoiding replacement costs over product integrity. That stance is likely to escalate resentment, not calm it.

The financial concern is real: replacing every chip would be very expensive. But the cost of appearing to hide a defect is higher. Once buyers believe the company cannot be trusted to acknowledge a flaw, they will factor that distrust into every future purchase decision. The reputational damage would outlast any short-term savings.

A better path is to acknowledge the flaw openly, state the practical risk honestly, and offer replacement chips to any user who requests one—without requiring proof of impact. This removes the adversarial test, demonstrates accountability, and lets the company control the narrative rather than let widespread discussion define it.

The proposal as written asks customers to trust a company that is simultaneously unwilling to make them whole unconditionally. That contradiction is unsustainable. Approving it now would lock the company into a defensive posture during active public scrutiny.

The responsible choice is to reject the conditional-replacement proposal and instead commit to a no-questions-asked replacement program, paired with transparent technical disclosure.

DECISION: NO-GO
