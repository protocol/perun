The proposal is rejected on its own facts. The core mechanism—discs that expire after 48 hours and require payment to watch again—directly contradicts the established consumer expectation that a purchased disc is owned permanently. The proposal itself acknowledges that customers dislike paying for repeated viewing, yet the entire revenue model depends on exactly that behavior.

The hardware economics are equally fatal. The special players cost more than standard players, and the discs cannot be played on standard players. This means the retailer is asking customers to pay a premium for a device that plays a restricted format, while standard players play standard discs without expiration. No rational consumer adopts the inferior, more expensive option when the better, cheaper option already exists in the market.

The partnership risk compounds every other problem. Studios and electronics makers must agree to support a new format. But studios have no incentive to back a format that sells discs for a few dollars when they can sell standard discs at full price. Electronics makers have no incentive to produce special players when standard players already have market traction. Without both groups on board, the format has no content and no hardware—yet the proposal offers no evidence that either group is willing, let alone committed.

The proposal asks the executive team to accept a business model where every assumption must break the customer's way: customers must accept repeated payments, pay more for special players, and abandon a standard format they already use. Every single risk raised is structural, not operational. These are not problems that better execution can solve; they are flaws in the fundamental design of the offering.

The market conditions described—a new disc format, an existing rental market, standard players already available—do not create an opening for a more expensive, more restrictive alternative. The proposal fails on its own terms.

DECISION: NO-GO
