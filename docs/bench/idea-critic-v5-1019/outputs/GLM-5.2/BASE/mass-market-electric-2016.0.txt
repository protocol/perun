The proposal asks us to commit to an unprecedented leap in production volume within a single year, despite no demonstrated capability in mass manufacturing. That alone is a critical operational risk: scaling from hand-built, low-volume vehicles to tens of thousands requires factory build-out, workforce training, supplier qualification, and quality control systems that cannot realistically stand up in the proposed timeframe.

The battery supply risk compounds this. A sharp scale-up in cells demands long-lead contracts with chemical and cell suppliers who themselves face capacity constraints. If battery supply lags, production stalls; if it surges ahead of our assembly readiness, we carry heavy inventory costs.

Refundable deposits are not committed revenue. They can create an illusion of demand and lock us into a public timeline, but buyers can withdraw, especially if delays erode confidence. Relying on deposits to fund capital expansion is fragile.

The margin issue is severe. A much lower price point, combined with our lack of scale efficiencies, means each vehicle could be produced at a loss. Without a credible cost-reduction path, this proposal risks burning capital faster than it generates value.

The strategic intent—moving toward affordability and scale—is sound, but the execution plan as presented is not. The timeline is too aggressive, the financial assumptions too thin, and the operational risks too high to approve now.

DECISION: NO-GO
