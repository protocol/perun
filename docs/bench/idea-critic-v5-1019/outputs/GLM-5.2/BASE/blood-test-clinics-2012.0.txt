The proposal asks us to commit 350 million dollars to deploy an unvalidated medical technology across hundreds of stores, granting exclusivity to a company that refuses to share its methods.

Four critical facts make this proposal untenable as presented.

First, the technology has not been shown in published studies. For a medical diagnostic device, this is a fundamental disqualifier. We would be investing a third of a billion dollars into a product whose core claims have never been independently verified. The absence of peer-reviewed evidence means we cannot assess accuracy, reliability, or clinical utility.

Second, the company will not share its methods. This means we cannot conduct our own technical due diligence. We are being asked to trust a young company's assertions without any ability to verify the underlying science. This opacity is incompatible with a 350 million dollar commitment, particularly in healthcare where the stakes involve patient safety.

Third, our chain has no medical expertise. Operating clinical blood-testing services requires regulatory compliance, quality control, medical oversight, and liability management. We lack the infrastructure, personnel, and institutional knowledge to safely deploy or oversee this offering at scale.

Fourth, regulators may later question the tests. This risk is not speculative but near-certain given that the technology is unvalidated and the company is secretive. Regulatory intervention could halt operations, force costly remediation, or expose us to legal liability after we have already built out hundreds of clinics.

The combination of unproven science, corporate secrecy, our own lack of medical capability, and foreseeable regulatory trouble creates an unacceptable risk profile. The potential upside of store traffic and exclusivity does not offset the possibility that the technology simply does not work as claimed, or that patients could be harmed by inaccurate results.

This proposal requires published validation, full technical transparency, a credible regulatory pathway, and a partnership structure addressing our lack of medical infrastructure before any investment is warranted.

DECISION: NO-GO
