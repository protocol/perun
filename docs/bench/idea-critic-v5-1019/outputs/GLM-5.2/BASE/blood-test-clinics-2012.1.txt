The proposal asks us to commit 350 million dollars to install clinics offering unvalidated blood tests in hundreds of stores. The financial exposure is massive, but the strategic and reputational risks are even more severe.

The core product has no published studies demonstrating that it works. The company refuses to share its methods, meaning we cannot independently verify the technology before investing. We are being asked to take the founder’s word that the device can do what is claimed, and to pay a premium for exclusivity on a product we cannot evaluate.

We have no medical expertise. Operating clinical laboratories is not an extension of retail; it is a highly regulated, specialized field. If the tests are inaccurate, the harm is direct: patients could receive false diagnoses, undergo unnecessary treatments, or miss critical interventions. The liability for a supermarket chain entering this space without its own medical oversight is enormous.

Regulators questioning the tests later is not a minor risk; it is a likely operational disruption. If regulators intervene, the clinics could be forced to halt operations, leaving us with sunk costs and damaged credibility. The proposal offers no plan for regulatory engagement or compliance.

The potential upside—affordable, convenient diagnostics—is attractive and aligns with consumer trends. But the path to realizing that upside is blocked by fundamental gaps: no proof of efficacy, no transparency, no internal expertise, and regulatory uncertainty. Investing 350 million dollars under these conditions would be irresponsible.

We should not proceed as proposed.

DECISION: NO-GO
