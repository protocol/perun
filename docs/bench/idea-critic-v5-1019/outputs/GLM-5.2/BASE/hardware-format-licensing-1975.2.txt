The proposal’s core strategy is self-defeating given the stated risks. By keeping the format proprietary and limiting partners, the company is deliberately choosing the exact path that makes the rival’s strategy potent. The rival is licensing broadly, which will accelerate installed base, drive down prices through scale, and attract pre-recorded content. Meeting that with a closed, narrowly licensed format relies on picture quality and early lead to carry the day.

The one-hour tape limitation is a fatal flaw in this configuration. A home recording device that cannot capture a full film or sports event in one sitting has a structural disadvantage in its primary use case. The proposal does not address this; it merely hopes quality and timing compensate. They will not, because the rival’s longer tapes solve a real consumer problem while this format creates one.

Fewer manufacturers also means fewer pre-recorded titles, which compounds the risk. A format lives or dies on content availability. If the rival format achieves broader distribution and richer title supply, the early lead evaporates quickly. Network effects are asymmetric here: the rival is positioning to win them, while this proposal is positioning to be isolated by them.

The downside scenario is not merely losing market share; it is losing the format war entirely. The company would have invested in manufacturing, marketing, and content relationships for a format that becomes obsolete, with no ecosystem to fall back on. The cost of being wrong here is total, and the proposal offers no hedge against it.

Approving as proposed means accepting a high probability of strategic defeat against a competitor with a more open, more practical, and more scalable approach. The risks are not marginal; they are existential to the product line. The proposal needs fundamental revision before any commitment.

DECISION: NO-GO
