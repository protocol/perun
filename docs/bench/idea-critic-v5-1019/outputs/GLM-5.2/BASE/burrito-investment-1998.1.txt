The proposal is sound. The structure directly mitigates the primary risks while preserving upside.

The minority stake with an option to increase investment is the core advantage. It caps downside at a small, affordable amount while retaining the ability to scale funding if the concept proves viable. This is crucial because the concerns about scalability, labor intensity, and operational speed are valid but premature. A 16-unit chain is a proven local concept, not a hypothesis. The question is whether it can expand nationally, not whether the product works. The option-to-invest structure allows the giant to answer that question with minimal capital exposure.

Letting the chain run independently resolves the operational friction. The giant's infrastructure is built for low-skill, high-speed, low-cost execution. Forcing this new chain into that mold would destroy its value proposition. Independent operation preserves the chain's identity and culture, which are its actual assets. The giant provides capital and oversight; the chain maintains its operational model. This also addresses the management attention risk: the giant does not need to embed its own operators, so its core business is not drained of talent.

The price point being above the giant's core menu is an asset, not a liability. It accesses a different customer segment and daypart, providing portfolio diversification rather than internal competition. The giant's customers trade up; the chain's customers are distinct.

The risks are real but manageable through the proposed structure. The investment is small relative to the giant's resources, the optionality is valuable, and the independent operating model prevents cultural and operational clash. The potential return is significant: entry into a high-growth, premium segment with limited downside.

DECISION: GO
