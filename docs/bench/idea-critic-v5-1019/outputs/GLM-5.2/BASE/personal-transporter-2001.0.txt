The proposal asks us to launch a novel transportation device at $5,000, targeting urban consumers as a replacement for short car trips and walking. The risks raised are not merely operational hurdles—they are fundamental barriers to the value proposition.

**Price vs. alternatives:** At $5,000, this device costs roughly ten times a quality bicycle, several times a motorized scooter, and competes with a used car. The target use case—short trips—means it must compete with walking (free) and cycling (cheap). The price-to-value ratio is difficult to justify for the mass market.

**Learning curve:** Walking and cycling, once learned, require minimal conscious effort. This device demands that users invest time to master self-balancing, which introduces friction at the point of adoption and raises liability concerns during the learning period.

**Regulatory uncertainty:** There is no clear path to legal operation. If sidewalks are off-limits (reasonable, given speed and safety concerns for pedestrians) and roads are inhospitable (reasonable, given the device's speed relative to cars and lack of protection), then there may be no legal space for it. Launching without regulatory clarity invites bans that could permanently damage the product's reputation.

**Cargo and passenger limitations:** The device cannot carry groceries, a child, or a second person. For short trips, this matters—people run errands. A device that replaces walking for a solo trip but cannot handle the multipurpose demands of urban life has a narrow utility window.

Taken together, these risks suggest the product is not ready for a wide consumer launch at the proposed price. The regulatory question alone warrants a hold. A narrower pilot—targeted commercial users, controlled environments, or early adopters at a lower price point—would provide the learning needed to refine the model. As proposed, the downside is too high and the assumptions too untested.

DECISION: NO-GO
