The concerns raised are real but manageable, and the upside is substantial.

On the "try before you buy" objection: computers in 1984 are becoming standardized business tools, not novelty items. Buyers increasingly care about specifications, price, and reliability rather than showroom experience. Direct ordering works for catalogs and mail-order; it can work here.

On established competitors: their large sales channels and retail networks are actually their cost burden. Brand-name PCs carry significant markups covering distribution, dealer margins, and overhead. Selling direct creates a structural cost advantage that lets the student undercut branded prices while preserving margin. The competitors' size also makes them slow to adapt.

On service networks: this is the weakest point in the proposal. A no-store model needs a credible answer for repairs, warranty claims, and technical support. The proposal does not address this. However, it is solvable: mail-in repair, third-party service partnerships, and phone support are all viable. This is a revision item, not a fatal flaw.

On supplier credit: a start-up will struggle to get favorable credit terms, but the proposal explicitly plans to grow from revenue rather than debt. Bootstrapping is slow but safe. Component suppliers in the IBM-compatible ecosystem are numerous; dependency risk is low.

The core logic is sound. Direct-to-customer distribution eliminates retail markup. The IBM-compatible standard means parts are interchangeable and widely available. The student already operates the business, proving the model works at small scale. Incorporation limits personal liability and enables future investment.

The main gap is the service plan. The executive team should require a concrete service and support strategy before full approval. But the business model, cost structure, and growth approach are fundamentally attractive.

DECISION: NO-GO
