The proposal hinges on a single unverified claim: that exposure is roughly once in many thousands of years for a typical user. That is an internal estimate from the party with the most to lose, and it is being advanced at the very moment the problem is already widely discussed. Once public scrutiny has begun, a narrow, evidence-gated remedy looks defensive rather than responsible.

The gating mechanism is also impractical. Users cannot easily show they are affected, so the remedy is effectively inaccessible to most buyers. That converts a rare technical defect into a visible fairness problem: customers are asked to prove harm against a company that has not independently proven the defect's limits.

The cost of full replacement is described as very large, but the proposal does not weigh that against the likely larger cost of sustained reputational damage if the company is perceived as minimizing or concealing a defect. In a widely discussed situation, the dominant risk is not the replacement expense itself; it is the loss of trust and the possibility that external parties will define the company's responsiveness for it.

The company should not proceed with the proposal as written. It needs an independent validation of the error rate, a remedy path that does not require customers to prove harm, and a public posture that treats the defect as a credibility test rather than a claims-management problem.

DECISION: NO-GO
