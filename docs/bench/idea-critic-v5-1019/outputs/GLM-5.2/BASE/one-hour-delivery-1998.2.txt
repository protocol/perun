The economics are broken. Free delivery on a $4 coffee with no minimum means each order loses money. The proposal acknowledges this risk but offers no mechanism to cover it. Subscription fees, minimums, or delivery charges are all absent.

Direct employment of delivery staff creates fixed costs that scale badly. Every new city requires a fleet, a payroll, and management overhead before a single order arrives. The model assumes density and volume that take years to build, if they materialize at all.

The expansion plan compounds the problem. Rapid city-by-city rollout means replicating an unproven, likely money-losing operation across markets with different logistics, labor costs, and demand patterns. There is no evidence the unit economics work anywhere before committing to everywhere.

The customer-behavior risk is the most damaging. If users only order when delivery is free, the service becomes a perpetual subsidy machine with no path to profitability. Raising prices later risks losing the customer base entirely. The proposal offers no answer to this trap.

The core question is whether one-hour delivery of low-margin convenience items can ever cover its costs at zero delivery fees. Nothing in this proposal suggests it can. The margins on snacks, books, and coffee are thin. The delivery cost per order in early-stage cities will be high. The gap between revenue and cost per order is large and structural.

A viable version of this idea would need: a delivery fee or minimum order threshold, a subscription model with proven retention, contractor or partner-based logistics to reduce fixed costs, and a single-city proof of concept demonstrating positive contribution margin before any expansion. This proposal has none of those elements.

Approving this means funding rapid expansion of a model that likely loses money on every order, with no clear path to changing that. The risks raised are not marginal concerns; they are foundational flaws in the unit economics.

DECISION: NO-GO
