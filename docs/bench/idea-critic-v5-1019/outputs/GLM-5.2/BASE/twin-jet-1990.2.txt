The proposal presents severe risks that outweigh the single-airline commitment. Launching an all-new wide-body on the strength of one 34-aircraft order exposes the company to catastrophic financial and operational risk.

The central problem is regulatory uncertainty. A twin-engine jet on long overwater routes requires aviation authority approval that does not yet exist. Committing to a very large development cost before this approval is secured is reckless. If the regulatory framework does not evolve as hoped, the aircraft's fundamental value proposition collapses, leaving the company with a massive sunk investment and no viable market.

The order base is dangerously thin. One airline ordering 34 aircraft cannot amortize the development cost of an all-new program. While eight airlines participated in the design process, participation is not commitment. Designing with multiple airlines introduces change risk and schedule delays at a moment when a rival is already building new models. The competitive window could close before this aircraft reaches certification, especially given the regulatory dependency.

The combination of unproven regulatory assumptions, inadequate firm orders, high development cost, design-by-committee risk, and an active competitor makes approval premature. The company should first secure regulatory clarity, convert airline participation into firm commitments from multiple carriers, and lock down the design scope before committing capital.

DECISION: NO-GO
