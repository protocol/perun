The proposal faces real obstacles: modest category sales, phone dependency, one-day battery life, and unproven wrist-screen demand. Yet none of these are fatal, and several cut in the proposal's favor.

The "earlier smartwatches sold poorly" argument is the weakest objection. Those products came from makers with weak design, fragmented software, and limited retail reach. A company that has already mastered phones, computers, operating systems, and app distribution has a different foundation. The fact that no one has succeeded yet does not mean no one can; it means the category is still waiting for the right execution.

Phone dependency is a genuine limitation but not a dealbreaker in 2015. Most target buyers already own the maker's phone. The watch does not need to replace the phone; it needs to extend it with quick glances, notifications, and health tracking. That is a narrow but real value proposition, especially for fitness-minded users.

One-day battery life is a constraint, but it is a known one. If the device charges nightly alongside the phone, the friction is manageable. Waiting for multi-day battery life could mean missing the window entirely.

The "second screen" objection is the most serious. If buyers cannot articulate why they need a watch, adoption will be slow regardless of execution. But this is precisely why the multi-material, multi-price-level strategy matters: it creates an entry point for enthusiasts and early adopters without requiring mass-market acceptance on day one.

The proposal is not risk-free. But the category is early, the maker has the ecosystem to make the watch useful, and the pricing strategy allows for gradual market education. The downside of waiting is that competitors gain a head start and the maker cedes ground in health and wearable computing. The upside of acting now, even imperfectly, outweighs the cost of caution.
