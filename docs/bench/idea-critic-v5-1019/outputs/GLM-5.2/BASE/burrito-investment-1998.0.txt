The proposal offers optionality with limited downside. A minority stake caps financial exposure, while the right to invest more later captures upside if the chain scales. Letting the chain run independently mitigates operational risk: its made-to-order model, higher price point, and need for skilled workers are fundamentally different from the giant's core business. Attempting integration would create friction without clear benefit; independence preserves the chain's identity while giving the giant a learning platform in a higher-tier segment.

The risks raised are real but manageable. The chain is tiny relative to the giant, so even total failure is immaterial to the balance sheet. Scalability is uncertain, but that is precisely why a minority stake with follow-on rights is the right structure: the giant learns whether the concept works before committing significant capital. The operational differences—slower service, skilled labor, higher prices—are reasons to keep the chain at arm's length rather than reasons to walk away. Management attention is a concern, but a passive minority investment with an independent operator demands far less attention than an acquisition or joint venture would.

The strategic logic is sound. The giant gains exposure to a growing segment without disrupting its core model. If burritos made to order resonate with customers at a premium price, the giant has a pathway to scale through its follow-on right. If they do not, the loss is bounded. The alternative—waiting and watching—costs the giant its first-mover advantage and risks a competitor acquiring the chain if it proves successful.

The structure aligns risk and reward appropriately. The giant is not betting its future; it is buying an option on a category that may matter. The downside is finite, the upside is meaningful, and the operational firewall protects the core business.

DECISION: GO
