The proposal asks us to burn cash on an unproven bet. The core economics do not work: paying full ticket price while charging a low flat fee means every active subscriber loses money, and power users will make losses worse. The promised rescue revenue is speculative. Data monetization has no demonstrated market here, and studio or theater deals are not contracted—indeed, theaters may resist, since the service disrupts their pricing and attendance patterns.

A wealthy owner can subsidize growth, but subsidy is not a business model. Rapid membership growth under a broken unit-economics model would scale losses, not value. Even if the owner can absorb early losses, the path to profitability depends on counterparties—studios and theaters—cooperating on terms favorable to the service. Those parties have their own margins and incentives, and no evidence is given that they will grant discounts, revenue shares, or data access sufficient to close the gap.

The risk is asymmetric. If the new revenue streams fail to materialize, the company is left with a large subscriber base expecting cheap access, mounting ticket liabilities, and no viable cost cap. Shutting down or raising prices later would trigger churn and reputational damage. The downside is fast and structural; the upside is slow and contingent.

This is not a case of modest uncertainty around a sound model. The model itself assumes that volume will solve a per-unit loss, while the very volume that grows revenue also grows cost at a higher rate. Without firm commitments from theaters or studios, a tested data-revenue stream, or a hard cap on usage, approving this as proposed would amount to funding a cash burn with no proven exit.

DECISION: NO-GO
