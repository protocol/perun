This proposal asks us to deliberately ship a product we know is inferior, remove a beloved solution, and hand our competitors a strategic weapon—all to achieve "more control."

The core problem is the value proposition: users lose functionality they rely on, and we gain nothing they can perceive. We are trading a proven, data-rich experience for an unproven one, built on a patchwork of providers, with acknowledged gaps in place and business data. This is not a controlled migration; it is a forced substitution during a peak sales period.

The risks are not mere possibilities; they are stated certainties. We *will* have less data. Information *will* be wrong. Users *will* be annoyed. Removing the rival product by default does not eliminate that rival; it simply creates an opening for our competitors to position themselves as the premium, reliable alternative. We would be creating a marketing crisis for ourselves at the exact moment we need to drive upgrades.

Control is a valid strategic goal, but it cannot be achieved by holding users hostage to an incomplete product. The proposal conflates *default* status with *readiness*. A mapping application is only as good as its data accuracy and coverage. Shipping with known deficiencies in core functionality erodes brand trust, which is far more expensive than the cost of delaying this transition.

A viable path forward would involve a parallel strategy: introduce our mapping app as an option while maintaining the established solution as the default, gather real-world usage data to improve accuracy, and only then transition to default status once parity is demonstrably achieved. The current proposal sacrifices user experience and competitive positioning for an arbitrary timeline.

DECISION: NO-GO
