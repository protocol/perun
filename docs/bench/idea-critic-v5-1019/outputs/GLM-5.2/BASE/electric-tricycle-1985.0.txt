The core concept is commercially compelling: a sub-£400, licence-exempt electric tricycle taps into a genuine gap in the market for affordable personal mobility. The involvement of a reputable appliance manufacturer provides crucial credibility and production capacity. The pedal-assist feature and low running costs are strong selling points.

However, the go-to-market strategy as proposed contains fatal flaws that warrant holding approval.

The primary concern is the channel strategy. Selling a novel, unproven vehicle by mail order in large numbers immediately is overly aggressive. Consumers will be reluctant to purchase a relatively complex piece of hardware sight unseen, especially given the known limitations. The lack of weather protection and low speed make this a fair-weather, short-distance vehicle. Positioning it as a mass-market commuter solution will lead to customer dissatisfaction and high return rates.

The safety objections are significant but manageable. A low-slung vehicle at 15 mph among traffic is a legitimate concern. However, this can be mitigated through targeted marketing that emphasizes its use on cycle paths, in pedestrianized zones, or for localized neighborhood travel rather than as a primary road vehicle.

Before approval, the proposal should be revised to include a phased rollout. A high-profile launch event is sound, but initial sales should be routed through established retail channels where customers can physically interact with the product. Mail order can be introduced once the brand is established and consumer confidence is built. The optional extras strategy is sensible and should be retained to keep the entry price point low.

The concept is strong, but the immediate large-scale mail order launch is too risky given the product's inherent limitations and the potential for safety-related backlash.

DECISION: NO-GO
