The proposal asks us to commit 350 million dollars to install clinics in hundreds of stores based on technology that has never been validated in published studies. That is the single most important fact before us, and it is disqualifying on its own.

We are a supermarket chain. We have no medical expertise. We are being asked to place clinical blood-testing services into our stores, under our brand, using a device whose accuracy is unproven and whose methods we are not allowed to see. If the tests are wrong, the harm falls on real patients and on us. We would have no internal ability to assess the technology, and the company has refused to let anyone outside assess it either.

The exclusivity term does not protect us here. It ties us to a single unproven vendor and blocks us from pursuing safer, better-validated alternatives if this one fails. We would be paying a premium to take on concentrated risk we cannot evaluate or mitigate.

The regulatory risk is not a future footnote. It is a present, active condition. If regulators later question the tests, we could face forced closures, public scrutiny, liability, and stranded investment across hundreds of locations. That is not a manageable risk for a company with no healthcare infrastructure.

Even setting aside safety, the commercial logic is weak. We would be investing 350 million dollars into a category outside our core competency, dependent on a partner that will not share its methods, with no independent proof the product works. The upside is speculative; the downside is operational, reputational, legal, and potentially catastrophic.

This is not a close call. The capital is too large, the validation is too thin, the transparency is absent, and the fit with our capabilities is nonexistent. We should not approve this proposal as presented.

DECISION: NO-GO
