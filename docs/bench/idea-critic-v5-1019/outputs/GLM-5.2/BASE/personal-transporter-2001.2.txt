The proposal faces fundamental barriers that prevent approval as presented.

First, the price point of roughly $5,000 places this device far beyond the cost of every alternative it claims to replace. A serviceable bicycle costs a fraction of that. A used car, while more expensive, offers cargo capacity, passenger seating, weather protection, and established legal status. Electric scooters, which would soon emerge as a competing option, are dramatically cheaper. At this price, the addressable market narrows to affluent early adopters, which cannot support the proposed scale of wide urban sales.

Second, the learning curve is a serious adoption barrier. Walking requires no instruction. Bicycles take some practice but are universally understood. A device that demands balancing skill and leaning-steer coordination will deter casual users. If riders need hours or days to feel competent, the product is not a simple mobility substitute but a recreational vehicle for enthusiasts. That is a different business with a smaller customer base.

Third, the regulatory environment is unresolved and potentially hostile. Sidewalks are crowded with pedestrians, and municipalities typically restrict motorized vehicles there. Roads carry cars and trucks moving at speeds that make a low-speed, unprotected rider vulnerable. Without a clear legal pathway, widespread urban deployment is speculative. Retailers and buyers will hesitate if legality is uncertain in their city.

Fourth, the lack of cargo and passenger capacity removes two of the most common reasons people choose cars for short trips. Grocery runs, school drop-offs, and errands requiring bags are poorly served. This further narrows the use case to solo leisure or commuting under favorable conditions.

The core technology may have merit, and a narrower pilot targeting controlled environments such as gated campuses, tourism districts, or corporate fleets could validate demand and safety. But launching directly to consumers at this price, with unresolved regulation and significant usability friction, carries too much risk relative to the evidence presented.

DECISION: NO-GO
