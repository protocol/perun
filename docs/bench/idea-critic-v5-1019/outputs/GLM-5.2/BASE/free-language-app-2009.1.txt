The proposal has a strong core mission and a plausible early technical concept, but as presented in 2009 it is too underdeveloped to approve.

The central appeal is clear: free language learning addresses a real access gap, and the idea of learners producing useful translations while practicing is an elegant way to align educational value with potential revenue. That alone, however, is not enough to commit resources now.

The translation model is the proposal's biggest vulnerability. Learner output is inherently uneven, and publishers paying for such content is speculative. There is no evidence here that buyers exist, that quality can be assured, or that the two-sided dynamic—enough learners producing enough usable work to attract paying clients—can be sustained. Without even a rough validation path, this reads as a hypothesis stacked on another hypothesis.

The competitive landscape also raises concerns. Established paid courses are more polished, and while "free" is a powerful differentiator, it is not a strategy by itself. The team is small and academic, with no demonstrated experience in commercial product development, sales, or large-scale operations. That does not disqualify them, but it heightens the risk of an unproven revenue model remaining unproven indefinitely.

The plan to build first and seek revenue later is not unreasonable for an early-stage venture, but it defers the hardest question. If the translation market fails to materialize, there is no fallback described, and the cost of finding out is borne entirely upfront.

A more responsible path would be to validate demand on both sides before full build-out: confirm that learners will engage with short web-based lessons, and test whether any publisher would pay for crowd-translated content at an acceptable quality. With those answers, the proposal could become investable. As it stands, the risks outweigh the justification for approval.

DECISION: NO-GO
