This proposal asks us to bet our brand's core identity—the purity of sports-car engineering—on a segment where we have no experience and no inherent advantage. The financial cost is high, the technical fit is poor, and the competitive landscape is hostile. Approving this now would be reckless.

The brand exists because enthusiasts trust us to prioritize driving dynamics above all else. A heavy, off-road-capable crossover fundamentally contradicts that promise. Our loyal customers are not merely a revenue source; they are the foundation of our credibility. If we alienate them, we lose the very equity that would justify a premium price tag on an SUV. We cannot charge a high premium if our core audience views the vehicle as a betrayal.

The decision to co-develop with a larger group on a shared platform introduces significant risk. While this may offset some development costs, it means we will be building a high-priced, powerful vehicle on architecture we do not fully control. The larger group’s priorities will not perfectly align with ours, and the resulting compromises could further dilute the sports-car character we are supposed to be leveraging.

The competitive environment is perhaps the most damning factor. Established luxury SUV makers already possess the scale, dealer networks, and brand familiarity in that space. They are strong. Entering this market with a first-year high cost, on a shared platform, against entrenched competitors, is a recipe for a financial and reputational setback.

We must protect the brand. This proposal, as presented, asks us to risk the thing that makes us valuable for a chance in a market where we are outmatched. It is not a matter of avoiding growth, but of pursuing it in a way that does not jeopardize our foundation. This is not the right vehicle, nor the right partnership structure, to expand our range.

DECISION: NO-GO
