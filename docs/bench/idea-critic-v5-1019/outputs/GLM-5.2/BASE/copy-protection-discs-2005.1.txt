The proposal asks us to ship millions of audio discs containing software that installs itself on the buyer's computer when the disc is played, without clear consent. This single fact presents the most significant problem: we would be secretly installing software on our customers' property. The risks raised are not hypothetical concerns; they are direct, logical consequences of the proposal as described.

If we proceed, we are accepting that our copy-protection software may be hard to remove. This means we could be permanently altering the functionality of our customers' computers without their permission. Furthermore, security researchers could find weaknesses in the software. Once discovered, these vulnerabilities could be exploited by malicious actors, turning our anti-piracy measure into an attack vector against our own customers. The potential for widespread security incidents is high, given the plan to ship millions of these discs.

The proposal also acknowledges that customers may object to being treated as thieves. This is a critical point. Our revenue is derived from the goodwill and purchases of these very customers. If we alienate them by secretly installing software that is difficult to remove and potentially compromises their computer security, we risk a severe backlash. The loss of customer trust could far exceed any short-term reduction in copying. Negative publicity surrounding compromised computers and intrusive software could damage the brand irreparably.

The core issue is the conflict between the proposed method—secret, hard-to-remove software installation—and the necessity of maintaining customer trust. The proposal as presented does not offer a mechanism to mitigate these risks; it merely lists them. Shipping millions of discs with software that installs without clear consent is an unacceptable risk to our customers' security and our company's reputation.

DECISION: NO-GO
