The proposal relies on assumptions that are unproven and potentially fatal. Offering free delivery with no minimum order guarantees that low-value transactions will dominate. The cost of fulfilling a small order—driver time, logistics coordination, and vehicle expense—will almost always exceed the margin on a coffee or a snack. There is no clear path to profitability on individual transactions, and the model depends on either high average order values or sufficient density to make routes efficient. Neither has been demonstrated.

Direct employment of delivery staff adds fixed costs and operational complexity. In each new city, the company must recruit, train, manage, and pay workers regardless of demand fluctuations. This creates high burn rates before viability is established. Rapid multi-city expansion compounds this risk by replicating an unproven cost structure across markets before any single market has been validated.

The risk that customers will use the service only while it is subsidized is serious. If usage drops once fees are introduced, the model collapses. There is no evidence that customers will pay a premium for convenience on low-margin items, nor that order frequency will sustain the underlying cost base.

The revenue assumptions are thin. Venture funding may cover early losses, but without a credible plan to reach per-transaction profitability or to reduce fulfillment costs over time, capital will be consumed without a clear return. The market may not support the proposed economics at the scale required.

Approval as proposed would commit significant resources to a model with unresolved unit economics, untested demand elasticity, and high operational leverage. The risks are not marginal; they go to the core of whether the business can function. Revision is required before any investment proceeds.

DECISION: NO-GO
