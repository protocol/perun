The proposal targets a market gap that the established brands' own structures prevent them from closing. The direct-order model eliminates reseller markups, which is the mechanism that allows prices to stay below established brands while protecting margins. This is a structural cost advantage, not a temporary promotion.

The risks raised are real but manageable under the proposed plan. The desire to see and try a computer before buying is a preference, not an absolute barrier; lower prices and clear specifications can motivate buyers to accept the risk. The established makers' large sales channels and brands are assets tied to their current high-cost, high-margin model. If they move to direct, low-cost sales, they cannibalize their own channels and brand positioning. Their size makes them structurally slow to pivot to a direct model without alienating partners.

The lack of a service network is the most significant operational risk. However, the direct relationship with the customer actually provides an opportunity to offer faster, more personalized support than a fragmented reseller network. Standardized components simplify troubleshooting and repair. The company can build a mail-in or on-site service capability incrementally, funded by the revenue growth central to the proposal.

The concern about supplier parts being hard to get on credit is a standard working capital constraint for a new business. The proposal explicitly accounts for this by choosing to grow from revenue. This conservative approach to financing means the company is not relying on supplier credit to scale; it will expand only as cash flow allows. Starting with a limited inventory and reinvesting early profits mitigates the need for extensive credit lines.

The proposal presents a viable, self-funding business model with a clear competitive advantage. The risks are inherent to any hardware startup but are adequately addressed by the low-overhead structure and the phased, revenue-driven growth strategy.

DECISION: GO
