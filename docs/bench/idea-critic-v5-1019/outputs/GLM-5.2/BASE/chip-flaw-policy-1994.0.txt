The proposal underestimates the asymmetry between technical severity and market perception. Even if the flaw is statistically rare, it is verifiable, and verification is spreading beyond the company's control. Once customers know the processor can produce wrong results, the burden of proof cannot safely be shifted to them.

The gating mechanism—requiring users to prove they are affected—is operationally fragile. Most users lack the tools or inclination to demonstrate susceptibility, yet they will still bear uncertainty. This converts a finite engineering issue into an open-ended trust problem. The replacement cost for all affected chips is large, but it is a known, bounded liability. The cost of perceived indifference is unbounded: lost design wins, institutional buyers switching suppliers, and durable skepticism about future product claims.

Reputation risk is not a secondary effect here; it is the primary decision variable. The company's own analysis may be correct, but "rare over thousands of years" is not a message that survives contact with procurement officers, auditors, or enterprise risk committees. A policy that limits remedies to those who can "show" impact will read as evasion, especially while public discussion intensifies.

The prudent path is to get ahead of the narrative: acknowledge the flaw transparently, offer replacement chips broadly to those who request them, and frame the program as standing behind the product. This is expensive, but it converts a credibility crisis into a service commitment. The alternative—minimizing the issue and rationing remedies—preserves short-term cash while risking the franchise.

DECISION: NO-GO
