The proposal faces severe alignment and adoption risks. Launching a new disc format requires simultaneous cooperation from studios and electronics manufacturers, yet the model offers them uncertain upside. Studios already earn from DVD sales and rentals; a format that restricts viewing to 48 hours threatens existing revenue streams and retail relationships. Electronics makers have little incentive to produce special players when the installed base for standard DVD is growing rapidly. Without their commitment, the format cannot reach meaningful scale.

The consumer proposition is weak. Customers must buy more expensive players to use discs that cannot be played on standard equipment. They then pay for content that expires after 48 hours. This combines the worst features of purchase and rental: hardware cost, limited compatibility, and recurring fees. The dominant alternative, standard DVD rental, offers lower cost, broader selection, and no hardware barrier. The 48-hour window also conflicts with established rental norms, where customers expect several days of access.

The phone-line requirement adds operational complexity and privacy concerns. It assumes reliable connectivity and customer willingness to accept monitoring of their viewing habits. This feature increases player cost and creates a service infrastructure burden without clear consumer benefit.

Market timing is unfavorable. DVD is emerging as the dominant optical disc standard, with broad industry support and rapidly declining player prices. Introducing an incompatible, higher-cost alternative fragments the market at the moment when standardization is most valuable. The history of format wars suggests that technical merit alone does not determine success; content availability, hardware affordability, and retailer support are decisive. This proposal lacks all three.

The risks are not merely execution challenges; they are structural flaws in the value proposition. The model asks every participant—studios, manufacturers, retailers, and consumers—to accept higher costs and greater constraints for unclear benefits. Approval would commit resources to a format that conflicts with industry direction and consumer preferences. The proposal should be rejected unless a revised model can demonstrate compelling advantages over standard DVD rental and secure binding commitments from content and hardware partners.

DECISION: NO-GO
