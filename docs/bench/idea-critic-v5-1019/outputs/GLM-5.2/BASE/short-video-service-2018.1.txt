The proposal asks us to commit roughly 1.75 billion dollars to an unproven content model, on a timeline that leaves no room to validate core assumptions before scaling.

The central problem is demand. We are asking viewers to pay for short, phone-only episodes at a moment when free short video is already abundant and habit-forming. There is no evidence here that audiences perceive professionally made, under-ten-minute episodes as worth a subscription, particularly when those episodes cannot be watched on television at launch. That TV exclusion is not a minor feature gap; it removes the largest screen in the home and the viewing context where subscription habits are strongest. Launching without television support compounds the demand risk rather than isolating it.

The cost structure is the second failure point. High-budget shows commissioned from well-known producers will carry production costs comparable to premium television, but the format yields shorter runtimes and, presumably, faster consumption. If each episode is under ten minutes, the cost per minute of finished entertainment is extremely high. Recouping that spend through a monthly subscription and an ad-supported tier requires either massive scale or unusually strong retention, and the proposal gives no basis for either. The ad tier in particular needs volume to generate meaningful revenue; volume is exactly what an untested phone-only service cannot guarantee.

The two-country launch in spring 2020 is also too ambitious given the uncertainty. A better path would be a smaller pilot to test whether viewers will pay for this format at all, before committing nearly two billion dollars. The leadership team’s experience is valuable, but experience does not offset a model where the price, format, screen, and competitive landscape all push against breakeven.

Approving this as proposed means accepting a large capital commitment against four unresolved risks, any one of which could prevent recoupment. That is not a defensible bet.

DECISION: NO-GO
