The proposal asks us to sell a product for $10 that can cost up to $300 per month per user, then make up the difference through revenue streams that do not yet exist. That is not a business plan; it is a bet that scale will somehow produce profitability where the core unit economics are catastrophically negative.

The math is fatal. If a member sees one film per day at a $10 ticket price, the company pays theaters roughly $300 per month while collecting only $10. Even if most users see fewer films, the average cost per member will still vastly exceed the monthly fee. Adding more members at this price does not reduce losses; it multiplies them. The proposal explicitly depends on unproven revenue: data monetization, studio partnerships, and theater deals. None of these are contracts in hand. They are aspirations layered on top of a guaranteed cash bleed.

Heavy users are not an edge case to manage later; they are the predictable outcome of offering unlimited access below cost. Any member who sees two or more films per month is likely unprofitable. The more successful the service becomes at attracting customers, the faster it burns cash.

Theater chains have their own economics and competitive interests. They may resist a third party that discounts their product, captures their customer relationship, and shifts pricing power away from them. Cooperation is not assured, and without it the entire model collapses further.

A new owner with cash can subsidize losses temporarily, but cash is not a strategy. Unless the revenue streams are validated in advance, the company is simply buying customers at a massive loss and hoping someone will pay to reach them later. The downside is open-ended; the upside is speculative.

The core problem is simple: you cannot sustainably give away something worth more than you charge, then promise to fix it later with income that may never arrive.

DECISION: NO-GO
