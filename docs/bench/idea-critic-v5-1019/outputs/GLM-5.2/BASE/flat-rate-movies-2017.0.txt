The proposal depends on one untested assumption: that future revenue from data, studios, and theaters will exceed the losses created by selling access below cost. The math is straightforward and unfavorable. If the company pays theaters the full ticket price and charges only ten dollars a month, a member who sees two films already creates a loss. A member who sees one film every day creates a catastrophic one. There is no breakeven mechanism described. Growth does not solve this; it multiplies it. Every new subscriber is a potential liability, and the most active users are the most expensive.

The proposed offsetting revenue streams are speculative. Data has value only if users remain engaged long enough to generate meaningful behavioral signal, and only if buyers exist at sufficient scale. Studio and theater deals require the very cooperation that has already been flagged as a risk. Theater chains have little incentive to accept a third party that discounts their product, captures their customer relationship, and pays them full price only temporarily while burning investor capital. Studios may like increased attendance, but they will not subsidize a model that trains audiences to expect near-free admission.

The new owner's cash is not a strategy. It is a runway with a steep downward slope. Subsidizing losses during rapid growth works only when a durable, high-margin revenue model is clearly visible at scale. Here, that model is asserted, not demonstrated. The gap between price and cost is too wide, the user behavior most encouraged by the offer is the most destructive to margins, and the partners whose cooperation is essential have conflicting incentives.

Approving this as proposed means committing capital to a model where success and failure both lose money, and where the only path to sustainability depends on counterparties who are not aligned. That is not a calculated risk; it is an uncontrolled one.

DECISION: NO-GO
