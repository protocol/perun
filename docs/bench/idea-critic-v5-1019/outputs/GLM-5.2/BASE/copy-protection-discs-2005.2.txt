The proposal creates unacceptable exposure by secretly installing software on millions of customer computers while fundamentally misunderstanding the threat landscape.

The core economic problem is misdiagnosed. The stated threat is copying on computers, but the dominant revenue loss in 2005 comes from uncompressed ripping and peer-to-peer distribution. Software limiting copying addresses only one vector while ignoring the larger piracy infrastructure. The proposal treats the symptom, not the disease.

The legal and reputational risks are severe and asymmetric. Installing software without clear consent crosses established boundaries of consumer trust and likely violates computer fraud and abuse principles. The difficulty of removal compounds this—customers who discover hidden, persistent software they cannot easily uninstall will generate intense negative publicity. The customer objection risk is not incidental; it is central. Treating legitimate purchasers as presumed thieves destroys the relationship with the most valuable customer segment.

The security researcher risk is not merely possible but virtually certain given the scale of deployment. Millions of installations create a massive attack surface. When vulnerabilities are found—and they will be—the company faces liability for compromising customer systems, potential class action litigation, and reputational damage exceeding any piracy losses the software might prevent. The downside scenarios include security breaches, regulatory scrutiny, and consumer backlash that could dwarf the projected benefits.

The benefit case is weak. Even technically successful copy protection on audio discs faces circumvention through simple analog copying, foreign pressings without protection, or single compromised discs distributed online. The software will inconvenience legitimate users while failing to stop determined pirates. The cost-benefit analysis fails: high certain costs in customer trust, legal exposure, and security risk outweigh uncertain and likely marginal revenue protection.

DECISION: NO-GO
