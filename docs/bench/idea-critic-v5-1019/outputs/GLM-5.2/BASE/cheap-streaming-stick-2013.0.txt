The proposal turns prior failure into an asset: a cheap dongle controlled by devices buyers already own removes the main friction of earlier hardware, while the open kit lets the company seed a catalog without bearing all development costs. The lack of a remote is not a defect but a deliberate trade-off that lowers bill-of-materials cost and simplifies setup. Confusion risk is real, but it can be mitigated through clear packaging and onboarding; the core interaction—phone to TV—is already familiar to the target audience.

Thin margins are acceptable here. The strategic goal is not per-unit profit but expanding the footprint of the company's video and music services onto the living-room screen. Each dongle in use creates a new endpoint for subscription growth, advertising, and ecosystem lock-in. If the device is cheap enough to be an impulse buy, distribution can scale rapidly, and scale is what attracts third-party developers. The open kit is the right lever: it converts a launch weakness into a growth path, and the company's existing developer relationships can accelerate early support.

Competing boxes have larger catalogs, but they also cost more and carry their own interface burdens. A nearly invisible dongle that relies on a phone or computer as the controller offers a distinct positioning: minimal hardware, maximal simplicity. The very low price also lets the company bundle the device with subscriptions or promotions, further lowering adoption barriers.

The risks are manageable and the upside—service distribution, ecosystem expansion, and a low-cost foothold in television—is substantial relative to the investment required. The proposal is coherent, strategically aligned, and cheap enough to test at scale.

DECISION: GO
