First call: GO. The maker owns the phone the watch pairs with, which is the one thing every prior smartwatch got wrong.

Steelman: a watch tethered to an installed base of hundreds of millions of phones, sold through existing retail, turns a modest category into an accessory business with watch-grade margins.

Blockers: NONE. The cited risks (poor prior sales, phone dependence, one-day battery, second-screen skepticism) are the category's known conditions, not facts that break this plan. The maker's phone integration is the differentiator prior entrants lacked.

Risks (become kill criteria): one-day battery is the sharpest objection — buyers who charge a phone nightly may reject charging a watch too. Kill criterion: return rate and 90-day active-use rate; if active use drops below 50% of 30-day users, pivot to longer-battery variant or stop. Leading metric: 30-day retention; review at 90 days.

The proposal is a capped, staged first step: several materials and price levels let it test demand across segments without a single all-in bet. The app store and health tracking are the recurring-revenue and stickiness hooks.

Strongest attack survived: "buyers won't see a need for a second screen on the wrist." This failed because the watch's value isn't a second screen — it's glanceable notifications, fitness tracking, and Apple Pay, all of which work better on the wrist than in the pocket. The phone dependence is a feature, not a bug: it means the watch doesn't need its own cellular radio or full OS, keeping it simple and cheap to make.

Dissent ledger: one-day battery is a real constraint that could limit adoption. The multi-material strategy adds supply-chain complexity. The app store may struggle to attract developers at launch.

Remaining risk: the category is unproven for this maker, and the watch may cannibalize phone accessory sales rather than create new revenue.

DECISION: GO
