First call: NO-GO. The unit economics are broken on the proposal's own numbers and the rescue revenues are asserted, not shown.

Steelman: at scale, data monetization and studio/theater deals could exceed the per-ticket subsidy, making the low price a rational customer-acquisition spend.

Attack: the proposal's core claim — that data and deals will cover the subsidy — rests on revenues that are entirely unproven, while the cost (paying full ticket price for every film) is certain and immediate. At ~$10/month and one film/day, the company loses roughly $200+ per member per month in markets where average ticket prices exceed $10. Even doubling or tripling membership amplifies the bleed, not cures it. The proposal provides no evidence that data monetization or studio deals can close a gap of that magnitude, and theater chains — whose cooperation is essential — have a clear incentive to refuse, since they capture none of the subscription upside while the company devalues their pricing.

Better-way: a tiered or capped subscription (e.g., 2–3 films/month at $10–15) preserves the customer-acquisition logic while bounding the worst-case loss per heavy user. This reaches the same strategic upside — membership growth, data collection, deal leverage — at a fraction of the cash burn.

Pre-mortem: the company runs out of cash before data/deal revenues materialize, because heavy users cost far more than projected and theater chains withhold cooperation. The one-way door is the cash commitment: once the company is subsidizing tickets at scale, stopping means alienating the membership base it just built.

Kill criteria: cap the monthly loss per member (e.g., via a film limit); set a date within 90 days to verify signed studio or theater deals that plausibly close the gap; define the membership-growth rate and average-revenue-per-member threshold below which the pilot stops.

The proposal as stated commits to an uncapped, certain cash loss against entirely speculative revenues, with no evidence the gap closes.

DECISION: NO-GO
