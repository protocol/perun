First call: NO-GO — launching a multi-billion-dollar program on one 34-unit order, before the regulatory approval that makes the aircraft usable on its target routes exists.

Blockers:
- The entire commercial premise — a twin-engine wide-body for long overwater routes — depends on ETOPS approval that has **not** been granted. Without it, the aircraft cannot serve the missions airlines are buying it for. This is a stated fact, not a risk.
- Launching on a single airline's 34-aircraft order at very large cost means the break-even case rests on unproven follow-on demand. The proposal presents no second buyer, no order pipeline, no market forecast.

Risks (become kill criteria):
- Development cost overrun could threaten the company's solvency — cap exposure, stage commitments.
- Designing with eight airlines in the room may produce scope creep and schedule slip — set a configuration freeze date.
- Rival new models may capture the market window — name the delivery-date deadline that loses the opportunity.

Strongest attack survived: "The market need is real, airlines want this aircraft, and the first-mover advantage justifies launching now." This fails because real demand from one airline is not confirmed market demand, and first-mover advantage is worthless if the regulatory approval does not come or comes late — the proposal banks on an outcome it cannot control.

The proposal's own numbers don't work: one 34-aircraft order cannot cover very large development cost, and the regulatory linchpin is unresolved. Approving as proposed means committing billions before knowing whether the aircraft can legally fly the routes it is designed for.

DECISION: NO-GO
