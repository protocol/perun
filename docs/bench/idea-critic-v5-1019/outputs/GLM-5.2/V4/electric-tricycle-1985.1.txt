**First call: GO.** Sub-£400 personal mobility in a regulated gap, backed by a committed assembler, is a sound operator bet.

**Steelman:** A licence-exempt, sub-£400 tricycle captures a vacant transport niche—cheaper than a car or moped, faster than walking, with mail-order distribution removing dealer overhead.

**Alternatives:** *Do nothing*—entrepreneur stays in computers, misses a known mobility gap. *Sell through dealers*—higher reach but adds margin and complexity the £400 price cannot absorb. Mail order is the cheapest path to volume.

**Pre-mortem:** It fails because 15 mph is too slow for roads yet too fast for pavements; the low seat angers safety groups; and winter weather halves the usable season. A two-way door: small batch, reversible if demand disappoints.

**Evidence check:** The proposal's own facts contain the killer. Top speed 15 mph, short range, no weather protection, low seating position, British winters. Every risk raised is a *stated negative the plan does not answer*. There is no cap, no pilot territory, no kill metric, no weather-protection extra named.

**Blockers:** The plan proposes a national January launch with large-volume mail-order sales while its own stated negatives—speed, range, weather, safety—remain unaddressed and no kill criterion or capped pilot is attached.

**Risks (become kill criteria):** Cap the first batch; pilot in a dry, flat region before winter; set a kill metric (e.g., sub-X units in 8 weeks triggers stop); name the weather-protection extra.

**Strongest attack survived:** "15 mph is too slow for traffic and too fast for pavements; the vehicle has no natural lane." This is severe but it is a *risk*, not a blocker: the niche may still exist for short suburban trips, and a capped pilot tests exactly this before national exposure.

The proposal is sound in concept but ships too much scale too early against its own unaddressed negatives. Revise: cap the pilot, name the kill metric, then launch.

DECISION: NO-GO
