First call: NO-GO. The format war is won by title availability and installed base, not by picture quality alone, and this plan concedes both.

Steelman: superior picture quality and an early lead create a defensible premium niche; controlled licensing protects margins and brand. That is the strongest version, and it still loses the mass market.

The rival's strategy—broad licensing, longer tapes, lower prices through competition—directly attacks the two things that matter for a consumer format: reach and content. One hour cannot record a film or a sports event. That is not a risk; it is a product-limiting fact stated in the proposal itself. A consumer who cannot record what they want to watch has no reason to buy, regardless of picture quality. Fewer licensed manufacturers means fewer pre-recorded titles, which means fewer reasons to own the player. Each of these is a blocker resting on the proposal's own facts.

The pre-mortem is straightforward: the rival format becomes ubiquitous because it is cheap, records a full film, and has titles from many manufacturers; the proprietary format becomes a high-quality orphan with a small library. The cost of delay here is low—waiting to broaden licensing does not forfeit the early lead in the near term, and the one-hour limitation can be addressed before launch.

Open the answer with the corrected framing: this is a network-effects business, not a premium-product business. The decision turns on that.

DECISION: NO-GO
