First call: GO — a smaller player losing the desktop performance lead has one move: bet the architecture, ship more cores, undercut the rival, and let the price gap force adoption. Doing nothing concedes the segment.

Steelman: a clean architecture with more cores at lower prices can leapfrog the rival's current generation, win back design wins, and give software makers a reason to optimize for core count.

Better-way check: "do nothing" concedes desktop permanently — not viable. The strongest alternative is a conservative refresh of the existing architecture at a lower price point. That avoids the all-new-design risk but does not close the performance gap that caused the problem. The bold path reaches the upside the conservative path cannot.

Skeptic: the proposal's own facts carry the sharpest objection — the last all-new design underperformed, finances leave no room for a second miss, and software may not use the extra cores. If the new architecture repeats the prior miss, the company is insolvent. That is a one-way door with existential stakes.

Kill criteria: the architecture must clear a defined performance-per-clock threshold in silicon before volume commit; if it does not, stop. Software adoption needs a leading metric — design wins or committed optimization from at least two major ISVs — reviewed within two quarters of launch.

The rival's ability to cut prices or ship faster chips is a risk, not a blocker — it is true of every competitive launch and does not invalidate the move.

This is a sound bet made under real uncertainty. The alternative is managed decline. The architecture is the only lever that can close the gap, and the price aggression is the only way to force the ecosystem to follow. Approve with the kill criteria above; the performance gate before volume commit is the non-negotiable term.

DECISION: GO
