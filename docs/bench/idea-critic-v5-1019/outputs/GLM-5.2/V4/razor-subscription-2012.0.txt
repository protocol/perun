**First call: GO.** Low spend, reversible, attacks a known markup.

**Steelman:** two incumbents overcharge for a commodity; a cheap subscription with a funny video can win attention that money can't buy.

**Alternatives:** do nothing — safe but no upside. Sell retail — needs shelf deals and capital they don't have. The proposed path is the cheapest reversible test.

**Pre-mortem:** the video flops and nobody signs up. That's a small loss, not a failure mode that blocks the attempt. One-way vs two-way door: this is two-way — kill the site, lose a few thousand dollars.

**Evidence check:** the dominant brands spend heavily on advertising — stated. The founders don't make blades — stated. The real question is whether the numbers work: can the subscription price plus shipping cover the supplier cost at low volume? That arithmetic isn't in the proposal, but the spend to test it is trivial.

**Blockers:** NONE. No fact stated in the proposal contradicts the core claim. The risks raised are all *risks*, not blockers:
- Brands cut prices or sue — possible, not yet real; a kill criterion if it materializes.
- One supplier — a dependency risk, not a plan-killer for a launch test.
- Single video may bring little traffic — exactly why the spend stays low and the test is fast.
- Customers may not like subscriptions — the test answers this directly.

**Kill criteria:** if the video doesn't drive a target order count within 30 days, stop spending on acquisition and reassess. If supplier cost makes the unit economics negative at the stated price, revise the price or the model before scaling.

**Strongest attack survived:** "a single low-budget video won't generate enough traffic to validate anything." It failed because the proposal isn't betting the company on one video — it's making a cheap, fast, reversible test where even modest signups prove the subscription model has demand. The downside is a few thousand dollars; the upside is a category-defining brand. That asymmetry favors acting now.

DECISION: GO
