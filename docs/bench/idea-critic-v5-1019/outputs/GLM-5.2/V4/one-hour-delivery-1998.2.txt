First call: NO-GO. The unit economics fail on the proposal's own numbers.

**Steelman:** Dense urban demand plus direct control of the delivery fleet could, at scale, make a one-hour convenience service defensible against incumbents.

**Blockers:**

1. **The numbers contradict the claim.** Free delivery with no minimum order means a customer ordering a $3 snack costs the company more in delivery labor than it earns in margin. The proposal does not state a path to positive contribution margin per order. Every order could be a loss, and volume multiplies the loss.

2. **The cost structure is fixed and per-city.** Directly employed delivery staff in each city means recurring payroll, vehicles, and management overhead before a single order ships. Expanding to more cities quickly replicates this fixed cost without proving the first city works.

3. **The stated risk is not answered.** "Customers may use the service only when it is subsidized" is acknowledged but not addressed with a kill criterion, a pilot, or a margin model. If true, the business is a charity that stops when funding stops.

**Risks (become kill criteria if revised):**
- No pilot named; first city is the pilot but the proposal commits to expansion before results exist.
- No cap on spend stated.
- No leading metric or review date.

**Strongest attack survived:** None. The attack that free delivery of small orders costs more than the margin on them is the proposal's own stated risk, and it is not answered.

**Dissent ledger:** The bet is not wrong because delivery is hard; it is wrong as proposed because the unit economics are inverted and the plan does not show when or how they flip positive. A revised proposal would need: a capped single-city pilot, a stated contribution-margin model per order, a kill criterion (e.g., repeat purchase rate below X% within 90 days means stop), and a decision on whether delivery staff must be direct employees versus contractors.

DECISION: NO-GO
