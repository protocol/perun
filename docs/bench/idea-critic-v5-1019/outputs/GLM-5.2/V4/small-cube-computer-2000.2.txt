First call: GO. A recovered computer maker's eleven profitable quarters are the balance sheet that lets it ship a differentiated, low-volume product without betting the company.

Steelman: a fanless, acrylic desktop is a premium lifestyle object that earns margin above the tower and reaches buyers the tower never could.

Alternatives: do nothing (safe, cedes the premium-lifestyle segment); refine the tower line (cheaper, but commodity); ship the same design in a conventional case (cheaper to manufacture, loses the entire point of differentiation). None reaches the same upside.

Pre-mortem: the product fails if the acrylic case cannot be manufactured without visible flaws at volume — a one-way door on tooling. Price above the tower and all-in-one is a feature, not a bug: it signals premium and protects tower margins. Lack of expansion slots is intentional: the target buyer never opens the case.

The risks raised are real but none is a blocker. Manufacturing difficulty is an execution risk with a clear mitigation: pilot production run, inspect for flaws, gate volume on yield. The price and slot objections assume the target buyer is the tower buyer — the proposal's logic is that it is a different buyer entirely. That assumption is the bet, and it is a reasonable one for a company with eleven profitable quarters of runway.

Kill criteria: pilot production yield above an acceptable threshold (say 90%); first-quarter sales above a minimum floor; return rates below a ceiling. If any fails, stop. Leading metric: first-month sell-through against the pilot production batch.

The proposal is a sound bet: differentiated product, premium pricing, protected tower margins, capped downside through a pilot run, and clear kill criteria. The risks are the plan's kill criteria, not reasons to block it.

DECISION: GO
