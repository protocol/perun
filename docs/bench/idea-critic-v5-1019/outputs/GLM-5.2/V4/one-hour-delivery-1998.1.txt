**First call: NO-GO.** The unit economics are broken on the proposal's own facts: free delivery of a $3 snack costs more than the margin on it, and the model requires direct employment of delivery staff in every city — the two highest cost inputs, both unaddressed.

**Steelman:** Dense urban demand for convenience is real; direct employment ensures quality control; fast city expansion captures mindshare before competitors.

**Strongest attack survived:** None — the central attack was not survived. The proposal's own risk list states the failure as already true: free delivery of small orders costs more than the margin on them. The plan does not answer this. It is not a risk that might materialize; it is the model's starting condition.

**Alternatives:**
- **Do nothing:** beats the proposal — no capital burned on negative-margin delivery.
- **Minimum order or delivery fee:** fixes the unit economics but contradicts the stated value proposition ("free delivery, no minimum").
- **Courier network, not direct hires:** cuts fixed cost per city, but the proposal commits to direct employment.

**Pre-mortem:** The company raised and burned hundreds of millions, expanded to many cities, and collapsed because every small order lost money and direct staffing made the loss structural. This is Kozmo.com (1997–2001), and the outcome is known.

**Blockers:** The proposal states free delivery of small orders costs more than the margin on them (its own risk #1), and commits to direct employment of delivery staff in every city (risk #2, structural cost). The plan does not answer either. A model whose stated starting condition is that every transaction loses money, scaled by direct hiring across cities, fails as proposed.

**Kill criteria if revised:** Average order contribution margin must be positive within 90 days of launch in city one (delivery cost + cost of goods < revenue per order), measured on actual orders, not projections. If not, stop expansion and revisit pricing.

DECISION: NO-GO
