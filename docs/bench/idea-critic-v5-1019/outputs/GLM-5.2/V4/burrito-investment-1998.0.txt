First call: GO. The upside is a real call option on a category the giant cannot build internally, and the structure caps the downside at the minority stake.

Steelman: a small, high-margin, made-to-order concept could become a distinct growth engine the giant's core menu cannot reach, and the option to invest more later means the giant learns before committing.

The strongest attack: the chain is tiny and may not scale, and operational complexity (skilled workers, slower service) could make scaling harder, not easier. This fails to kill the proposal because the structure already answers it — a minority stake with an option to increase is precisely how you test scalability without betting the company. If it doesn't scale, you lose the stake. If it does, you exercise the option. The risks raised are real but they are risks the investment structure is designed to manage.

Blockers: NONE. The proposal's own numbers (16 restaurants, minority stake, option to invest more) do not contradict its core claim. No alternative reaches the same upside cheaper: building internally loses time and brand separation; doing nothing forfeits the option entirely.

Risks (become kill criteria): the chain fails to scale beyond its current footprint within a defined window; unit economics deteriorate as it grows; management attention is genuinely diverted. Kill criterion: if the chain has not demonstrated scalable unit economics in new markets within 24 months, do not exercise the option to invest more. Leading metric: same-store sales growth and new-location profitability, reviewed at 12 and 24 months.

The proposal is a capped, time-boxed first step with a clear kill criterion and an option to scale. Approve as proposed.
