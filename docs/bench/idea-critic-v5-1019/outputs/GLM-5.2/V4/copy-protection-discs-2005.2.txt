**First call: NO-GO.** The proposal treats paying customers as attackers and hides the mechanism from them.

**Steelman:** Copying is a real revenue loss; a disc-level technical control deployed at scale could materially reduce casual ripping and recover sales.

**Attack:** The core mechanism — silent installation of software the buyer did not agree to — is not a risk to mitigate; it is the plan itself. Every listed risk flows directly from that design choice and is therefore unaddressable without abandoning it.

**Better way:** Do nothing. The discs already work; the proposal breaks them for legitimate buyers. Licensing (FairPlay, already proven) achieves the goal without altering the customer's computer.

**Pre-mortem:** It already failed. A researcher finds the rootkit (it runs at kernel level with no disclosure). Removal requires tools the company itself cannot safely distribute. A class action and product recall follow. The brand damage dwarfs any recovered sales. This is a one-way door: once millions of discs ship with hidden software, there is no recall path that restores trust.

**Evidence check:** The proposal's own risk list is the blocker. It states as already true that the software installs without clear consent and is hard to remove. Those are not future contingencies; they are the mechanism. A plan whose stated facts remove its own route to the upside, and that the plan does not answer, fails as proposed.

**Kill criteria:** None possible. Any deployment at scale is irreversible; any pilot small enough to be safe is too small to measure.

**Strongest attack survived:** None. The attack succeeded.

**Remaining risk:** Legal liability, brand destruction, and the certainty that hiding software from millions of buyers cannot stay secret.

DECISION: NO-GO
