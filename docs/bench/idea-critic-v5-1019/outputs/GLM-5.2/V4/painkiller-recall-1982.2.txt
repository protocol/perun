First call: GO. Seven deaths, no identified source, a product taken by millions daily. The cost of withdrawal is bounded and financial; the cost of not withdrawing is unbounded and includes further deaths and the permanent destruction of the brand's core asset—trust.

Steelman: A nationwide recall of a tamperable product after confirmed poisonings is the only action that (a) stops the killing vector regardless of where the tampering occurred, and (b) preserves the long-term franchise by demonstrating that the company places public safety above short-term profit. The "may be local" objection assumes the attacker's geography is fixed; a copycat or a wider distribution of poisoned bottles is not ruled out by the facts given. The "signal it is dangerous" objection is inverted: failing to withdraw after confirmed deaths signals callousness, which is the true brand killer.

Alternatives considered:
- **Regional recall only**: cheaper, but leaves the killing vector active nationwide; one more death outside the recalled region ends the franchise anyway.
- **Do nothing / wait for source**: the attacker is unknown and may strike again; every day of delay is a day of unbounded liability.
- **Withdraw nationally without production halt**: inconsistent; poisoned inventory could remain in the supply chain.

The nationwide withdrawal dominates all alternatives on the stated facts because the threat is not localized tampering but a tamperable delivery system exploited by an unknown actor. The relaunch in tamper-resistant packaging converts a one-way crisis into a two-way door: the company can re-enter the market with a structural fix that addresses the root cause.

The risks raised are real but all are financial or reputational, and every one is dwarfed by the existential risk of a third, fourth, or tenth death while the product remains on shelves. The cost of recall is a known, bounded number. The cost of a death traced to continued sale is bankruptcy plus criminal exposure.

DECISION: GO
