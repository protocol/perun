First call: GO. A large, young, fast-growing audience is a scarce strategic asset; the advertising business is the acquirer's, so the combination question is real.

Steelman: this audience is growing faster than the acquirer's properties and is the demographic advertisers pay most to reach; owning it is cheaper than renting it once competitors bid for the same inventory.

Better-way: a partnership or exclusive ad deal captures most of the revenue upside at a fraction of the capital and with a clean exit; do-nothing leaves the acquirer buying remnant inventory later at a higher price. The partnership is the cheaper path to the same upside and is the standing objection to the purchase.

Kill criteria: the plan commits 580 million dollars with no stated cap, pilot, metric or fallback. The first step is a capped, time-boxed exclusive ad deal: spend at most X, run for N months, measure revenue per young-audience page view against the acquirer's existing youth properties. If revenue per page view is below Y by date D, stop and do not acquire. Only if that pilot clears the threshold does the 580 million dollar question return.

Blockers: NONE stated. The proposal's own risks (price, taste, competition, monetization) are risks, not blockers: they become the kill criteria above. The single sharpest objection actually tried is "monetization is unproven, so buying is overpaying for an option." It fails because an ad deal tests that exact option at a fraction of the cost, and the purchase price is justified only if that test passes.

Remaining risk: a pilot delays the acquisition and a competitor may buy the target in the window; the cost of delay is the strategic asset, the cost of an uncapped purchase is the downside.

DECISION: NO-GO
