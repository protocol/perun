First call: NO-GO — every premise that makes the economics work is a dependency on a competitor's cooperation.

Steelman: a proprietary pay-per-view disc, sold cheap and unlocked by phone, captures the rental market's margin and recurring revenue.

Blockers:
1. **Format adoption depends on competitors.** Studios and electronics makers must agree to support a new format. These same parties have competing interests in the standard format. The proposal states this as a requirement, not a solved problem — the entire revenue model is gated on third-party cooperation that hasn't been secured.
2. **The discs cannot be played on standard players.** This means every customer must buy the special player, which costs more than standard players. The format's adoption is therefore blocked at the hardware layer — customers won't buy expensive proprietary players for discs they can't get, and partners won't make discs for players customers don't own.
3. **Customers dislike paying for repeated viewing.** The proposal's own risk list names this. The business model is recurring fees on a disc the customer already holds — if customers reject this mechanism, the format has no revenue path even if adoption succeeds.

These three form a single circular dependency: no players without discs, no discs without studios, no studios without an installed base of players — and even if the circle closes, customers may reject the pay-per-view mechanism that funds it.

Risks (not blockers, but kill criteria if the plan proceeds): studio exclusivity deals that lock out the format; phone-line infrastructure costs scaling faster than adoption; a standard-format price war that makes the premium player uncompetitive.

The proposal asks to launch a format whose success requires competitors to cooperate, customers to buy more expensive hardware, and those same customers to accept a payment model the proposal itself flags as disliked. That's a NO-GO as proposed.

DECISION: NO-GO
