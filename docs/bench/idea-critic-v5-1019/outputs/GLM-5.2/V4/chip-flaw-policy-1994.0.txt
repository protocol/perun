First call: NO-GO. The plan's gate (prove you're affected) is unsatisfiable for the stated user, so the remedy reaches no one while the company still bears the reputational hit.

Blocker: the proposal's own remedy contradicts its own evidence. It concedes users "cannot easily show" they're affected, yet conditions replacement on exactly that proof. The result is a non-remedy: the rare user who hits the bug cannot demonstrate it, so no one qualifies, and the company has both the defect and the perception of evasion.

Strongest attack survived: none — the attack succeeds.

Better way: offer no-questions replacement to anyone who asks, capped by inventory. It costs less than full replacement, removes the proof burden, and converts the story from "hiding a flaw" to "standing behind the product."

Kill criteria: if replacement requests exceed X within 30 days, move to broader exchange; if negative coverage persists past two weeks despite the offer, escalate. Leading metric: request volume and sentiment, reviewed at day 14.

Remaining risk: even a generous exchange may not fully restore trust once the public believes the company initially minimized the defect.
