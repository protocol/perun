**First call: NO-GO.** The economics fail on the proposal's own facts.

**Steelman:** A cheap disc with built-in expiration creates a rental model without returns, inventory tracking, or late-fee disputes — a compelling operational story.

**The attack that breaks it:** The proposal states discs play *only* on special players that cost *more* than standard players, which themselves cannot play these discs. This is a two-sided market (players + content) requiring both studios and electronics makers to agree to a new format — against an already-shipping standard. The customer must pay a premium for a *less* capable player (one that won't play the existing standard discs), then pay again after 48 hours. The proposal's own risks list confirms customers dislike paying for repeated viewing. The format locks out the installed base of standard players, so adoption depends on households replacing working hardware with more expensive, restricted hardware. No party in the chain — studio, manufacturer, consumer — has an incentive to move first.

**Blocker (stated fact, not risk):** The discs cannot play on standard players, and the special players cost more than standard players. This removes the route to adoption: the plan needs an installed base of special players to attract studios, and studio content to attract player buyers, while the cheaper standard player is already in homes. The proposal states this as already true and does not answer it.

**Alternatives:** Do nothing — studios and retailers continue the existing rental model without a format war. This is cheaper and lower-risk. A subscription or standard-rental partnership reaches the same revenue without requiring new hardware adoption.

The proposal is a one-way door (new format, partner commitments) with no kill criterion, no pilot, and a cost structure that punishes the customer it needs.

DECISION: NO-GO
