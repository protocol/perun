First call: GO. The proposal's own risks are all manageable, and none of them rises to the level of a blocker that would justify killing the project outright.

The steelman here is that this is a genuinely novel personal transportation device that could carve out a new niche between walking and driving. The strongest attack against it is the risk that consumers simply won't adopt it at that price point, especially given the learning curve and the fact that it doesn't carry cargo or passengers. That's a real risk, but it's a risk that can be tested and measured, not a fatal flaw in the proposal itself.

The alternatives — doing nothing, or waiting to see if the technology matures — are weaker. The competitive landscape for personal transport isn't moving fast, and first-mover advantage in a new category is significant. The price is high but not prohibitive for the target market of urban professionals making short trips.

The key risks are the regulatory uncertainty (sidewalks and roads may not allow it), the learning curve, and the price sensitivity. These are all things that can be addressed through a phased rollout, pilot programs in specific cities, and careful attention to the regulatory environment. The proposal doesn't need to solve all these problems before launch; it needs to acknowledge them and have a plan to address them.

The bottom line is that this is a bet on a new category of personal transportation. The upside is significant, the downside is manageable, and the risks are known and addressable. This is the kind of bet that's worth making, with appropriate caution and a clear plan for measuring success and pivoting if necessary.
