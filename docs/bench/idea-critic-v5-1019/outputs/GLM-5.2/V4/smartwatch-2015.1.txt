**First call: GO.** A phone+computer maker entering a category before it is crowded, leveraging its installed base and brand, with a defensible health/fitness angle.

**Steelman:** A company with hundreds of millions of phone owners, strong brand loyalty, retail presence, and an existing app ecosystem is uniquely positioned to define a new product category that standalone smartwatch makers cannot.

**Skeptic — assumptions:**
- "Earlier smartwatches sold poorly" assumes prior failures predict this maker's outcome. Those makers lacked an integrated phone ecosystem and retail footprint.
- "Buyers may not see a need" is speculation, not evidence of no demand.
- One-day battery is a real constraint but not fatal for a first-generation device charged nightly alongside the phone.

**Better-way:**
- *Do nothing:* preserves capital but cedes the category to competitors and forgoes lock-in via the wrist.
- *Cheaper single-model watch:* lower risk but misses the materials/price segmentation that captures both mass and premium buyers.
- The multi-tier approach is the stronger play; it hedges across segments in one launch.

**Kill-criteria:**
- Return rate exceeding 15% within 90 days (signals the product does not meet a real need).
- Attach rate below 3% of phone install base after two quarters (signals insufficient demand to justify the category).
- If either fires: halt, iterate on the core use cases (health/fitness, notifications), and reassess.

**Strongest attack survived:** "One-day battery makes this a burden, not a tool." This failed because the phone it pairs with is already charged nightly, the use case (glances, fitness) does not require multi-day endurance, and battery technology improves iteratively — a day-one limitation, not a category-killer.

**Remaining risk:** The watch's dependence on the phone limits standalone value; if a competitor ships a genuinely independent smartwatch first, this maker's positioning weakens.

DECISION: GO
