First call: GO. The company already pays full ticket price per film, so cutting the fee while subsidizing the loss is a cash-burning growth play with an unproven monetization model.

Steelman: The strongest defensible version of the claim is that by lowering the price to about ten dollars a month, the service can rapidly scale its membership base, creating a valuable network of moviegoers that studios, theaters, and advertisers will pay to access, with the new owner's capital covering the initial subsidy gap.

Attack: The core financial premise is that future data and partnership revenue will exceed the per-ticket subsidy cost. The proposal itself states the fee does not cover the cost and the company pays full price for every ticket. This creates a structural loss that scales linearly with usage. Heavy users seeing one film a day at an average ticket price of approximately nine dollars would cost the company around two hundred seventy dollars per month per heavy user, against ten dollars in revenue—a net loss of roughly two hundred sixty dollars per heavy user per month. The proposal acknowledges heavy users will cost more than they pay, but does not present a cap or a mechanism to limit this exposure.

The new revenue streams—data and deals with studios and theaters—are explicitly unproven. The proposal also notes theater chains may refuse to cooperate, which directly threatens the partnerships the model depends on for future revenue.

Blockers: The stated facts show the plan's own numbers do not work without an uncapped subsidy, and the revenue streams that would close the gap are unproven and threatened by the cooperation risk the proposal itself raises.

Risks: Unproven demand at the new price point, execution difficulty in scaling infrastructure, and the possibility that theaters refuse to participate.

Kill criteria: If average revenue per user from data and partnerships does not exceed the average subsidy per user within the first growth window, the model fails and must be stopped.

DECISION: NO-GO
