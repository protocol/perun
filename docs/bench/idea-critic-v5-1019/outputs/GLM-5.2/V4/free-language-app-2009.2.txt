First call: GO. The product itself is a two-way door — build it, ship it, learn. The revenue model is genuinely unproven, but the proposal explicitly defers it ("look for revenue later"), which is the right call when the downside of building is low and the option value is high.

The strongest attack: the entire business model rests on learners producing translation quality good enough to sell, and the proposal provides zero evidence this works. If learner translations are unsellable, there is no revenue path at all — not deferred, absent.

This attack fails to block for a specific reason: the product can be built and launched without the translation marketplace functioning. Free language lessons have standalone value and near-zero marginal cost per user. The team can validate the core hypothesis — can learners produce sellable translations? — with a tiny pilot before building marketplace infrastructure. The proposal's own framing ("keep learning free, look for revenue later") already embeds this optionality.

The real risks (translation quality, conversion, polished incumbents) are genuine but they are risks, not blockers. They become kill criteria: if an early pilot shows learner translations cannot reach sellable quality with reasonable review effort, the team pivots to a different revenue model or folds. The team's small size and low burn mean the cost of testing this is minimal.

The nonprofit-vs-for-profit structure question is deferred but not blocking at the build stage.

Kill criteria: (1) within the first pilot, measure translation quality against a publisher's acceptance bar; if fewer than 20% of learner submissions pass without heavy editing, the translation-revenue thesis is dead. (2) Track whether free learners engage with translation tasks at all — zero willingness to produce work kills the model regardless of quality.

Leading metric: learner submission rate and quality pass-through in the first pilot. Review within 90 days of launch.

DECISION: GO
