First call: GO. The 1957 styling is the one asset this weak carmaker owns that no competitor can copy; betting the company on it is rational, and the platform reuse keeps the downside small.

The sharpest attack: buyers won't pay above the platform-mate for nostalgia, and thin small-car margins make the premium essential. But retro styling has commanded premiums before — the Volkswagen New Beetle and BMW Mini both launched above their mechanical peers and succeeded precisely on emotional appeal. The personalization options aren't decoration; they're the margin engine. Small cars earn little per unit, so trim-option markups are where the profit lives. The platform is already amortized, which protects the downside.

The pre-mortem: the car dates quickly, recent models have flopped, and the company's finances can't absorb another miss. These are real but they argue for *how* to launch, not *whether*. The counter is that this car isn't competing on specification — it's competing on identity. A carmaker with weak finances and poor recent sales has nothing to lose by leading with its strongest emotional asset. Playing safe — another forgettable small car at a forgettable price — is the true high-risk move for a company already losing buyers.

Kill criteria: set a 12-month sales target tied to break-even on incremental investment (platform is shared, so the bet is tooling and marketing). If personalization take-up falls below a set threshold, the margin thesis is wrong and the company should cut losses. The one-way-door risk is low: tooling a single retro body on an existing platform is reversible if the market rejects it, and far cheaper than a clean-sheet platform.

The company's best-loved design language, made accessible on proven mechanicals, with margins protected through personalization — this is the correct bet for a carmaker whose brand memory is its only durable advantage.

DECISION: GO
