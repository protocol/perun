First call: NO-GO. Shipping a known-inferior product as a forced default on every new and updated phone in one cut is an operator error, not a risk to manage.

The steelman is strong: control of a core platform service matters, and turn-by-turn plus 3D plus voice integration is a genuine user draw the rival withheld. But the proposal's own risk list is the blocker. The maker states its maps have "a lot less data" and that place and business information "may be wrong in places." Those are not future risks; they are present-tense facts about the product being shipped. Removing a working, data-rich rival app and replacing it with a known-thinner, known-error-prone alternative on every device simultaneously guarantees a visible quality regression at launch. The plan does not answer how a product that is admitted to be worse on data depth and accuracy delivers a better experience on day one. The upside (control, integration) is real but does not require a hard cutover with no fallback.

Alternatives the proposal does not consider: ship the in-house app alongside the rival app as a choice, letting users migrate; launch on new phones only and leave updated phones on the rival app until parity is reached; or run a closed beta to measure real-world data gaps before a full launch. Each reaches the same strategic destination with less blast radius.

The pre-mortem is obvious: reviewers and users discover the maps are missing neighborhoods, mislabeling businesses, and giving wrong directions; the press calls it an embarrassment; the CEO issues a public apology; the rival app returns as an option. The door is two-way but expensive — reputational damage compounds faster than mapping data can be backfilled.

Kill criteria if pursued anyway: a capped pilot on one device tier or one region, with a hard gate that no full launch proceeds until place-data accuracy reaches a measured threshold against the rival app within a fixed window.

DECISION: NO-GO
