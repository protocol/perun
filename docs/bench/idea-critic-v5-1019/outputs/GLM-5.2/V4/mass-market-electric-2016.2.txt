First call: NO-GO. The company's entire production history is two cars; the plan asks it to multiply that by orders of magnitude in under twelve months.

Steelman: refundable deposits convert enthusiasm into committed demand and funded working capital, validating the market before the factory is built. That is the strongest version.

Strongest attack survived: none. The blocker is in the proposal's own facts. It has built two vehicles. It proposes high-volume production next year. Nothing stated closes a gap that large in a window that short. The battery supply scale-up compounds the production risk: two unproven scaling problems stacked on each other, both must succeed in the same year. Refundable deposits are not sales; if production slips, deposits walk. Lower margin means the plan must hit volume to survive — the one thing it has never done.

Do nothing: the company keeps selling expensive cars in small numbers, losing the mass market to incumbents but not betting the firm on a production ramp it cannot evidence.

Cheaper alternative: unveil the sedan, take deposits, but commit to a pilot build in the hundreds before full volume. This tests the production system and the battery supply chain at recoverable cost, rather than betting the company on an unproven ramp.

The proposal's own evidence contradicts its core claim: it asserts high-volume production next year while stating a two-car production history. That is the blocker.

Kill criteria if revised: a capped pilot build (hundreds of units) with a date inside the first production window; a signed battery supply agreement covering the pilot volume; deposit-to-sale conversion measured at the pilot stage before any full-volume commitment.

The mass-market electric sedan is likely the right strategic bet. Approving it as proposed — full volume next year on a two-car production history — is not. The production gap is the blocker; a pilot build is the fix.

DECISION: NO-GO
