First call: GO. The model is internally coherent and the risks are execution risks, not blockers.

Steelman: a membership-funded, thin-margin warehouse turns retail from a goods-margin business into a subscription business, so scale and renewal matter more than per-unit markup.

Blockers: NONE. No stated fact contradicts the core claim. The risks listed (fee resistance, incumbent advantage, margin fragility, site/customer requirements) are conditions that *could* fail, not facts already showing failure.

Risks → kill criteria: If membership sign-ups at the first warehouse fall below a viable threshold within the first 6–12 months, or if renewal rates disappoint in year two, the model does not scale — stop expanding and reassess. The leading metric to watch is paid memberships per warehouse; review at 6 months and 12 months.

Strongest attack survived: "Shoppers won't pay a fee to shop, so membership revenue — the profit engine — never materializes." This is the sharpest objection because if it holds, the entire economics collapse. But the attack fails on the proposal's own logic: the fee is low relative to the savings from bulk pricing, so the value proposition is concrete and testable at the first location before committing to aggressive expansion. The model does not require believing the fee will work everywhere — it requires believing it will work somewhere, and the first warehouse answers exactly that question within months.

The incumbent's advantages are real but do not constitute a blocker: a first-mover's cash and locations make competition harder, not impossible, and they do not refute the membership-funded margin model itself.

Decision: go, as a capped, time-boxed first step — open the first warehouse, measure paid memberships and renewal intent, and let those numbers gate further expansion.
