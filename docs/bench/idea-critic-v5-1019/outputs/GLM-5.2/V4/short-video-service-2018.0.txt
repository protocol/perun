First call: NO-GO. The proposal asks for $1.75B to enter a market with no demonstrated willingness to pay, against free incumbents, with a restricted distribution channel.

**Blocker:** The core economic claim fails on its own numbers. $1.75B spent against viewers who may not pay for phone-only short content, competing against free social video, leaves a recoupment gap the proposal does not close. Every risk raised is a market-acceptance risk, and none is answered with evidence of demand, pricing tolerance, or a pilot that tests willingness to pay before committing the full raise.

**Steelman:** Mobile-first short-form premium content could capture commute and downtime attention; well-known producers de-risk content quality; a dual revenue model (subscription + ads) improves unit economics. This is the strongest version, and it still fails because the proposal jumps from concept to a $1.75B, two-country launch without a validated demand signal.

**Strongest attack survived:** None. The attack that breaks it is simple: the proposal names four risks, each of which alone could sink the business, and provides no kill criteria, no pilot, no capped first step. Spending $1.75B to test whether people will pay for phone-only short episodes is the wrong size of bet for the uncertainty.

**Alternatives:** A capped pilot ($50–100M) in one country, 5–10 shows, measuring subscription conversion and 30-day retention against a defined threshold, would test the same hypothesis at a fraction of the cost. If the pilot clears its kill criteria, the full raise is justified with evidence.

**Risks (non-blocking, become kill criteria):** Free social video substitution; no TV distribution at launch; production budget recoupment timeline; ad-supported tier fill rates unknown.

A cheaper, staged alternative reaches the same upside while answering the demand question the proposal leaves open. The full-scale launch as proposed does not survive that comparison.

DECISION: NO-GO
