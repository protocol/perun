First call: NO-GO. The format war is won by installed base of players and breadth of pre-recorded titles, not by picture quality alone. A proprietary, narrowly licensed format sacrifices exactly those levers to a rival already doing the opposite.

**Blocker:** The proposal's own evidence shows the rival licensing to many makers while this format stays closed. In a two-sided market (players + pre-recorded content), installed base is the moat. Fewer licensed manufacturers means fewer players on shelves, higher consumer prices, less retail penetration, and critically, less incentive for content owners to release titles. The rival's broad-licensing strategy directly attacks this. The proposal names the risk and answers it with "picture quality and early lead" — but early lead without distribution acceleration is a temporary advantage, and picture quality is a marginal consumer benefit against the utility of recording a full film or sports event. The one-hour tape limitation compounds the problem: it is not merely a risk but a product-fit failure for the primary use case (time-shifting long content). The rival offering longer tapes captures the use case this format cannot serve.

**Risks (kill criteria):** If pre-recorded title count falls below 50% of the rival's within 18 months, the format is losing the content war — pivot to broad licensing immediately. If fewer than 5 manufacturers are producing players within 12 months, distribution is too narrow — open the format. If the rival reaches 2× installed base of players within 24 months, the network effect has tipped — exit or join.

**Strongest attack survived:** "Picture quality wins consumers." Tested and failed: Betamax's superior picture did not prevent VHS dominance. Consumers prioritize recording duration and content availability over marginal quality gains. The proposal bets on a lever history shows loses format wars.

DECISION: NO-GO
