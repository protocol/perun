**First call: NO-GO.** The flaw is confirmed and the proposed gate (users prove they're affected) is one users cannot pass.

**Blockers:**
1. **The proposed solution is self-defeating on the company's own facts.** The proposal offers replacements only to users who "can show that their work is affected," while simultaneously acknowledging that "users cannot easily show that they are affected." The gate and the admission of the gate's impossibility are both in the proposal. This is not a risk; it is the plan failing as proposed.
2. **The flaw is confirmed, not theoretical.** Slightly wrong answers in floating-point division is a correctness defect in a product whose entire value proposition is computational accuracy. "Rare" is a frequency argument, not a safety argument.

**Risks (kill criteria, not blockers):**
- Reputation damage from appearing to hide a defect — this is already materializing ("widely discussed").
- Replacement cost for all chips is very large — true, but this is a known, bounded cost, not an existential one.
- The "once in many thousands of years" figure is the company's own analysis of its own defect; it has not survived independent verification.

**Strongest attack survived:** None. The strongest attack — that the proposal's own admission (users can't prove they're affected) makes its own remedy (replace only those who prove it) a dead letter — was not survived. It is the blocker.

**The asymmetry the proposal misses:** The cost of replacing all chips is large but finite and known. The cost of a reputation for hiding a confirmed correctness defect in a precision-computing product is unbounded and compounds. A company whose customers buy chips *because* they compute correctly cannot frame "we shipped wrong answers and made it hard to get a fix" as a reasonable risk trade. The replacement program should be unconditional and frictionless; the gating criterion proposed here is the defect, not the remedy.

DECISION: NO-GO
