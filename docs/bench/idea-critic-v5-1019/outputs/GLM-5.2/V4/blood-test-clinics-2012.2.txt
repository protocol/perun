**First call: NO-GO.** $350M exclusivity on an unvalidated, secret technology is a bet no operator should make as proposed.

**Steelman:** If the device works as claimed, the chain captures a high-margin health services category and a durable competitive moat for a fraction of the category's potential value.

**Skeptic — the blocker is in the proposal's own facts.** The technology has not been shown in published studies, the company will not share its methods, and regulators may later question the tests. The chain has no medical expertise to evaluate what it cannot see. The proposal asks for $350M and exclusivity while every fact that would justify it is absent. The company's refusal to share methods, combined with no published validation, means the chain is buying a black box at a premium price with no independent evidence it works.

**Better-way — do nothing beats this as proposed.** A staged alternative: pay nothing for exclusivity now; run a capped pilot (e.g., $5–10M) in a handful of stores, contingent on the company providing published peer-reviewed validation and regulatory clearance for the specific tests offered. If the pilot hits adoption and accuracy targets, expand. This captures the upside at a fraction of the risk and costs almost nothing to abandon.

**Kill-criteria:**
- No exclusivity commitment without published, peer-reviewed validation of the device's accuracy for each test offered.
- No rollout without explicit regulatory clearance for the specific tests.
- Pilot metric: test accuracy within X% of standard lab results across N samples, reviewed within 90 days.

**Pre-mortem:** The device does not work as claimed. The company has overpromised, the tests are inaccurate, patients are harmed, regulators intervene, and the chain has spent $350M to damage its brand in a category where it has no expertise to catch the problem early.

**Remaining risk:** Even with a pilot, the company's secrecy about methods means the chain may never fully understand what it is deploying.

DECISION: NO-GO
