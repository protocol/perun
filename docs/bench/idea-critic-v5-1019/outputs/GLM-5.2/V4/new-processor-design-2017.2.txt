First call: GO. The stated facts show a smaller player, behind on desktop performance after one miss, proposing more cores at lower prices on a new architecture. The upside (reclaim desktop competitiveness) beats do nothing, which cedes the segment.

Steelman: a new architecture with higher core counts at lower prices can win the price-performance tier where most buyers sit, even against a stronger rival.

Skeptic: the proposal's own evidence carries the sharpest risk — finances leave no room for a second miss, and the last all-new design underperformed. That is a risk, not a blocker: it becomes the kill criterion. Software adoption of extra cores is a known lagging variable, not a failure scenario by itself.

Better-way: do nothing is worse (continued decline). An incremental refresh on the existing architecture is cheaper and safer but unlikely to close a performance gap severe enough to warrant an all-new design — it does not reach the same upside. The proposed path is the strongest option on the stated facts.

Kill criteria: a capped, time-boxed first step. Name the leading metric (desktop performance-per-dollar against the rival's equivalent tier) with a review date inside the first quarter of availability. Define the kill threshold: if the new architecture fails to match or beat the rival's price-performance at launch, stop further investment in this architecture line. Pre-commit the pivot: fall back to an incremental refresh on proven architecture rather than doubling down on a second miss.

The strongest attack actually tried: the company cannot survive a second miss, so launching an all-new architecture is an existential bet on unproven silicon. This fails to block because the proposal already names the financial constraint — the rational response is to cap the bet and pre-commit the kill criterion, not to avoid the bet entirely. A smaller player behind on performance has no low-risk path to competitiveness; the question is whether the bet is sized to survive failure, and it can be.

DECISION: GO
