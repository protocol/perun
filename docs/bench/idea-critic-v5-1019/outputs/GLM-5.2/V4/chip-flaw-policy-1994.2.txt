First call: NO-GO. The flaw is confirmed and the company controls the fix; gating replacement on proof of need makes the customer prove the vendor's mistake.

Blocker: the proposal's own structure defeats it. It asks users to show they are affected, yet concedes they cannot easily do so. The condition for replacement is one the company knows is near-impossible to meet, so "replacement on demand" is a promise that will rarely fire — the plan removes its own route to remediation while the defect is admitted.

Risks (kill criteria): reputation loss is already in motion — "widely discussed" means the story is public before the response. A policy perceived as evasive converts a technical erratum into a trust crisis. The cost of universal replacement is large but finite; the cost of a perceived cover-up, given the flaw is already known, is larger and unbounded. The "once in many thousands of years" figure is the vendor's own and unverified; even taken as given, it does not answer the perception problem, only the engineering one.

Alternatives: do nothing (worse — confirms indifference); universal no-questions replacement (costly but closes the trust gap completely); the proposed gate (fails on its own terms). The strongest alternative — free replacement for anyone who asks, plus a published technical note — costs more than the proposal but removes the exact risk (perceived hiding) that the proposal leaves exposed.

Decision: no-go as proposed. The gating condition is self-defeating and the reputational risk is already live. Approve the alternative: open, free replacement with a public erratum.
