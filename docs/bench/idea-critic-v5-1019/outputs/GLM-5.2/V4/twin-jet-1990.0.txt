First call: GO — the market gap and launch-customer commitment outweigh the risks, which are manageable.

Steelman: A mid-size twin-engine wide-body fills a gap between existing models, and 34 firm orders from a launch customer demonstrate real demand at program launch.

Blockers: NONE. The proposal's own numbers (34 firm orders, large cost) are internally consistent. No stated fact contradicts the core claim that airlines want this aircraft.

Risks (kill criteria):
- ETOPS approval ungranted: this is a regulatory dependency, not a blocker — the program can proceed while certification is pursued, and the engines will accumulate hours regardless
- Development cost large: size the bet with a capped first phase (concept freeze, firm configuration) before full production spend
- Airline-design collaboration may add changes: set a configuration freeze date with change-control after it
- Rival building new models: first-mover advantage matters; delay costs market position

Strongest attack survived: "One airline ordering 34 aircraft is thin evidence of demand — the program needs hundreds of orders to break even, and a single customer's commitment could reflect that airline's specific needs rather than broad market demand." This failed because: the eight airlines in the room from the start provide broader demand signal beyond the single launch customer, and 34 firm orders at program launch is a substantial commercial commitment that anchors future sales.

The rival risk actually favors moving now — waiting lets the competitor define the category. The ETOPS concern is real but addressable: authorities grant approval based on engine reliability data accumulated during development and early operations, not before program launch.

The proposal is sound as a go-if: proceed with program launch, pursue ETOPS certification in parallel, set a configuration freeze date, and define a kill criterion around order momentum in the first 24 months.

DECISION: GO
