**First call: GO.** The previous generation's failures — late launch, expensive hardware, developer hostility — are all directly addressed by this proposal, and the core bet (price + developer ease + consumer-friendly policies) is coherent and executable.

**Steelman:** Undercut the rival on price while matching PC-like developer friendliness and preserving offline/used-game rights, to win back the mainstream buyer the previous generation lost.

**Strongest attack survived:** "A lower price destroys unit economics and the maker has no launch software, so it wins share but loses money with nothing to show for it." This fails because console economics are razor-and-blades: the install base is the asset, software attach and online-service revenue follow it, and a below-rival price is the one lever that moves the mainstream buyer at launch when the maker's software is thin. The margin hit is a feature, not a bug — it buys the installed base the previous generation never built.

**Blockers:** NONE. The proposal's own facts support it: the failures being fixed (lateness, cost, developer friction) are precisely what caused the lost ground, and the proposed fixes are direct inversions of those failures.

**Risks (kill criteria):**
- *Rival's online head start* — real, but the proposal does not need to win online day one; it needs to win the box. Kill criterion: if online attach at 12 months trails the rival by more than [X]%, accelerate the services roadmap rather than discounting further.
- *Thin launch software* — mitigated by price and by PC-like hardware lowering porting cost. Kill criterion: if attach rate at 6 months is below [Y], the first-party gap is the problem.
- *Consumer spending pullback* — a below-rival price is the correct response to a tighter buyer.

**Decision:** GO. The plan fixes the right things, the bet is the standard console model applied correctly, and delay only widens the rival's installed-base lead.
