**First call: NO-GO.** The numbers do not work as proposed.

**Blockers:**
- **Unit economics contradict the price.** A battery, motor, chassis, and three wheels assembled by an appliance maker — plus mail-order logistics — cannot clear a profit at sub-£400 in 1985. The proposal states the price as fixed; it does not state a margin. That gap is the blocker.
- **Mail order cannot deliver this product.** A 15 mph, 40 lb tricycle with no weather protection, sold in winter, delivered by post, returned by post. The channel is wrong for the product, the season, and the buyer's need to sit on it before paying.

**Risks (kill criteria if the plan is revised):**
- Top speed of 15 mph puts it in traffic too slow and on pavement too fast.
- No weather protection + British winter = a product launched into the season that kills demand.
- Safety groups objecting to a low-sitting vehicle is a regulatory risk, not a feature.
- "Several parts sold as optional extras" at this price point reads as unbundling what should be standard to hit the headline number.

**Strongest attack survived:** None. The attack that killed it is the one the proposal invites: *Can you build, ship, and support a motorised vehicle at a profit for under £400?* The proposal does not answer this, and the answer in 1985 is almost certainly no.

**Cheaper alternative:** Do not launch in January. Pilot through one dealer in spring at £499, with weather protection included. If 500 units sell in eight weeks at positive margin, scale. If not, stop.

The entrepreneur's instinct — a cheap, licence-free electric trike — is sound as a concept. The proposal as written is not.

DECISION: NO-GO
