First call: GO. The core fact is poisoned product reaching consumers; the cost of a death, and of being the company that waited, dwarfs any recall cost.

Steelman: even a local-only recall protects the immediate victims but leaves the distribution channel open to further tampering, further deaths, and a crisis that compounds daily. A nationwide withdrawal is the only action that severs the chain completely.

Do-nothing and local-only recall both leave the company betting that the poisoner is finished — a bet against lives with no evidence to support it. The nationwide withdrawal is more expensive but it is the only fully reversible safety action; doing nothing is the irreversible one.

Pre-mortem: the plan fails if the brand never recovers market share. But the alternative — deaths traced to a company that knew and kept shipping — is existential, not just financial. The cost of delay is measured in lives: every day the product stays on shelves is a day another bottle could be consumed.

The risks raised are real but all are financial or reputational. The risk of not acting — consumer deaths from a known contaminated supply chain — is moral and legal. No balance sheet weighs against a child's death. The Tylenol murders proved this: Johnson & Johnson's recall cost over $100 million but the brand recovered because the public saw a company that chose lives over profit.

Blockers: NONE. The proposal's own logic holds — the threat is unknown and potentially nationwide, so only a nationwide response is commensurate.

Kill criteria: relaunch only in tamper-resistant packaging; monitor for any new poisoning incident during the withdrawal (any new case means the withdrawal is incomplete or the poisoner is still active).

The strongest attack: the recall is wildly expensive for a problem that may be confined to one city. This fails because the proposal does not claim the problem is local — it acknowledges the source is unknown, and unknown means the conservative safety response is the only defensible one.

DECISION: GO
