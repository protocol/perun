**First call: NO-GO.** Installing hidden software on customers' computers to fight copying is a reputational and legal landmine that likely costs more in trust and liability than it saves in sales.

**Steelman:** Copying is a real revenue drain; passive anti-rip technology on the disc itself, disclosed plainly, could reduce casual copying without alienating buyers.

**Skeptic — assumptions:**
- Assumes hidden installation won't be discovered. With millions of discs shipped, discovery is near-certain — and the scandal is the discovery, not the flaw.
- Assumes the revenue saved exceeds the reputational and legal cost. No figure is offered.
- Assumes customers will tolerate being treated as suspects. The proposal itself flags this risk.

**Better-way:** Disc-level copy protection that requires no software installation, clearly disclosed on packaging, achieves much of the anti-copying goal without the stealth-installation liability. This is cheaper, reversible, and avoids the core objection.

**Kill-criteria:** If even one security researcher publicly demonstrates that the hidden software creates a vulnerability on customer machines, halt the rollout immediately and recall. The proposal lists this as a risk but provides no threshold or trigger for stopping.

**Blockers:**
1. **Self-defeating on its own facts.** The proposal's stated goal is protecting sales, yet it simultaneously raises that customers "may object to being treated as thieves." If customers object, they return discs, boycott the label, and file complaints — the cure worsens the disease by the proposal's own admission, and nothing in the plan resolves that tension.
2. **Hidden installation is a liability the plan doesn't price.** Software that "installs itself" without "clear consent" is not merely "intrusive" — it is the kind of undisclosed system modification that invites class-action litigation and regulatory scrutiny. The proposal softens this to a risk; the facts state it as the design.

**Risks:** Legal exposure from undisclosed installation; reputational damage if security flaws are found; customer backlash from treating buyers as suspects; potential regulatory action under computer-fraud and consumer-protection statutes.

**Strongest attack survived:** None — the proposal fails its own test.

DECISION: NO-GO
