First call: GO. The proposal attacks every cause of the prior failure directly.

Steelman: by converging on familiar PC hardware, undercutting the rival on price, and removing the restrictions consumers hated, the maker can win back developers and players even from behind.

Blockers: NONE. No fact stated contradicts the core claim. The prior generation was late, expensive and hard to develop for; this plan is on-time, cheaper and developer-friendly. The rival's head start is real but does not block the move — it is the reason to differentiate on price, openness and simplicity, not a reason to stay the course.

Risks (each becomes a kill criterion):
- Margin per unit falls. Kill criterion: attach rate and digital revenue per unit at month 6; if below the prior generation's figure, raise price or bundle.
- Weak launch software. Kill criterion: Metacritic median of exclusive launch titles below 75 triggers a pricing or marketing shift.
- Rival's online lead. Leading metric: monthly active users on the maker's network at month 6, reviewed against the rival's published figure.
- Consumer spending softening. Kill criterion: sell-through below the prior generation's first-holiday pace by more than 15% triggers a price cut funded by software revenue.

The four risks raised are the right risks, and each has a measurable tripwire. The plan is reversible on price (can cut or hold), partially reversible on hardware (cannot change silicon post-launch, but the PC-like architecture is itself the low-regret choice), and two-way on policy (used games and offline play can be adjusted). The cost of delay is high: every quarter late extends the rival's installed-base advantage and cements developer habits around the competitor's tools.

A cheaper alternative — match the rival's price and restrictions — reaches less upside: it concedes differentiation and asks developers to port to harder hardware for no reward. Do-nothing concedes the generation.

DECISION: GO
