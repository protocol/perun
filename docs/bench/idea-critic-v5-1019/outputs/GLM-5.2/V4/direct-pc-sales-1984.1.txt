**First call: GO.** Direct-to-buyer assembly undercuts branded retail on price; the model funds itself from revenue and stays reversible.

**Steelman.** A direct-order assembler captures the branded-retail margin as lower price; with no stores it stays asset-light and grows from cash.

**Skeptic.** "Buyers want to try before buying" is a preference, not a blocker — the low price and direct relationship are the test. "No service network" is a real gap but also a solvable one (mail-in, on-site contract). "Parts on credit" matters only if growth outpaces cash; the proposal already says grow from revenue. "Established makers have large channels" is exactly the cost the direct model avoids.

**Better-way.** Do nothing: keeps the student at hobby scale, forgoes the margin capture. Sell through one reseller: adds a markup layer and defeats the price advantage. Neither beats the direct model on the stated facts.

**Kill criteria.** Cash conversion cycle exceeds 30 days; defect/return rate exceeds 8%; gross margin falls below 15%.

**Blockers: NONE.** Risks: brand trust gap, no service network, supplier credit terms, channel competition. All become kill criteria, none blocks.

**Strongest attack survived:** "No stores means no service network, and buyers won't trust a brandless assembler." This fails because the direct model's lower price *is* the trust mechanism — buyers accept some risk for meaningful savings — and service can be contracted rather than owned. The proposal's own logic (grow from revenue) already answers the credit concern.

**Decision: GO.** The model is asset-light, reversible, and self-funding. The risks are real but addressable; the price advantage is structural.

DECISION: GO
