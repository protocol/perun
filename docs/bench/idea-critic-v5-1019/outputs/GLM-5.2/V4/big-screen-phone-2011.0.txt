First call: GO. A premium niche device at this scale is a cheap, reversible bet on an unmet segment.

Blockers: NONE.
Risks: reviewer mockery, stylus indifference, no-man's-land sizing, internal cannibalization.

The sharpest attack: the device falls in a dead zone — too big for a phone, too small for a tablet — so it replaces nothing and serves no one. This fails because "replaces nothing" is a feature, not a bug: it creates a category rather than splitting an existing one. The maker's own line already covers phones and (presumably) tablets; a premium intermediate device captures buyers who want more screen without carrying a second device. Cannibalization is the weaker form of this objection: a sale taken from your own 4-inch phone is still a sale kept in-house, likely at higher margin.

The stylus objection is real but not fatal. Reviewers may mock it; the question is whether *buyers* reject it. A premium price point and stylus differentiation target a segment willing to pay for precision input — artists, note-takers, business users. If the stylus is optional or unobtrusive, it need not alienate the base.

The reviewer-size objection is the weakest. Reviewers said 4 inches was too big once. Phone screens have been growing. The trend supports, not undermines, this bet.

Kill criteria: sales below a stated threshold within the first two quarters triggers discontinuation or repositioning. The maker should set that number before launch. Leading metric: units sold per week in the first month, reviewed at day 30.

This is a one-way-ish door (hardware inventory, brand positioning), but the blast radius is contained: it sits *alongside* the existing line, not replacing it. If it fails, the existing line is intact. The cost of the attempt is one device cycle; the cost of not trying is ceding a growing segment to a competitor who moves first.

DECISION: GO
