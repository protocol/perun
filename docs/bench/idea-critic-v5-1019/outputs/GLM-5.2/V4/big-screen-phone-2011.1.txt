First call: GO. The stated risks are industry-wide anxieties, not blockers; every objection is a known trade-off that a premium launch can absorb.

Blockers: NONE. No figure in the proposal contradicts itself. No fact already in hand shows the target users do not want it. The risks are "may" scenarios, not negatives stated as already true.

Risks (become kill criteria): reviewer discomfort is a perception problem, not a demand problem. The stylus concern is real but narrow — it assumes the stylus is the selling point rather than a differentiator for a specific use case (note-taking, precision input). The "too big for a phone, too small for a tablet" framing assumes the device must replace one or the other; the proposal positions it as a premium companion device, which is a different claim. Cannibalization of the maker's own phones is the sharpest risk: if the large phone pulls buyers from higher-margin existing models, net revenue falls. But this is a reason to price it premium and cap the initial run, not a reason to abort.

Kill criteria: initial sell-through below a stated threshold within the first 60 days → stop or cut price. Reviewer sentiment is a leading indicator but not a kill metric on its own. The cannibalization question needs a capped pilot (limited geographic launch, bounded units) so the maker learns the cross-elasticity before a full roll-out.

Strongest attack survived: "The device falls in the dead zone between phone and tablet — too large to pocket comfortably, too small to serve as a real tablet — so it satisfies neither existing category and sells to almost no one." This fails because it assumes the only buyers are people seeking to replace a phone or a tablet. The proposal's premium positioning targets users who want a third device that does things neither a four-inch phone nor a tablet does well, and the stylus is the mechanism for that. The category may not exist yet, but that is a risk to cap and pilot, not a blocker.

DECISION: GO
