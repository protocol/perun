First call: GO. The cost of a death is unbounded; the cost of a recall is finite.

Steelman: the maker's core asset is trust, not the product itself. Tampering occurred post-factory, so the product was not defective—but trust dies the moment a second victim appears and the public learns the company knew and kept shipping.

Skeptic: the proposal rests on one assumption—that the poisonings are not isolated and further harm is plausible. Given an unknown actor, an unknown motive, and a metro area with millions of bottles already in homes, that assumption holds. The "may be local" objection fails because the company cannot verify the attacker's reach, and the downside of waiting is a child's death on the front page beside the headline "they knew."

Better-way: do nothing and cooperate with police. Cheaper today, fatal if a second incident occurs. Partial recall of the metro area? The attacker's scope is unknown; a partial recall gambles lives to save money. There is no cheaper alternative that reaches the same upside—protecting the public and preserving long-term trust.

Kill criteria: relaunch in tamper-resistant packaging within a defined window; restore baseline market share by a set date; zero further poisonings after recall completes. If share is not recovered, pivot to a new brand identity on the same formula.

Pre-mortem: the plan fails if the brand never recovers and competitors permanently take the shelf space. But a dead customer buys nothing. The one-way door here is not the recall—it is inaction. A recall is reversible (relaunch); a death is not.

Blockers: NONE. The stated risks (cost, share loss, competitor entry, danger signal) are all real but are costs of acting, not blockers. The numbers work: finite recall cost vs. unbounded liability and brand destruction.

DECISION: GO
