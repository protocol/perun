**First call: NO-GO.** The proposal's own risks, taken as stated facts, already remove its route to upside.

**Blockers:**

1. **Self-defeating on its own evidence.** The stated goal is *protecting sales*. The stated mechanism is *software that installs without clear consent*. Those two facts collide: customers who discover hidden software on their computers will trust the label less, not more. The very feature that is supposed to restore revenue is the one the proposal itself flags as intrusive. A plan whose own listed risks negate its core claim fails as proposed.

2. **One-way door with no rollback.** "Hard to remove" is stated as a property of the software, not a risk that might materialize. Shipping millions of discs with software that cannot be cleanly uninstalled is an irreversible deployment. There is no kill criterion, no pilot, no cap. The proposal commits to a one-way door and names no tripwire.

3. **Security researchers are named as a near-certainty.** "Could find weaknesses" is framed as a risk, but given the scale (millions of discs) and the hidden-install design, exposure is a when, not an if. The proposal has no answer for what happens when a vulnerability in hidden, hard-to-remove, auto-installing software is published on millions of machines.

**Risks (become kill criteria if revised):** legal exposure under existing computer-trespass statutes; brand damage that dwarfs any piracy recovered; no measurable success metric defined.

**Strongest attack survived:** none — the strongest attack (the proposal's own risks, taken as already-true facts, contradict its core claim) was not survived, it is the reason for NO-GO.

DECISION: NO-GO
