**First call: GO.** Eleven profitable quarters fund a bet; a premium, design-led niche product does not need to beat the tower on price or slots to succeed.

**Steelman:** A recovered maker can use industrial design to create a premium niche product that attracts buyers who value form, silence, and aesthetics over raw specs and expandability.

**Alternatives:** Do nothing (safe, forfeits a premium-design market segment and brand halo). Cheaper tower refresh (lower risk, but competes on price/specs, not design). The acrylic fanless design is the only path to that specific premium segment.

**Pre-mortem:** It fails if the acrylic case cannot be manufactured flawlessly at scale — visible defects in clear plastic are unforgivable. The stated manufacturing difficulty is the core execution risk.

**Evidence check:** The proposal's own risks confirm the price gap, lack of slots, and manufacturing difficulty. These are acknowledged, not hidden. The tower is faster and cheaper; the all-in-one is cheaper. The product's entire thesis is that design and silence command a premium over specs — a thesis that is testable with a capped first run.

**Blockers:** NONE. No stated fact contradicts the core claim. The manufacturing risk is real but not shown to be unsolvable. The price/feature gap is the product's positioning, not a flaw.

**Risks → kill criteria:**
- Manufacturing defect rate above threshold in pilot run → fix process or kill.
- Sales below a named unit target within the first two quarters → discontinue.
- Gross margin below target at the stated price → kill or reprice.

**Strongest attack survived:** "It costs more than a faster tower with expansion slots, so no rational buyer chooses it." This fails because the product competes on design, silence, and aesthetics — not on price-per-feature — and premium niches are sustained by exactly this kind of differentiation. The Power Mac G4 Cube's later commercial struggles were an execution and pricing problem, not proof the thesis was wrong.

DECISION: GO
