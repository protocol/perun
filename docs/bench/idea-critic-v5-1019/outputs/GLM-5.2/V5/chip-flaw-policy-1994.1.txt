First call: GO. The stated frequency makes universal replacement hard to justify, and the policy is reversible if evidence changes.

Steelman: a rare, low-severity flaw need not trigger a blanket recall; targeted replacement serves genuinely affected users without a very large cost.

Strongest attack survived: "Users cannot easily prove they are affected, so the gate is effectively closed — the policy hides the defect behind an impossible bar." This fails against the stated facts only because the proposal is a policy stance, not a final logistics design: the gate can be opened by offering replacement to anyone who asks, or by publishing a test. The objection reshapes the first step; it does not block the call.

Blockers: NONE. No fact stated shows the plan fails as proposed. The numbers are not contradicted; the cost of universal replacement is stated as very large; the flaw frequency is stated as rare.

Risks (become kill criteria): users cannot easily show harm (the real defect in the gate); wide public discussion means the stance is already public; reputation damage if buyers believe the company is hiding a defect; universal replacement cost.

Kill criteria: (1) publish a reproducible test so any user can check their own work — this dissolves the "cannot show affected" objection; (2) replacement offered to any user who runs the test and sees a mismatch, not only to those who prove impact; (3) a named owner statement acknowledging the flaw and the offer, dated, so the company is on record as not hiding it.

Leading metric: fraction of affected users who receive replacement within 30 days; review date one month after the statement.

The core uncertainty is whether the flaw is as rare as the company's own analysis claims. The proposal does not answer that; it assumes the analysis is correct. That is the one fact that, if wrong, reverses the recommendation.

DECISION: GO
