**First call: NO-GO.** The proposal's own facts show it loses: customers are asked to buy a *more expensive* player that locks them into *paying again* for discs they already own, while the standard format they already have lets them watch indefinitely for free. The value proposition is inverted.

**Steelman.** The strongest version: a few-dollar disc that self-destructs after 48 hours could capture the rental market at lower cost than physical rental stores, if studios and electronics makers back it.

**Attack.** That steelman fails on the proposal's own facts. The format requires *special* players costing *more* than standard ones. So the customer must: buy a pricier player, buy discs that expire, and pay again to rewatch — all to replace a standard player and rental-store combo that already works. No partner agreement exists; it is listed as a risk, not a fact in hand. The pre-mortem writes itself: customers compare the special player's total cost against a standard player and a few rentals, and never buy the hardware. The format dies for lack of installed base, studios withdraw, and the investment is sunk.

**Alternatives.** Do nothing: the retailer keeps its stores and margin. Cheaper path: rent standard-format discs through existing stores — same rental revenue, no new hardware, no partner consortium. This reaches the stated upside (rental market participation) at a fraction of the cost and risk.

**Blockers:** the proposal's own facts show the target users have a cheaper, unrestricted alternative already in hand (standard players + rental stores), and the plan does not answer why they would buy costlier locked hardware. That removes the route to the upside.

**Risks:** no studio or electronics-maker commitment; discs incompatible with installed base; customers reject repeat fees. These are real but secondary — the blocker is the inverted value proposition.

DECISION: NO-GO
