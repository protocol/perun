**First call: GO.** Brand-extension bets with a strong platform partner and a loyal base are exactly the calls worth making — the upside (new revenue tier, dealer traffic, surviving a segment shift) dominates the risks, which are real but manageable.

**Steelman.** A sports-car brand entering the fastest-growing, highest-margin segment with shared cost and a loyal base willing to pay a premium is a defensible bet on category expansion.

**Alternatives.** Do nothing: safe, but cedes the segment and leaves the brand a single-category specialist. Cheaper version: undercuts the premium positioning and the margin case. This proposal, as the most ambitious option, reaches the highest upside.

**Fit.** A loyal enthusiast following is the precondition for a high-priced launch: they tolerate the stretch if the product delivers the brand promise.

**Pre-mortem.** The bet fails if loyalists defect *and* luxury-SUV buyers reject the brand. The proposal acknowledges the first risk; the second is the core uncertainty that only a launch can settle.

**Blockers: NONE.** Risks: brand dilution, high first-year cost, image damage, strong incumbents. **Kill criteria:** first-year sales below the break-even volume; enthusiast-retention rate below the pre-launch baseline at month 12; net brand-perception score below the pre-launch level at month 18. Pivot or stop: if the crossover fails to hit the sales threshold by year 1, reassess the segment or withdraw; if brand metrics fall, reposition or exit. Leading metric to watch: pre-order conversion rate among existing customers at month 3. Review date: end of year 1.

**Strongest attack survived.** The loyalist-betrayal risk is the sharpest objection — but it is a *risk*, not a *blocker*: the proposal states it as a concern, not a fact already proven, and the kill criteria (enthusiast retention, brand perception) directly measure and bound it. A sound plan wrongly blocked costs as much as a weak one wrongly passed.

DECISION: GO
