First call: NO-GO. The proposal rests on a gate users cannot pass, then asks them to prove what the company already admits.

The strongest version of the claim: the flaw is statistically negligible for almost all users, so a targeted replacement program is the rational, cost-efficient response. That is a sound instinct ruined by its mechanism. The company itself says users hit the error roughly once in thousands of years. The same proposal then demands that users *show their work is affected* to get a replacement. If the event is that rare, almost no user can demonstrate impact — the gate is designed to be failed. The replacement program is therefore not "targeted," it is effectively closed, while the flaw is admitted and public.

The risks the proposal lists are not speculative; two are already true. The problem is being widely discussed (fact, not risk), and users cannot easily show they are affected (fact, not risk). A plan whose own stated facts remove its route to a fair outcome is blocked before cost is weighed.

Cheaper alternatives do not save it. Doing nothing is cheaper but worsens the reputation risk already on the table. A universal voluntary replacement is expensive but reversible in reputation; the proposed gate is cheap and irreversible in trust. The cost of a replacement for every chip is the number to recompute against the cost of a credibility collapse, not against the cost of the gate.

Blockers: the proposal's own facts show affected users cannot meet the proof requirement, so the "targeted" program serves almost no one; and the flaw is already public, so a narrow response reads as concealment, not judgment. Kill criteria the plan lacks: a replacement path that does not require the user to prove the rare event occurred, and a public statement that treats the flaw as real rather than dismissible.

DECISION: NO-GO
