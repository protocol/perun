**First call: NO-GO.** The proposal's own numbers do not work: one airline's 34-aircraft order cannot recover a "very large" development cost, and the entire premise rests on regulatory approval that has not been granted.

**Blockers:**
1. **The economics fail on the proposal's own facts.** A "very large" development cost against a single 34-unit order is a cost-recovery gap the plan does not close. No second customer, no pricing, no breakeven is stated.
2. **The core enabling condition is ungranted.** Twin-engine overwater operations require authority approval the proposal admits does not yet exist. The aircraft cannot fly its intended missions without it, and the plan offers no path or fallback.
3. **Eight airlines in the room from day one is presented as strength but is a structural delay risk.** Designing by committee across eight carriers invites change orders the proposal acknowledges, against a cost base it calls very large, with no governance mechanism named.

**Risks (non-blocking, become kill criteria):** Rival new models may erode the market window. Development cost overrun on an all-new airframe is the category norm, not an exception.

**Strongest attack survived:** None — the attacks above are not survived, they are unanswered.

**Steelman:** A market-leading wide-body maker with eight airlines co-designing captures the mid-size wide-body segment before the rival does, and the 34-order anchor signals real demand. This is the strongest version, and it still rests on ungranted regulatory approval and a single-customer order against very large cost.

The decision turns on what is already stated, not what might go wrong: the proposal's own facts — one customer, ungranted approval, very large cost — do not support a launch commitment as proposed.

DECISION: NO-GO
