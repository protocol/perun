First call: GO. The previous launch failed on price, complexity and restrictions; this proposal reverses all three. The rival's advantage is precisely in the areas being ceded (online services, premium pricing), making differentiation on openness and affordability the strongest available play.

The strongest attack: the rival's online head start is the decisive factor, and a cheaper, more open box cannot overcome it. This fails because the proposal does not compete on online services; it competes on the dimensions where the rival is vulnerable. Developers want easy hardware; consumers want used games and offline play; a lower price removes the friction that hurt the previous generation.

The margin objection is real but secondary. Console economics run on software attach and licensing, not launch hardware margin. Taking a per-unit hit to rebuild the installed base is the standard playbook, and the alternative (premium pricing against a stronger rival) repeats the exact failure being addressed.

The risks named (online gap, thin launch lineup, consumer spending pressure) are genuine but none is a blocker. They are conditions the launch must survive, and the kill criteria below address them.

Kill criteria: attach rate below 3.0 software units per console in the first holiday quarter triggers a pricing or bundling reassessment. Third-party developer adoption below the rival's launch window by the end of year one triggers a developer-relations escalation. Online service parity not reached by year two triggers a resource reallocation.

Leading metric to watch: weekly console sell-through versus the rival's comparable window, reviewed at the first quarterly earnings call.

DECISION: GO
