**First call: NO-GO.** The economics don't work as stated.

**Steelman:** Urban density plus direct employment gives speed and control that competitors can't match; scale brings per-delivery costs down fast enough to cover small-order margins.

**Attack:** The proposal's own numbers defeat it. Free delivery of a $3 snack costs more in courier time than the product margin — and the proposal explicitly names this risk without answering it. Scale doesn't fix it: the cost gap is per-order, structural, and grows as you attract the small-order customers the model invites. The "expand quickly" plan deepens the loss in every new city simultaneously, with no kill criterion named.

**Alternatives:** Charge delivery fees (kills the value proposition but stops the bleed); minimum order (retains speed, loses casual users); staged single-city pilot with measured unit economics before any expansion. The last is strictly cheaper and reaches the same learnings.

**Blockers:**
1. The proposal states free delivery of small orders costs more than the margin — and proposes free delivery with no minimum. This is the plan contradicting its own core claim.
2. "Expand to more cities quickly" commits to multiplying a model whose unit economics are already negative and unverified, with no pilot gate.

**Risks:** Customer subsidy-dependence (named, unaddressed); direct employment overhead per city; no stated metric or threshold to stop.

**Kill criteria (if revised):** Single-city pilot, 90 days. Stop if blended contribution margin per delivery is negative after week 8, or if repeat-order rate from non-subsidized customers falls below 30%. Leading metric: average revenue per delivery against average cost per delivery, reviewed weekly.

The idea isn't wrong — rapid urban delivery has real demand. But approving it *as proposed* means funding simultaneous multi-city expansion of a model whose own listed risks show it loses money on every small order, with no pilot, cap, or kill metric. Revise to a capped single-city pilot with measured unit economics before any expansion commitment.

DECISION: NO-GO
