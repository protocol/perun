First call: NO-GO. Treats paying customers as hostile endpoints; the stated risks are all core-claim, not side-effects.

**Blockers:** (1) The target users are paying customers, not copiers — the proposal's own facts show it harms the revenue base it claims to protect. (2) Consent is absent ("installs without clear consent") — a stated negative that removes the plan's own legitimacy route and invites legal exposure the proposal does not answer. (3) The four "risks" are all core-claim: consent, removability, security, customer trust each concern whether the mechanism can work or be accepted. Three kill-check questions return three yeses for each.

**Risks (kill criteria):** Stop if uninstall-related support calls exceed X within 30 days; stop if a security vulnerability is published; stop if retail returns rise above baseline. Leading metric: customer-complaint volume in the first two weeks; review date 30 days post-launch.

**Assumptions:** That copying is the primary driver of sales decline (unproven). That technical copy-protection can defeat determined copiers (historically false). That the legal environment of 2005 tolerates undisclosed rootkit-style installation (not verified, likely wrong).

**Better ways:** (1) Do nothing — copying may be a smaller factor than assumed, and the cure is worse than the disease. (2) Compete on value: bonus content, lower prices, digital bundles. (3) Pursue legal/enforcement channels against large-scale copiers rather than installing software on every buyer's machine. All three avoid the consent, security, and trust failures that are already stated as facts in the proposal.

The plan's own stated facts — installs without clear consent, hard to remove, security vulnerabilities likely, customers treated as thieves — are not risks to mitigate later; they are the mechanism. Approving as proposed means approving the harm to the paying customer base as the primary cost of a measure whose technical efficacy against copying is itself unproven.

DECISION: NO-GO
