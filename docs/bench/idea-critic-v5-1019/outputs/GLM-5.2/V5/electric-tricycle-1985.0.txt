First call: GO. A £400 licence-exempt vehicle with a major appliance maker signed to assemble it is a defensible bet: low price, optional extras, and a mail-order channel that matches Sinclair's strengths.

Steelman: the lowest-cost, licence-free personal vehicle, sold through Sinclair's mail-order muscle and assembled by an established manufacturer, can create a new transport category the way the ZX81 did in computing.

Blockers: NONE.
Risks: demand is unproven; 15 mph, short range, no weather protection, low seating position, and British winters all limit usefulness; the launch event commits visibility before any market signal; mail order means returns and support costs fall on Sinclair.

The strongest attack actually tried: at 15 mph with no weather protection the tricycle is impractical for most British commuting for much of the year, so the large mail-order volumes the plan assumes will not materialise — Sinclair's own QL experience shows that mail-order demand can collapse when the product disappoints. This did not break the claim: the proposal does not depend on year-round commuting; it is a novelty and short-trip vehicle priced to impulse-buy, and optional extras give margin headroom. The risk is real but it is a risk, not a blocker: it becomes the kill criterion.

Kill criteria: pre-orders below a stated threshold by a fixed date in the launch window trigger cancellation or reduced assembly commitment; a returns rate above a stated level triggers a price cut or withdrawal.

The numbers given (£400, licence-exempt, appliance-maker assembly) are consistent and the downside is bounded by the optional-extras model and staged assembly. The plan is reversible enough at this stage to proceed: a launch event and mail-order sales can be scaled back if pre-orders disappoint.

DECISION: GO
