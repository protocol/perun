**First call: GO.** A cheap dongle that uses the phone you already own is a smaller, faster, cheaper bet than another full TV device.

**Steelman.** The phone is the remote and the menu; the dongle is just a dumb pipe. That removes the most expensive and most confusing parts of a TV device and lets the open kit fill the app gap over time.

**Attack — the remote objection.** "No remote confuses buyers" assumes a dongle needs its own remote. The proposal's own model is that the phone *is* the remote — something every buyer already carries and knows. The objection attacks a device the plan does not build.

**Attack — margin.** "Almost no margin" matters for a business whose revenue is the box. Here the box is an acquisition channel for the services; the dongle can lose money per unit and still win if it grows paid subscribers. The stated facts do not show the services' LTV is too small to cover a subsidized dongle.

**Attack — apps and incumbents.** Few launch apps and strong incumbents are real risks, but not blockers: the open kit is the stated answer to the app gap, and incumbents with large catalogs have not yet made the phone-as-remote model standard, so the wedge is open.

**Blockers: NONE.** The proposal's own facts support the model: phone-as-remote removes the confusion source, services revenue can cover thin hardware margin, and the open kit addresses the app gap over time. No stated fact contradicts the core claim.

**Risks:** app catalog gap at launch; incumbent response; dongle-margin pressure if services attach is low.

**Kill criteria:** developer-kit adoption below target by month 6; services attach rate below threshold by month 9; subscriber LTV not covering dongle subsidy by month 12.

**Decision:** go. One-way door? No — a cheap dongle is reversible; stopping production or pivoting the model is cheap within the first year. Price of delay: incumbents extend their catalog lead and own the phone-to-TV flow before this ships.

DECISION: GO
