**First call: NO-GO.** The unit economics are broken on the proposal's own facts: paying theaters full ticket price while charging ~$10/month means every member who sees more than one film a month is a loss, and the "grow rapidly" strategy deliberately maximizes the number of such loss-making members.

**Steelman:** Scale unlocks leverage — studios pay for audience data and marketing access, theaters accept revenue sharing to fill seats, and the resulting network effects make the model defensible. At millions of subscribers, the data and partnership revenue could exceed the per-member ticket subsidy.

**Attack on the steelman:** The proposal states the new revenue streams are *unproven* — yet the plan commits to subsidizing every ticket at full price *now*, betting that unproven revenue arrives before the subsidy buries the company. Heavy users are not an edge case; they are the predictable consequence of a one-film-a-day offer, and each one costs more than they pay. Theater cooperation is named as a risk, but theaters have no reason to accept a middleman extracting data and partnership revenue while the subscription company's own model cannot cover ticket costs.

**Blockers:** The fee does not cover the cost of the tickets the company must pay, and the proposal's own facts state the offsetting revenue is unproven. That is the plan failing on its own numbers, not a risk to manage.

**Risks (kill criteria if pursued):** Heavy-user cost exceeds subsidy revenue — cap monthly views or require a minimum subscription period. Theater chains refuse cooperation — secure at least one major chain agreement before the price cut.

The core claim — that data and partnership revenue will cover per-member ticket subsidies — rests on unproven revenue against certain, compounding cost. The proposal does not answer how it survives the gap between launching the subsidy and earning the revenue it bets on.

DECISION: NO-GO
