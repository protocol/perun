**First call: GO.** People are dying from your product. Everything else is cost; this is survival.

**Steelman:** A nationwide withdrawal costs more than a local one, but the alternative — leaving poisoned bottles on shelves while you investigate — is corporate homicide. The brand's value is trust; selling a product that is killing people destroys that trust faster than any recall.

**The attack that matters:** The proposal withdraws nationwide but the company's own plants are unaffected — the tampering happened *after* the factory. So the product was safe when it left you. Why pull everything? Because "safe when we made it" is not a defense when bottles are killing people in stores. The local-only option leaves poisoned inventory on shelves in other areas you haven't checked yet. You don't know the scope. Withdrawing everything is the only response that doesn't gamble with lives.

**Alternatives:**
- *Local recall only:* cheaper, but you don't know if other areas are affected. A second death in another city ends the company.
- *Do nothing:* the product is killing people. No.
- *Investigate first, then decide:* every hour of investigation is an hour a poisoned bottle sits on a shelf.

**Pre-mortem:** The company did nothing and more people died. The brand became synonymous with murder. Or: the company recalled but slowly, and the delay looked like indifference.

**Kill criteria:** None that override the moral and legal obligation. The financial risks are real but secondary to the fact that people are dying.

**The sharpest objection I tried:** "This is overreaction; the product is safe, the tampering is local, and the cost is catastrophic." It fails because the company cannot *know* the tampering is local — it knows only that it happened after the factory. Uncertainty about scope, when lives are at stake, demands maximum withdrawal, not minimum.

The cost of being wrong about withdrawing (lost money, lost share) is recoverable. The cost of being wrong about not withdrawing (more deaths) is not.

DECISION: GO
