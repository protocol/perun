**First call: NO-GO.** The economics don't work at the stated scale.

**Blockers:**

1. **The numbers don't work.** $1.75B raised, high-budget commissions, a paid tier plus ad-supported tier, launching in only two countries. The production spend alone — spread across enough short-form content to justify a subscription — leaves a recoupment gap that two-country subscription revenue plus ad revenue cannot plausibly close in any reasonable timeframe. The proposal itself acknowledges the budgets leave a lot to recoup.

2. **The target users don't want it.** Free short video on social platforms is already popular and entrenched. The proposal asks people to pay for something they already get free, in a format (under ten minutes, phone-only, no TV at launch) that is strictly worse than what the free alternatives offer. A paid product must be meaningfully better than the free substitute; this one is meaningfully worse — no TV, shorter than what social offers, and behind a paywall.

3. **The core claim is unproven at the committed scale.** The bet rests on viewers paying for phone-only short episodes. That is an untested category. Committing $1.75B to an unproven demand model, with no pilot, no smaller launch, and no kill criterion, is a one-way door sized too large to stop once it fails.

**Risks (become kill criteria if pursued):** free-substitute dominance, no TV at launch, production-budget recoupment.

**Alternative:** a capped, time-boxed pilot in one country with a small production budget, measuring conversion from free to paid and ad revenue per user. If the pilot survives its kill metric, scale. The full $1.75B launch as proposed does not survive the attack.

DECISION: NO-GO
