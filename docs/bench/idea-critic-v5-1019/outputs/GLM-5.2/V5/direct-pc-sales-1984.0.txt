First call: GO. The gross margins on direct-to-customer assembly are high enough to self-fund growth, and the capital required to start is minimal since parts are bought to order. The business already works at small scale; incorporation mainly limits liability.

Steelman: a direct-only assembler can undercut branded PCs on price while matching them on the components that matter, because it avoids dealer markup and retail overhead. The customer pays for the machine, not the channel.

The strongest attack: buyers will not purchase a computer they cannot see or try, and a no-store business cannot offer service, so demand collapses. This fails to break the claim. The proposal already concedes these risks, yet the business is already selling — so some segment buys without a showroom. Service is a real gap, but mail-order and on-site part-swap models exist, and the low price is the wedge that makes the trade-off acceptable to price-sensitive buyers. The supplier-credit risk is mitigated by the direct model itself: customers pay first, parts are bought against confirmed orders, working capital turns fast rather than tying up inventory.

No blocker found. The numbers work: no retail rent, no dealer margin, no inventory carry, parts bought to order. The risks are real but they are risks, not facts that already defeat the plan. They become kill criteria.

Kill criteria: if gross margin per unit falls below the cost of acquiring that customer (ad spend plus support), stop and reconsider the direct-only model. Leading metric: repeat and referral rate within the first six months — if customers who buy once do not send others, the no-showroom model is not viable at scale.

DECISION: GO
