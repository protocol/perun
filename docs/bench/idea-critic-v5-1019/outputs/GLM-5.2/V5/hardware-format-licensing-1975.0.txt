**First call: NO-GO.** The proposal bets on quality and early lead while ceding the two things that decide a format war: breadth of hardware and breadth of titles. The rival's strategy attacks exactly those axes, and the proposal has no answer to it.

**Steelman:** Superior picture quality plus a one-year lead plus controlled manufacturing can establish a premium standard that partners rally around. Attack that: a format standard is won by installed base and content availability, not by picture quality alone. The rival licensing to many makers directly grows installed base and drives down prices; the proposal's few-partner model narrows both. One-hour tapes cannot capture a full film or sports event — a concrete product limitation on a device whose primary use is recording television. Fewer manufacturers means fewer pre-recorded titles, which means fewer reasons to buy the player, which is a negative feedback loop the proposal does not address.

**Blockers:** The rival's many-maker licensing strategy will produce more players at lower prices than the proposal's few-partner model — the proposal's own facts concede this. The proposal relies on picture quality and early lead, but its own listed risks show the rival winning on installed base, price, content, and standard-setting. The proposal names the threat and has no counter.

**Risks:** One-hour tape capacity is a product-fitness gap, not a market-fate question, but it compounds the content problem. Early lead is a depreciating asset if the rival is already preparing.

**Kill criteria:** If rival-format player count exceeds proprietary-format player count within 18 months, or if pre-recorded title count for the rival format exceeds the proprietary format within 24 months, the proprietary strategy has failed and must be opened.

The proposal as stated concedes the competitive dynamic that will defeat it. Opening licensing broadly, extending tape capacity, and competing on installed base rather than exclusivity is the stronger play.

DECISION: NO-GO
