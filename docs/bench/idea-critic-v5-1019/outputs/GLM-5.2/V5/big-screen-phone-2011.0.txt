**First call: GO.** The downside is bounded and reversible; the upside creates a new category at premium margins.

The strongest attack: this device falls into a no-man's-land — too big to be a phone, too small to be a tablet — satisfying neither user fully, and the stylus is a niche feature abandoned by the market. That attack fails because the proposal doesn't need *most* buyers to want a stylus or a 5+ inch screen; it needs enough premium buyers to justify a SKU that also differentiates the brand. The "cannibalization" risk is a feature, not a bug: if the large phone steals sales from the maker's own smaller phones, that means buyers wanted larger screens, and the maker captured that demand rather than ceding it to a competitor.

**Blockers: NONE.** No fact stated in the proposal contradicts the core claim. The risks are real but they are *risks*, not blockers: none of the proposal's own figures fail, and every risk raised has a plausible counter. Reviewers may mock the size, but reviewers mocked tablets too. The stylus may seem niche, but it differentiates in a commoditizing market. The device may not replace either a phone or a tablet, but it can be a *third* device for people who already own both.

**Risks:** demand is unproven; the category doesn't exist yet; the price premium may not hold. **Kill criteria:** if first-quarter sales fall below [the maker's stated threshold for a premium SKU], stop; if return rates exceed [the maker's average return rate for phones] due to size or stylus complaints, stop. Leading metric: week-one sell-through at the premium price. Review date: 90 days post-launch.

The commitment is capped: one device, one launch, one quarter to read the signal. The door is two-way: a failed large phone doesn't sink the existing line.

DECISION: GO
