First call: NO-GO. The proposal asks every party in the chain to move against its own interest simultaneously.

The steelman: a cheap disc with built-in expiration eliminates late fees and return trips, creating a new rental category with no shelf-space problem. That is genuinely clever.

But the attack that breaks it: **every participant is asked to take the worse deal.** Studios must support a new format that pays them less per view than sell-through VHS. Electronics makers must build special players that cost more and sell fewer units than standard players. Customers must buy a more expensive player, then pay again to re-watch a disc they physically own. The proposal needs all four parties to agree simultaneously, and the one party with leverage—studios—has no reason to prefer a few dollars per disc over their existing revenue model.

The better-way alternative is decisive: standard DVD rental through existing retail infrastructure reaches the same outcome (cheap home viewing) with zero new hardware, zero studio renegotiation, and zero customer behavior change. The retailer already has stores, inventory systems, and customers. The disc format that wins is the one that plays on cheap players everywhere.

Kill check: the core claim is that customers will pay more for players and accept re-paying for discs. The proposal's own risks confirm this is unproven. The commitment—new format, special players, studio deals—is large and hard to reverse. This is a three-yes risk: it concerns the core claim, the proposal's own facts support the failure, and the commitment is too large to stop once started.

No cap, pilot, or kill criterion is stated. Even with one—a single-store trial—the structural problem remains: nobody in the chain wins except possibly the retailer, and the retailer wins only if everyone else cooperates.

DECISION: NO-GO
