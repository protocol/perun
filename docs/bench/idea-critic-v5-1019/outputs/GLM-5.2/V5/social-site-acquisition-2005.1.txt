First call: GO. Two years of rapid growth and a large young audience is a rare, strategic asset for a media/advertising company; the risk profile (high price, fickle tastes, unproven monetization) is exactly what due diligence and deal structure address, not reasons to pass on a category-defining property.

Steelman: A fast-growing social network with a large, young, hard-to-reach-elsewhere audience is worth a premium because such audiences are scarce, and combining them with an existing advertising/media platform creates revenue optionality that neither asset has alone.

Attack — the sharpest objection: The core claim is that rapid audience growth can be turned into advertising revenue. That is unproven. The price (580 million) is high for a two-year-old site. Tastes change; competitors lurk. If monetization fails, the company has paid a premium for an asset whose audience may migrate before revenue catches up.

Better way: Do nothing — keep the 580 million, avoid the risk of fickle social tastes and unproven monetization. But the company forgoes a scarce young audience and a strategic position in a fast-growing category. Doing nothing is reversible; the acquisition opportunity may not be.

Evidence check (internal consistency of the stated facts): The proposal states rapid audience growth and a large young audience as facts. It states that turning growth into ad revenue is unproven as a risk. No internal contradiction. The price is high but the proposal does not claim the price is low; it claims the audience is worth combining with the company's media business. That claim survives.

Kill criteria the proposal needs before GO becomes safe: (1) a capped, staged first step — e.g., a smaller initial payment with earn-outs tied to audience retention and ad-revenue milestones; (2) a kill metric — if audience retention falls below a threshold or ad revenue per user stays below a floor by a set date, the integration stops or unwinds; (3) a fallback — if monetization stalls, the audience asset still has strategic value, but the deal structure should limit downside.

The proposal has a sound strategic basis and the risks are real but manageable through structure. The absence of earn-outs, kill metrics, and a fallback is a gap to close, not a reason to reject.

DECISION: GO
