**First call: GO.** The company is already behind and its last design missed; the status quo is not safe. An all-new architecture with more cores at lower prices is exactly the asymmetric bet a smaller challenger must make — it cannot outspend the rival, so it must out-spec at a price point the rival is structurally reluctant to match.

**Steelman.** The rival's margin model defends premium pricing; a smaller firm can win desktop share by delivering more cores per dollar, forcing the rival into a margin-destroying price war it will hesitate to start.

**Options.** Do nothing: the firm stays behind, finances deteriorate anyway — not viable. Incremental refresh of the disappointing design: cheaper and faster to market, but repeats the credibility damage and concedes the performance narrative. All-new architecture: highest risk, highest reward, and the only path that changes the story.

**Pre-mortem.** The new architecture underperforms again, software doesn't scale across cores, and the rival cuts prices — the firm has spent its last chip and faces exit. That is a real failure mode, but it is the same risk as doing nothing, just faster.

**Blockers: NONE.** The proposal's own facts show a company with no room for a second miss, yet the alternative — continuing to lose — is itself fatal. The stated risks (prior miss, rival response, software adoption, financial fragility) are all genuine but none is a fact already in hand that makes the plan fail as proposed; they are conditions to manage.

**Risks:** Prior all-new design underperformed; rival may cut prices or ship faster chips; software may not use extra cores; finances cannot absorb another miss. **Kill criteria:** If pre-launch benchmarks do not show competitive single-thread performance and meaningful multi-thread scaling in top desktop applications, stop and revise — do not ship on brand promise alone. Leading metric to watch: benchmark parity at launch; review date: silicon back.

**DECISION: GO**
