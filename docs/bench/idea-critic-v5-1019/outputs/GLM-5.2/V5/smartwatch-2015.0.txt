First call: GO. The maker's phone is already on hundreds of millions of wrists; a companion device there is a category extension, not a speculation.

The strongest attack: one-day battery, phone-dependence, and a second screen no one asked for repeat exactly why earlier smartwatches sold poorly. That is a real risk — but the proposal's own facts also state those earlier watches sold *modestly*, not failed. The category had demand without a definitive product. A one-day battery mirrors the phone itself, which buyers charge nightly without complaint. Phone-dependence is a feature, not a bug: it ties the watch to an installed base the maker already owns, creating switching cost the standalone watch makers lack.

The cheaper alternative — do nothing — preserves margin but cedes the wrist to competitors who will eventually anchor their own phone ecosystems there. The cost of delay in 2015 is ceding the health-tracking and notification surface to Google, Samsung, or a startup that gets there first with a defensible product. Materials, price tiers, health tracking, and an app store are the right bets: they create aspiration, utility, and platform lock-in simultaneously.

Kill criteria: if first-year sell-through falls below roughly 10 million units, the category is not materializing at this price and the product should be treated as a hobby, not a pillar. Leading metric to watch: attach rate among existing phone owners in the first two quarters. Review at the 12-month mark.

The risks are real but they are the known risks of a new category, not evidence the plan fails as proposed. The maker has the installed base, the brand pull, and the supply-chain muscle to make a one-day, phone-dependent watch into a product people buy anyway — because the phone itself taught them that daily charging is an acceptable trade for a screen they keep looking at.

DECISION: GO
