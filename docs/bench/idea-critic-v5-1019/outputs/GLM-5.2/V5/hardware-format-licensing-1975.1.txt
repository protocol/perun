**First call: NO-GO.** The proposal bets on picture quality and early lead while ceding the two variables that decide a format war: installed base and content. A one-hour tape cannot record a full film or sports event — that is a product that fails its core use case on its own facts.

**Steelman.** The best version of this claim: superior picture quality plus a controlled partner set protects margins and avoids commoditization, and early lead locks in the standard before the rival scales.

**Attack.** The steelman survives only if quality and control outweigh reach and content. The proposal's own facts say they do not. The rival is licensing to many makers, which spreads players and lowers prices. A format with fewer manufacturers will have fewer pre-recorded titles. One-hour tapes will not record a full film or sports event. Each risk the proposal names is a concrete failure of its core claim, and the plan does not answer any of them.

**Blockers.** (1) The product's own spec — a one-hour tape — cannot serve the primary use case (a full film or sports event), a negative the proposal states as already true. (2) The rival's licensing model, on the proposal's own facts, will produce a larger installed base at lower prices, and the plan offers no counter to that reach advantage. (3) Fewer manufacturers means fewer pre-recorded titles, which removes the content pull that locks in a standard.

**Risks.** The rival's format may become the standard — this is not a risk to manage; on the stated facts it is the trajectory.

**Kill criteria.** If one-hour tapes cannot record a full film or sports event, the format is dead on arrival for the home market. If the rival reaches 3× the installed base within 18 months, the standard is lost. Either fires now.

The proposal keeps a format proprietary and relies on picture quality and early lead while its own stated risks show that model losing on reach, content and usable recording time.

DECISION: NO-GO
