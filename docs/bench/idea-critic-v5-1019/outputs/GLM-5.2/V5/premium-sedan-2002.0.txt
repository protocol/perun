First call: NO-GO. The proposal's own stated risks are the core claim: luxury buyers pay for the badge, and this brand has no luxury image. Putting a mass-market name on a premium-priced sedan does not remove that barrier; it relies on buyers ignoring the very thing the proposal admits they care about.

Blockers: the proposal states as already true that buyers pay for the premium badge and that this brand lacks luxury image. That is not a *may* — it is the proposal's own fact, and the plan does not answer it. The same buyers are asked to pay rival-level prices for a name they associate with volume, not prestige. A dedicated factory at low volume compounds the problem: high fixed cost, small unit base, no brand equity to charge against.

Risks (kill criteria): no badge equity → no price premium → factory underutilised. Kill metric: if pre-orders from premium-intent buyers do not hit a stated threshold by a fixed date, stop the factory commitment. Leading metric: deposit-to-conversion ratio in target markets; review within the first sales quarter.

Better way: do not abandon the ambition — stage it. Either co-develop with an acquired premium marque and badge it there, or run a limited-volume flagship under the mass-market name to build credibility before committing a dedicated plant. Either reaches the same upside at lower blast radius.

The steelman — that engineering substance (large engine, advanced suspension, top materials) can override badge loyalty — is the strongest version, and it fails because the proposal itself concedes the badge is what buyers pay for. No evidence is given that this brand's engineering reputation alone clears that bar.

Decision: no-go. Build badge equity first (acquire, partner, or run a limited flagship), then commit the factory.
