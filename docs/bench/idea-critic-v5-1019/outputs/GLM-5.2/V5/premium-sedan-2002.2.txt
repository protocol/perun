First call: NO-GO. The proposal's own risk list names the core claim — luxury buyers pay for the badge — and the plan answers none of it.

The steelman: the largest regional carmaker has scale, engineering depth, and cost advantages; a genuinely superior product at premium-rival pricing could break into the class.

The attack: premium positioning is a two-way door only if committed lightly; this plan commits a dedicated factory and a worldwide launch before any market signal. The proposal's own stated facts — buyers pay for the badge, the brand has no luxury image, luxury buyers may not accept a mass-market name — are not risks that *could* materialize; they are the *premise* of the category, already true on day one. A factory and a global launch do not answer a badge problem; they multiply its cost. The cheapest alternative — a sub-brand with a pilot market — reaches the same upside at a fraction of the committed capital.

**Blockers:** the proposal states as already true that buyers pay for the premium badge, the brand has no luxury image, and luxury buyers may not accept a mass-market name. The plan's answer — large engine, advanced suspension, top-quality materials — addresses the *product*, not the *claim it attacks*: that buyers in this class pay for the badge, which the proposal itself concedes. No sub-brand, no pilot market, no badge strategy is named. The dedicated factory and worldwide launch commit irreversible capital against an untested core claim.

**Risks:** low volume at a dedicated factory raises unit cost; worldwide launch maximizes exposure before learning; no kill criterion is named.

**Kill criteria:** pilot in one market under a sub-brand; stop if premium-rival-priced units fail to clear a stated volume threshold within 18 months; watch badge-recognition surveys and repeat-purchase rate; review at 12 months.

DECISION: NO-GO
