First call: GO. A premium SKU with a stylus, priced as flagship, costs the line a margin cannibalization risk against an unproven category upside.

Steelman: a large-screen stylus phone creates a new premium segment (note-taking, precision input) that the maker can own before competitors, at margin uplift over its existing line.

**Blockers:** NONE. No internal numbers contradict the claim. The risks are stated as *may*—they are not facts in hand.

**Risks (become kill criteria):**
- Reviewers call it too big → leading metric: pre-order conversion rate vs. existing flagship; review date: 30 days post-launch; kill threshold: <60% of flagship pre-orders → stop production run.
- Stylus demand unproven → accessory attach rate tracked weekly; if <15% by week 6, cut the stylus from the next iteration.
- Cannibalization of own phones → track net-new vs. substitution; if >40% of buyers are upgrading from the maker's own mid-tier instead of net-new, reprice.

**Pre-mortem:** The device fails because it is neither fish nor fowl—too big to pocket comfortably, too small to replace a tablet. Reviewers anchor on "awkward size" and the stylus reads as gimmick. The lesson: the category must be *named* (not "big phone" but "note phone") so reviewers evaluate against the new frame, not the old one.

**Better-way check:** Do-nothing loses a first-mover window on a category that, if it works, reshapes the premium tier. A cheaper alternative (mid-size screen, no stylus) reaches a smaller upside. The full proposal beats both.

**Strongest attack survived:** "The device is too small to replace a tablet and too large to replace a phone, so it serves no need." This fails because the proposal does not claim it *replaces* either—it claims a *between* category. The stylus is the mechanism that makes "between" coherent: precision input is the use case neither a phone nor a tablet serves well. The attack assumes the device must win an existing category; the proposal bets on creating one.

DECISION: GO
