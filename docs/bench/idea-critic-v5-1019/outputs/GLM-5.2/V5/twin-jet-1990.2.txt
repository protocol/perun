First call: NO-GO. One anchor customer and an unproven regulatory pathway do not justify a very large commitment at launch.

The strongest version of the claim: eight airlines co-designing from day one creates a product the market must buy, and the 34-unit order proves demand. Attack that. The 34 aircraft come from **one** airline; the other seven have committed nothing. Launching an all-new airframe on a single customer's word is a bet on demand that has not been demonstrated.

**Blockers:**
1. **Regulatory approval does not exist.** Twin-engine overwater operation (ETOPS) at the range this aircraft implies is not yet authorized. The product's entire value proposition—long overwater twin routes—depends on a rule change that has not happened and that the proposal does not address.
2. **Cost vs. confirmed revenue.** Development cost is described as very large; confirmed orders are 34 units from one buyer. The proposal does not show the break-even order count, the production cost per unit, or the launch-order revenue against spend. On the stated figures the economics are unproven.

**Risks (kill criteria if revised):**
- Co-design with eight airlines may produce conflicting requirements and delay entry into service. Kill criterion: entry-into-service date slips beyond a named quarter.
- A rival is also building new models; first-mover advantage may erode if launch slips. Kill criterion: rival reaches certification first.
- Single-customer dependency: if that airline cancels or defers, the program lacks a replacement order book. Kill criterion: no second airline commits by a named date.

**Better way:** secure provisional regulatory wording and at least one additional firm order before committing the full development budget. A capped feasibility and design phase with the eight airlines, gated on regulatory progress and a second order, reaches the same upside at fractionally reversible cost.

DECISION: NO-GO
