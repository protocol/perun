First call: NO-GO. Every risk raised is a core-claim risk, not an execution detail, and the company's refusal to share methods means none can be checked before committing 350 million dollars.

The strongest version of the proposal is that a retailer with thin margins and falling foot traffic could become the convenience layer for preventive care, and that exclusivity locks in a category-defining platform before competitors notice. That is attractive. But the stated facts fail it.

The technology has not been shown in published studies. That is not a risk to manage; it is the whole bet. A 350-million-dollar commitment to an unproven device, from a young company that will not share its methods, is a one-way door through ignorance: the chain has no medical expertise to evaluate what it cannot see, and no independent corroboration that the device works at all. The regulator concern is not a future annoyance — it is a direct threat to the revenue model, because clinics in hundreds of stores depend on tests regulators permit.

Do-nothing costs nothing and keeps the option open. A capped pilot — a handful of stores, low single-digit millions, contingent on published validation and method disclosure — reaches most of the learning at a fraction of the blast radius. The proposal as written commits the full sum before any of its own stated risks are resolved.

Blockers: the device's accuracy is unverified (stated), the methods are undisclosed and unverifiable (stated), and the chain lacks the expertise to assess what it cannot inspect (stated). Three core-claim risks, each confirmed by the proposal's own facts, each on an irreversible, too-large commitment. That fails the kill check.

The right move is not 350 million dollars now. It is a small, time-boxed pilot gated on published evidence and method transparency — or walking away.

DECISION: NO-GO
