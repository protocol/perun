First call: NO-GO. The proposal's own logic defeats it: it gates relief on proof of impact, yet simultaneously admits users cannot easily show they are affected. The condition and the barrier contradict each other directly — this is not a risk, it is a structural flaw in the plan as stated.

The steelman is that the error rate is genuinely negligible for almost all users, making universal replacement a disproportionate cost. That is reasonable. But the chosen mechanism — proof-gated replacement — fails on its own terms. Users who *are* affected cannot prove it; users who *are not* affected but are frightened cannot get reassurance. The proposal solves neither the technical problem nor the trust problem.

The pre-mortem is obvious and was in fact the historical outcome: the company states the flaw is not serious while simultaneously restricting access to a fix. Buyers conclude the company is hiding a defect or dodging accountability. The reputation risk the proposal names is not a possible side effect of this plan — it is the predictable *result* of this plan. The proposal does not answer this; it lists it and moves on.

A cheaper alternative exists that still caps cost: offer replacement on request to any user who asks, without requiring proof of impact. This removes the proof barrier entirely, costs far less than universal proactive replacement, and directly addresses the trust problem. The proposal's binary — prove impact or get nothing — is the worst framing: maximum friction, minimum reassurance.

The kill criterion writes itself: if public discussion intensifies and large customers publicly demand unrestricted replacement, the proof-gated policy has already failed and must be abandoned. Waiting for that signal costs more reputation than acting now.

DECISION: NO-GO
