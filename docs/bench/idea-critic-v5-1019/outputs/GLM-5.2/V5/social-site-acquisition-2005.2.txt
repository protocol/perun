First call: GO. A large, fast-growing young audience is exactly the asset a media company's advertising business can monetize, and such audiences rarely stay available at any price.

Steelman: a media company's core competence is selling advertising; buying the only social network that has reached scale with young users gives it that audience exclusively, at a price that is small relative to the media company's market value and to the strategic value of owning the category leader.

Cheaper alternative: a multi-year advertising partnership gives access to the audience without the 580 million dollar outlay or integration risk. But it leaves the asset available to competitors and forfeits ownership of the platform itself. Do nothing cedes the category entirely.

The risks raised are real but are all risks, not blockers. The site is young — but two years of rapid growth is evidence of product-market fit, not a warning against it. Tastes can change quickly — yet network effects create stickiness that resists taste shifts. Other sites compete — but this one is winning. Converting audience to ad revenue is unproven — yet the media company's entire competence is exactly that conversion, which is the strategic logic for the acquisition rather than a partnership.

No fact in the proposal contradicts the core claim. The price is high but not shown to exceed the strategic value. The audience is young but demonstrated. Monetization is unproven but falls in the acquirer's lane. The one-way-door concern (irreversible spend) is the only item that could justify delay, and it does not: the opportunity is time-bound, as a fast-growing site will not remain available.

Kill criteria: if audience growth stalls for two consecutive quarters post-acquisition, or if the advertising integration fails to show revenue lift within twelve months, revisit the integration strategy or divest.

DECISION: GO
