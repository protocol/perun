First call: GO. Retro styling has worked (Mini, Beetle), the platform is shared so cost is contained, and personalization options are high-margin upsells that differentiate from the platform-mate.

Steelman: a beloved 1957 design on a proven platform, sold at premium with personalization options, recreates the Mini/Beetle formula at lower R&D cost.

Alternatives: do nothing — the company keeps losing money and relevance. Cheaper base model — moves volume but at thinner margins on a small car. The proposal beats both if the styling and price premium hold.

Pre-mortem: it fails if buyers see "backward styling" as dated rather than charming, or if the premium over the platform-mate exceeds what retro nostalgia can justify. The company's recent poor sales suggest weak brand pull, which is exactly what retro styling trades on.

Blockers: NONE stated. The risks are real but unproven — they are the kill criteria, not blockers.

Risks (kill criteria): thin small-car margins plus a price premium is sound only if personalization uptake is high; the company's weak finances and recent poor sales mean limited runway if launch demand disappoints; backward styling dating quickly is a taste risk that focus groups could de-risk pre-launch.

Kill criteria: pre-orders or dealer interest below a named threshold within 90 days of launch announcement triggers a price cut or trim reduction. Leading metric: configurator completion rate and personalization attach rate at pilot dealerships, reviewed at 60 days. Cap the initial production run to limit exposure given weak finances.

The strongest attack: this company's recent models sold poorly, so its brand may lack the nostalgic equity that made Mini and Beetle work — meaning buyers won't pay the premium. That is a real risk, but it is unproven on the stated facts and the capped, measured first step contains the downside. The proposal is a reasonable bet at lower cost than a clean-sheet car.

DECISION: GO
