First call: NO-GO. The economics are fatal on the proposal's own numbers.

The claim: cut the price to ~$10/month, pay theaters full ticket price (~$8-15/film), grow membership rapidly, then earn from data and studio/theater deals.

Steelman: a subsidized land grab can work if the subsidy is capped, the data revenue materializes, and the network effects lock in theaters before the cash runs out.

**Blockers:**

1. **The fee cannot cover the cost, by design.** A member seeing one film a day costs the company ~$240-450/month in theater payments against $10 in revenue. The proposal states this gap exists and does not close it. The new revenue streams (data, deals) are unproven — the proposal itself flags them as risks, not facts. There is no number on what data or deals would yield, so the plan's own core math is unsupported.

2. **Heavy users are the expected user, not an edge case.** "One film a day" at $10 selects precisely for the people who will cost the most. The pricing mechanism attracts the worst-cost customers. This is not a risk that might happen; it is the proposal's own design.

3. **Theater cooperation is assumed, not secured.** The model requires theaters to accept a third party paying full price while the subscription company runs a loss. Theaters refusing to cooperate is listed as a risk, but the entire revenue model depends on deals that do not yet exist.

**Risks (kill criteria if revised):** no cap on the subsidy; no pilot with a measured break-even point; no signed theater or studio partnerships; no evidence that data revenue can close a $200+/member/month gap.

The new owner has cash, but cash without a path to unit economics is a burn rate, not a business. The proposal asks the team to approve a model where the best-case customer is the most expensive one, the revenue side is unproven, and a key dependency (theater cooperation) is unresolved.

DECISION: NO-GO
