First call: GO. A sports-car maker with a loyal enthusiast following and a small range is exactly the profile that can stretch its brand into a high-margin segment — the enthusiast base provides the credibility, and the shared platform with a larger group de-risks the heavy engineering.

Steelman: the brand's racing heritage and engineering reputation can command a premium price in a luxury SUV segment that is growing and where buyers pay for distinction. Co-development spreads cost; the sports-car badge differentiates against generic luxury SUVs.

The strongest attack: loyal customers see an SUV as betrayal and the brand is permanently diluted. This fails because brand dilution is reversible — if the market rejects it, the model can be killed. The bet is asymmetric: downside is one bad model line and some near-term criticism; upside is access to a high-margin, growing segment that funds the sports cars the enthusiasts actually want. Porsche's own racing history included vehicles far removed from pure sports cars, so the heritage argument cuts both ways.

The cost risk is real but manageable through the shared-platform arrangement. The competitive risk — strong incumbents — is a reason to differentiate hard, not a reason to stay out. Every market worth entering has incumbents.

Blockers: NONE. The proposal's own facts do not show the plan fails. The risks named are execution risks, not contradictions in the core claim.

Risks: brand dilution (reversible), high first-year cost (shared platform mitigates), heavy vehicle image (addressable through positioning), strong incumbents (addressable through differentiation).

Kill criteria: if first-year sales fall below a stated threshold, or if brand-equity measures among core enthusiasts drop beyond a stated margin, stop and reassess. Leading metric to watch: pre-orders and deposit rates from existing customers. Review date: end of first full sales year.

DECISION: GO
