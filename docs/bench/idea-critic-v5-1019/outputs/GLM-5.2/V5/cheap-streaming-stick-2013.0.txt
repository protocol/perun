**First call: GO.** The proposal's own logic survives the strongest attack.

**Steelman:** A $35 dongle that uses the phone as the controller removes the two things that killed earlier attempts — high price and hardware complexity — while the open kit converts the app-gap from a permanent disadvantage into a launch-day risk.

**Strongest attack survived:** "Few apps at launch and incumbents already have large catalogs, so buyers have no reason to choose this." This fails because the proposal shifts the value proposition: it is not a box competing on catalog depth, it is a cheap bridge that puts content already on the buyer's phone onto the TV. At $35, a buyer does not need to commit to an ecosystem. The open kit means the catalog gap is a question of developer adoption over months, not a fixed deficit at launch.

**Blockers:** NONE. The proposal's own facts support the path: the dongle is cheap enough that app scarcity at launch is survivable, and the open kit is the mechanism that closes the gap over time. No internal contradiction in the stated numbers or logic.

**Risks (kill criteria):**
- Developer adoption: if fewer than N third-party apps ship within the first two quarters, the open-kit bet has failed.
- Buyer confusion from no remote: if return rates exceed a threshold in the first quarter, add a minimal remote or on-screen guide.
- Margin pressure: near-zero hardware margin is acceptable only if the software ecosystem generates revenue; set a date by which ecosystem revenue must materialize.

The no-remote design is the sharpest risk — it could confuse buyers — but it is also what enables the low price and simplicity. The proposal's own logic holds: cheap, open, phone-controlled is a coherent bet that does not require defeating incumbents on their terms.

DECISION: GO
