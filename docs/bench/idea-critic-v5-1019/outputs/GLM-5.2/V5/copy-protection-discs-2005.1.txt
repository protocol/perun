**First call: NO-GO.** Installing hidden software on customers' computers to restrict their use of a product they bought is a self-inflicted trust catastrophe.

**Blockers:**
1. The proposal's own stated risks — "installs without clear consent," "hard to remove," "customers may object to being treated as thieves" — are not risks but the plan's core mechanism. The plan achieves its goal by doing the things that destroy trust. No kill criterion answers this.
2. The stated facts contradict the core claim. The claim is "limit copying and ripping" to recover sales. But a determined copier defeats any software-only protection (hold the shift key, use a different OS, use a marker pen on the disc edge). The protection inconveniences the honest buyer while failing to stop the copying it targets. The plan's own mechanism removes its route to the upside.

**Risks (kill criteria):**
- Security researchers find a rootkit-style vulnerability → the company is liable for exposing millions of customers to attack.
- A class-action lawsuit or regulatory action follows from undisclosed installation.
- Customer churn exceeds any recovered sales.

**Better ways considered:**
- *Do nothing:* copying exists but customers trust the brand; discs play everywhere.
- *Visible, optional DRM with clear disclosure:* less hostile, still defeatable.
- *Compete on value:* lower prices, bundled digital downloads, loyalty perks.

Do nothing beats this proposal: the plan's upside (recovered sales) requires the protection to work, and the stated facts show software-only protection does not stop determined copying. The plan ships hostility, gets no upside, and invites liability — a one-way door with no recovery path.

DECISION: NO-GO
