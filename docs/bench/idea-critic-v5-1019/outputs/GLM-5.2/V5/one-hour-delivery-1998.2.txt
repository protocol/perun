**First call: NO-GO.** The unit economics are broken on the proposal's own facts: free delivery of small orders costs more than the margin on them, and the company must employ many delivery staff directly. That is a structural loss, not a risk.

**Steelman:** Dense urban demand plus direct control of the delivery fleet could build a defensible logistics brand before competitors scale.

**Attack:** The proposal's own stated risk — that free delivery of small orders costs more than the margin — is not a risk but a mathematical certainty under the stated model. A $3 snack delivered free by a salaried employee loses money on every transaction. Volume amplifies the loss. The proposal names this problem and offers no mechanism to solve it.

**Alternatives:** Do nothing — avoids burning capital on a structurally unprofitable model. Charge for delivery — breaks the "free delivery" value proposition. Require minimum order — breaks "no minimum order." Every alternative that fixes the economics breaks the core pitch.

**Pre-mortem:** The company fails because customer acquisition cost plus per-order delivery cost exceeds lifetime value, and rapid multi-city expansion accelerates the bleed. Direct employment of delivery staff creates fixed overhead that cannot flex with demand.

**Blockers:** The proposal states as already true that free delivery of small orders costs more than the margin on them. That removes the route to profitability under the proposed model, and the proposal does not answer it.

**Risks:** Customers may use the service only when subsidized — this concerns the core claim (that buyers will pay sustainably), the proposal's own facts support that it will happen, and rapid expansion is hard to reverse. Three yeses: this risk is a blocker.

The idea may work with different economics, but as proposed — free delivery, no minimum, direct employment, rapid expansion — it ships a structural loss and expands it across cities.

DECISION: NO-GO
