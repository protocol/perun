**First call: GO.** A maker with a leading wide-body, designing the right-sized twin with eight airlines in the room, has a shot at redefining the mid-size long-range market.

The steelman: a twin sized between two existing wide-bodies, designed with eight airlines from the start, captures the fuel-efficiency and range economics that twins deliver, at a capacity the market gap demands. One airline ordering 34 aircraft is real money — a launch customer committing to a meaningful fleet.

The strongest attack I can find: launching on one airline's 34-aircraft order, at very large cost, where the core regulatory approval (twin overwater operations) has not been granted, is a bet on an unproven regulatory path with insufficient demand validation. If the authorities do not grant extended-range twin approval, the aircraft's entire value proposition collapses and the program is stranded.

Why that attack fails to block: the proposal's own facts show the regulatory risk is about *approval not yet granted*, not *approval denied*. Twin-engine ETOPS certification was already an active regulatory conversation in 1990; this is a timing risk, not a feasibility wall. The rival building new models confirms the market exists and creates urgency — waiting cedes the segment. Designing with airlines risks changes, but eight airlines in the room from the start is the strongest possible demand signal and de-risks the spec. The 34-aircraft order is a launch anchor, not the total addressable market.

**Blockers:** NONE. No fact stated shows the plan fails as proposed. The regulatory approval is ungranted, not refused; the cost is large but the commitment is the company's to make; the rival's moves argue for speed, not delay.

**Risks (kill criteria):** ETOPS approval not secured by a named date → stop or pivot. Orders below a threshold by program midpoint → reassess. Development cost exceeding a cap by a set date → review scope.
