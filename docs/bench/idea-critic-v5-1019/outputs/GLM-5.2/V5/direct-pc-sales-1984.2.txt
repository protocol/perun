The core claim — that a direct-only, no-reseller model can undercut established brands on price while growing from revenue — survives the sharpest attack available on these facts.

The strongest objection: buyers need to see and try a computer before buying, so a no-store model loses the sale at the moment of decision. This fails to break the claim because the proposal's own evidence — the student is *already* selling directly to customers — shows the channel works today, not hypothetically. The risk is real but it is a scaling risk, not a falsification of the model.

The other raised risks (incumbent brands and channels, no service network, parts on credit) are execution risks that become kill criteria, not blockers. Each has a cheaper mitigation than building stores: third-party service contracts, supplier relationships built from proven order volume, and word-of-mouth marketing to the direct-buyer segment.

The alternative of opening retail locations would add fixed costs (rent, staff, inventory display) that directly undermine the price-undercutting advantage that is the entire basis of the proposal. "Do nothing" leaves a working business unincorporated, with no liability protection and no path to scale beyond personal capacity.

The proposal states its facts, its model is already proven in practice by the student's existing sales, and the raised risks are addressable without abandoning the direct-only structure. No blocker in hand: the numbers (lower price from no retail overhead, revenue-funded growth) are internally consistent, and the target buyers — price-sensitive direct purchasers — are already buying.

Kill criteria: if direct order volume cannot sustain parts procurement within the first two quarters, pivot to a hybrid model with limited showroom presence. Leading metric: repeat direct orders per quarter; review at month six.
