The strongest attack: this chain is tiny, its food is slower and needs more skilled workers, and its price is above the core menu — so it may never scale, and the giant is paying for something outside its competence. That attack fails on the proposal's own facts. The stake is *minority*, so capital at risk is bounded. The chain runs *independently*, so the giant's operational machine is not disrupted and management attention is a monitor, not a diversion. The *right to invest more later* means the giant learns before committing — the optionality is the point, not the initial size. The price and skill concerns describe the category, not a flaw in the bet; if the format works, those are solvable, and if it does not, the minority position limits the loss.

The real question is whether made-to-order, premium-priced food is a durable category or a fad. The proposal cannot answer that — but it does not need to. The structure lets the giant find out cheaply, with a path to scale the winner. Waiting costs the option entirely if a competitor moves first.
