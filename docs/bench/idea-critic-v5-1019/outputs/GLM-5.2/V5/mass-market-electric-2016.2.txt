First call: NO-GO. The company's own facts show it cannot execute the plan as proposed: no mass-production experience, unproven battery scaling, refundable deposits, and thin margins on a cheaper car.

The strongest version of the claim: a cheaper sedan at scale captures a far larger market than two expensive low-volume vehicles, and refundable deposits both fund the build and prove demand.

The sharpest attack I can find: every risk the proposal names is a core-claim risk, not a peripheral one. "Never produced at that volume" means the plan's central premise — build far higher volumes next year — rests on a capability the company has never demonstrated. The timeline (unveil, take deposits, begin production the next year) gives no room to learn mass production before committing. Battery scaling is not a procurement detail; it is the binding constraint on volume. Refundable deposits are not sales; if production slips or quality fails, the deposits leave. And lower margins mean the plan must hit volume to survive, yet volume is the unproven part.

Blockers: the proposal states as fact that the company has little mass-production experience and has never built at the proposed volume, yet its timeline assumes production begins next year. That gap between stated capability and committed schedule is a blocker the plan does not answer.

Risks: demand is unproven at the cheaper price point; battery supply may not scale; deposits may not convert; margins are thin. Each becomes a kill criterion: a capped first production run with a quality threshold, a battery-supply commitment date, a deposit-to-order conversion rate, and a unit-cost ceiling.

DECISION: NO-GO
