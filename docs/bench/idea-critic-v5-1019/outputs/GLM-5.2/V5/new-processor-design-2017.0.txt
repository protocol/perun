**First call: GO.** A smaller player losing the performance war has one path: a differentiated bet that changes the basis of competition. Standing still guarantees continued decline.

**Steelman:** The company attacks on two axes simultaneously — more cores at lower prices — using a clean-slate architecture to leapfrog rather than incrementally catch up. The rival's margin structure and product roadmap constrain its ability to respond instantly.

**The sharpest attack actually tried:** The company's finances "leave no room for a second miss," and its last all-new architecture underdelivered. So the proposal asks a firm with one failed architecture and no financial cushion to stake everything on another all-new architecture. If the architecture disappoints again, there is no recovery — the company is betting the firm on a variable it has already failed to control.

**Why it still survives:** The facts name the risk but not a *blocker*. The prior failure is a reason to validate harder, not to decline. Declining means accepting permanent second place with deteriorating finances — itself a slow death. The rival's response lag, the price undercut, and the core-count value proposition give the plan a plausible path to upside that the stated risks make harder but do not remove. Software adoption is a real risk but not a core-claim blocker: early adopters and competitive segments value cores even before broad optimization.

**Kill criteria:** Pre-launch performance parity with the rival's current line (not next-gen, which the rival controls); attach rate below a named threshold within one product cycle; the rival's price response erodes the margin advantage below a floor the company sets now.

**One concern the team must resolve:** Is the architecture genuinely new, or is it the same team's second attempt with a fresh label? The answer determines whether this is a calculated bet or a repeated mistake.

DECISION: GO
