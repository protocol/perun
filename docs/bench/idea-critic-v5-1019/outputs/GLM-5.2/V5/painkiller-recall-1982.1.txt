First call: GO. The maker's own plants are clean, so the threat is external and uncontrolled — every bottle on every shelf is a potential murder weapon and the company cannot know the limit of the contamination.

Blockers: NONE. The risks raised are real but none shows the plan fails as proposed. "May be local" is a hope, not a fact; the stated fact is that the source is unknown and post-factory, so nationwide withdrawal is the response that matches the evidence, not an overreaction to it.

Risks: the recall cost is very large but finite and survivable; the cost of a second wave of deaths — after the company knew and did not act — is unbounded and carries criminal exposure. Brand recovery and competitor encroachment are commercial concerns that rank below the duty to prevent known lethal harm. Signaling danger is unavoidable either way; a company that withdraws voluntarily signals responsibility, while one that waits for more deaths signals indifference.

Kill criteria: relaunch in tamper-resistant packaging only after the contamination vector is understood or closed; if a second poisoning occurs after withdrawal, the relaunch is deferred indefinitely. Leading metric: zero new poisonings traced to the product during the withdrawal window; review date: 30 days.

The strongest alternative — regional recall only — reaches less than the same upside: it leaves every bottle outside the known area as an untested risk and bets a stranger's life on "probably local." Do-nothing is a one-way door to catastrophe. The plan as proposed — withdraw, halt, relaunch safer — is the correct call and should be made now, before the next bottle is opened.
