First call: NO-GO. Entering a collapsed market with an unproven foreign brand, restrictive publishing terms, and unlimited financial liability via stock buybacks is financially suicidal.

**Steelman:** Repositioning the console as a "toy" with strict quality control and retailer guarantees can bypass the "video game" stigma and restore channel confidence.

**Attack:**
1.  **Financial Blocker:** The promise to take back *all* unsold stock creates uncapped downside. If the "toy" positioning fails to convince retailers or consumers, the company absorbs 100% of manufacturing and shipping costs for returned inventory, potentially bankrupting the venture before it scales.
2.  **Strategic Contradiction:** Limiting third-party publishers via a lockout chip restricts software library growth exactly when a massive catalog is needed to prove the system is not a "passed fad." This alienates the very developers required to make the hardware desirable.
3.  **Market Fit:** Retailers are actively clearing stock and refusing new categories. Without a proven hit title or a capped liability agreement, no rational buyer will allocate scarce shelf space to a toy company's expensive hardware.
4.  **Alternatives:** A "do nothing" approach avoids immediate loss. A better alternative would be a limited regional test with *no* buyback guarantee, relying on sell-through data to prove demand before national rollout.

**Pre-mortem:** The system launches, retailers order cautiously, but sales lag due to limited software. Retailers exercise the buyback clause en masse. The company is flooded with returned units and cash flow evaporates. The "toy" branding confuses core gamers without attracting enough non-gamers to compensate.

**Verdict:** The unlimited buyback clause is a fatal financial flaw that no amount of "entertainment system" branding can offset. The restriction on developers further undermines the product's value proposition. The plan commits to full-scale national exposure with irreversible financial liability before proving demand.

Blockers: Uncapped financial liability from the stock buyback promise; restricted software library contradicts the need for mass appeal.
Risks: Brand unfamiliarity, retailer resistance, home computer competition.

DECISION: NO-GO
