First call: GO. The request comes from the largest customer with a clear economic driver (cost per seat) in a growing market, despite massive capital requirements.

Steelman: Building a scalable, convertible wide-body jet secures market dominance against supersonic threats by maximizing subsonic efficiency and freighter longevity.

Alternatives:
1. Do nothing: Cede the high-capacity segment to competitors or lose the key customer.
2. Supersonic focus: Ignores current economic reality and unproven viability for mass transport.
3. Stretch existing jet: Insufficient to meet the "2.5x size" requirement or cost targets.

Fit: Aligns with growth and cost-reduction north stars. The freighter conversion feature hedges against the supersonic risk.

Pre-mortem: Failure occurs if the unproven engine delays certification or if supersonic travel renders subsonic jets obsolete prematurely. However, the raised cockpit design specifically mitigates the obsolescence risk by enabling a freighter pivot. The "one airline" risk is mitigated by that airline being the largest customer; their demand signals broader market needs.

Evidence: The proposal cites specific design choices (raised cockpit, four engines) addressing known risks (freighter conversion, thrust). The financial risk is inherent to the opportunity size; no alternative achieves the upside cheaper.

Blockers: NONE. The risks cited (cost, engine, supersonic competition, single customer) are execution challenges or market uncertainties, not factual contradictions. The plan addresses the supersonic threat via convertibility. The engine risk is managed by partnering with a supplier rather than internal development. The financial scale matches the strategic prize.

Risks: Engine development delays; slower-than-expected supersonic adoption; failure to sell beyond the launch customer.

Kill Criterion: If the engine supplier fails to deliver a certified prototype within 24 months, halt airframe finalization and pivot to a smaller derivative or freighter-only program. Leading metric: Engine bench-test success rate by Q4 1968.

The proposal survives the attack. The risks are real but managed through design flexibility and supplier partnership. Refusing based on cost alone ignores the existential threat of losing the largest customer and the growth market.

DECISION: GO
