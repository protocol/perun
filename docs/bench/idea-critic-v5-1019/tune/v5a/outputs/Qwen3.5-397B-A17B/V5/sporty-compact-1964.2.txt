First call: GO. The demographic shift is a verified fact; the product leverages existing assets to capture it cheaply.

Steelman: Reuse proven mechanicals to minimize risk while styling a distinct "sporty" image for a specific, growing demographic, using options to maximize margin.

Alternatives:
1. Do nothing: Cede the emerging youth market to competitors; rely on aging economy car sales.
2. All-new platform: Prohibitive cost and time; misses the immediate demographic window.
3. Upgrade current compact: Fails to shed the "dull/economy" perception required to attract buyers.

Fit: Aligns with capturing market share via segmentation without heavy R&D spend.

Pre-mortem: Failure occurs if the "cheap parts" perception overrides the styling, making it a "poor man's sports car." However, the long hood/short deck design specifically counters this visual cue. The risk of unproven demand is mitigated by the low base price (low barrier to entry) and the high reversibility of production (shared line).

Evidence: The proposal relies on the stated fact of the "postwar baby boom" entering the market. No external data contradicts this demographic wave. The mechanical sharing is a feature, not a bug, enabling the "low base price" critical for young buyers.

Blockers: NONE. The risks cited (unproven demand, cheap perception, ad spend) are execution risks, not factual blockers. The "cheap perception" is addressed by the specific styling cues. The "ad spend" is a calculated bet on a verified demographic shift, not a blind gamble.

Risks: Demand volume uncertainty; brand dilution if styling fails.
Kill Criterion: If first-year sales do not exceed 200,000 units (covering ad spend and tooling), halt convertible production and reduce ad buy by 50% for year two. Leading metric: Orders per dealer in first 30 days.

The plan beats "do nothing" by addressing a verified demographic shift with a low-cost, high-reversibility product. The risks are manageable via the proposed pricing and styling strategy.

DECISION: GO
