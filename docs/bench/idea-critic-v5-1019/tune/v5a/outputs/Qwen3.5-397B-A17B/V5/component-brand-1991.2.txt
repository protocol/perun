First call: GO. The proposal attacks a specific, solvable leak in the value chain: commoditization via buyer ignorance. If buyers cannot distinguish the superior component, they will not pay for it, and computer makers will swap to cheaper clones. Making the component a consumer demand driver forces computer makers to include it to sell their own units.

Steelman: By shifting pull-demand to the end-user, the chip maker transforms its product from a negotiable line-item into a mandatory sales feature for computer manufacturers, justifying the ad subsidy as a channel-control cost rather than pure marketing spend.

Options:
1. **Do nothing:** Continue competing on price and specs with computer makers; margin erosion and clone displacement are inevitable.
2. **Trade incentives only:** Pay computer makers directly. Risk: funds absorbed as margin; no consumer pull created.
3. **Consumer brand campaign (Proposed):** Highest upfront cost and channel friction, but the only path to owning the customer relationship and insulating margins.

Fit: Assumes the chip has a demonstrable performance lead worth branding. If the product is equal to clones, branding accelerates failure by highlighting interchangeability. Assuming performance superiority, this aligns with capturing value, not just volume.

Pre-mortem: Failure occurs if computer makers form a coalition to reject the logo, viewing it as an infringement on their brand sovereignty. However, the first mover among makers who accepts the subsidy gains a competitive advertising advantage, breaking the coalition. The cost of delay is the continued erosion of pricing power as clones gain perceived parity.

Evidence: The proposal relies on the assumption that end-users care about internal components. This is unproven but testable; the alternative (status quo) guarantees commoditization.

Blockers: NONE. The risks cited (unprecedented nature, resentment, budget share) are execution hurdles, not logical flaws. Unprecedented moves often yield monopoly rents; resentment is managed by the financial incentive of the subsidy; budget allocation is a choice between margin defense and volume.

Risks: Channel rebellion, campaign irrelevance. These are mitigated by the "go-if" nature of the pilot: if makers refuse the subsidy, the strategy fails cheaply.

Kill criterion: If, after 12 months, branded computer models do not command a price premium or grow share faster than non-branded equivalents despite the ad spend, terminate the program.

DECISION: GO
