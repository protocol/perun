First call: NO-GO. The proposal asks to spend heavily on marketing a material that is more expensive and potentially inferior (less comfortable) than the incumbent, while facing high switching costs for manufacturers.

Steelman: The synthetic offers unique durability or aesthetic consistency that justifies a premium price, creating a new "luxury durability" segment.

However, the attack reveals fatal flaws in the current plan:
1.  **Economic Disincentive:** Shoe manufacturers operate on thin margins. A material costing *more* than leather requires a proven consumer willingness to pay a significant premium. The proposal admits wearers may find it *less* comfortable. Marketing cannot easily overcome a functional deficit (comfort) combined with a price increase.
2.  **Switching Friction:** Retooling factories is a massive capital expenditure. Manufacturers will not incur this cost for a material that is both pricier and potentially unpopular due to comfort issues. The risk of fashion moving away is secondary to the immediate lack of value proposition.
3.  **Missing Validation:** The proposal assumes "premium" status can be manufactured via marketing spend alone. Without evidence of consumer demand for *this specific* trade-off (durability over comfort/cost), the spend is speculative gambling.

Alternatives:
*   **Do nothing:** Preserve capital; avoid launching a product with negative unit economics relative to leather.
*   **Niche Pilot:** Target a specific use-case where comfort is secondary and durability is paramount (e.g., industrial boots or specific fashion statements) before retooling mass-market lines. This reduces factory switching risk.
*   **Price Parity Strategy:** Do not launch until production costs drop below leather, removing the primary barrier to adoption.

Pre-mortem: The company spends millions on marketing. Manufacturers refuse to retool because the ROI isn't there. Early adopters return shoes due to discomfort. The brand is stuck with unsold inventory and a reputation for uncomfortable, overpriced shoes.

Blocker: The core value proposition is negative. The material costs more and performs worse (comfort) than the alternative it seeks to replace. No amount of marketing solves a fundamental product-market fit failure where the "upgrade" is objectively a downgrade in key consumer metrics.

Risks: Fashion trends shifting; manufacturer inertia.

Kill Criterion: If a blind wear-test shows <20% preference over leather at a 10% price premium, the project must stop.

DECISION: NO-GO
