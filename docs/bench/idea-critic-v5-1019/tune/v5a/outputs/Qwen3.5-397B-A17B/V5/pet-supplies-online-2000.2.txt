First call: NO-GO. The plan burns cash on every unit sold while assuming infinite capital availability.

Steelman: Aggressively consolidate the market via IPO proceeds to achieve monopoly scale before competitors can react, accepting deep short-term losses.

**Blockers:**
1.  **Negative Unit Economics:** Selling below cost on heavy, low-margin goods means every additional customer increases total losses. Scaling this model accelerates insolvency unless the lifetime value (LTV) drastically exceeds acquisition cost, which is unproven here.
2.  **Capital Market Dependency:** The strategy requires continuous access to public capital to fund operating losses. If markets close (a stated risk), the company cannot meet payroll or shipping costs immediately.
3.  **Acquisition Integration:** Buying a competitor while simultaneously building warehouses and launching a national TV campaign creates unmanageable operational complexity and cash drain.

**Risks:**
*   Competitors matching prices leads to a race to the bottom with no winner.
*   TV advertising has high fixed costs and delayed, unguaranteed ROI.
*   Shipping costs for heavy items erode any potential future margin.

**Pre-mortem:**
Six months post-IPO, the stock price collapses as quarterly losses widen due to the "buy below cost" strategy. Investors flee, credit lines freeze, and the company cannot fund the next inventory shipment or warehouse lease, forcing bankruptcy before scale benefits are realized.

**Analysis:**
The proposal treats venture funding logic (grow at all costs) as applicable to public markets without a path to profitability. Public investors tolerate losses for growth only when unit economics are positive or clearly trending toward profitability. Here, the core tactic (selling below cost) guarantees negative gross margins. The plan lacks a kill criterion or a cap on losses; it commits to full-scale expansion immediately. There is no "go-if" condition to validate that customer retention offsets the per-unit loss. The combination of negative margins, high fixed costs (TV, warehouses), and reliance on volatile public capital makes failure the baseline expectation.

This is not a calculated bet; it is a liquidity trap. The company would be selling $10 bills for $5, hoping to make up the difference in volume, while betting the entire firm on the stock market remaining open indefinitely.

DECISION: NO-GO
