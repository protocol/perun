First call: GO. The proposal attacks a critical vulnerability: commoditization. If buyers cannot distinguish the product, price becomes the only differentiator, destroying margins.

**Steelman:** Transform an invisible component into a primary purchase driver by subsidizing partners to display a quality seal, forcing clone makers into a discount position they cannot sustain.

**Options:**
1.  **Do nothing:** Continue B2B sales; risk margin collapse as clones proliferate.
2.  **Price war:** Cut costs to match clones; destroys profitability and R&D capacity.
3.  **Proposal:** Shift budget to pull-demand. High cost, but creates a moat clones cannot copy without admitting inferiority.

**Fit:** Assumes the end-user cares about performance/reliability enough to influence the buyer (computer maker). In 1991, PC marketing was shifting from specs to lifestyle; this aligns with that trajectory.

**Pre-mortem:** Failure occurs if computer makers refuse the co-op terms or if consumers ignore the logo. However, the financial incentive (subsidized ads) aligns maker interests with the campaign. Resentment is likely but manageable against the benefit of reduced ad spend.

**Evidence & Math:** The proposal admits "unprecedented" status and "large share" of budget. These are risks, not blockers. The math works only if the lift in volume/price premium exceeds the subsidy cost. Without current sales data, we assume the threat of cloning is existential, justifying the gamble.

**Blocker Test:**
*   *Blockers:* NONE. No fact presented proves the strategy cannot work. The risks (resentment, cost) are inherent to the strategy's mechanism, not contradictions of it.
*   *Risks:* Channel conflict, budget drain, consumer apathy.

**Kill Criteria:**
If, after two quarters, branded ad spend does not correlate with a ≥5% price premium or volume lift over non-branded equivalents, terminate the subsidy. The leading metric is "consumer mention rate" in retail interactions.

This is a classic "pull" strategy to bypass a congested channel. The lack of precedent is the opportunity, not the flaw. The cost is high, but the cost of inaction (commoditization) is fatal.

DECISION: GO
