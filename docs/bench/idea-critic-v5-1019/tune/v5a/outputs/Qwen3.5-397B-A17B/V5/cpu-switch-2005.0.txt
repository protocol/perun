First call: GO. The current roadmap fails the core requirement for thin, efficient laptops, and the dominant architecture offers a verified path to close that gap.

Steelman: Migrating to the dominant processor architecture within two years, bridged by a translation layer, is the only viable strategy to deliver competitive thin-and-light hardware while preserving the existing software ecosystem during the transition.

Alternatives considered:
1.  **Do nothing:** Guarantees product irrelevance as thermal and efficiency gaps widen; unacceptable risk.
2.  **Wait for current supplier:** Roadmap history shows consistent failure to meet thin-laptop requirements; betting on a turnaround is speculative.
3.  **Dual architecture:** Increases engineering complexity and cost without solving the immediate thermal deficit in the primary line.

Fit: Directly addresses the north star of delivering superior personal computing hardware, which is currently blocked by silicon limitations.

Pre-mortem & Door: If this fails, it will be because the translation layer performance is too poor for professional workflows, or the brand loses its "distinct" identity by becoming "just another PC." This is a one-way door; once the ecosystem shifts, returning is impossible. The cost of delay is continued market share erosion to competitors with superior form factors.

Evidence: The proposal states the current supplier has failed for a decade to deliver cool/efficient chips. The dominant maker's architecture is the market standard, implying better tooling and developer familiarity.

Blockers: NONE. The risks cited (sales freeze, rebuild effort, speed penalty, brand dilution) are execution challenges, not factual contradictions. They are mitigated by the two-year timeline and the translation layer. No evidence suggests the translation cannot meet minimum viability thresholds.

Risks: Developer friction and temporary performance degradation. These become kill criteria: if translated app performance drops below 70% of native speed by the midpoint review, the strategy must pivot.

Strongest attack survived: The claim that "brand distinctiveness will weaken." Attack: If the hardware advantage (thin/cool) is significant enough, users will accept standard internals. The brand becomes defined by the *experience* enabled by the new chip, not the chip itself. The attack fails because product superiority overrides architectural purism in the consumer mind.

DECISION: GO
