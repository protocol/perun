First call: NO-GO. The plan trades a scarce strategic asset (brand equity) for negligible financial return while ceding control of the customer relationship to an unproven partner in a dominated market.

**Steelman:** Licensing allows rapid, low-capital entry into the consumer console market by leveraging a toy manufacturer's distribution, generating incremental royalty revenue without distracting the core computer business.

**Attack:**
1.  **Economics:** "Small royalty per unit" in a low-cost hardware segment yields trivial absolute profit. It fails to cover the long-term opportunity cost of brand dilution if the product fails or is perceived as cheap.
2.  **Incentive Misalignment:** The licensee bears marketing costs but shares revenue. With "established console makers dominating," the licensee lacks the margin buffer to fight a price war or fund the massive awareness campaigns required to displace incumbents. They will likely under-invest in marketing, ensuring failure.
3.  **Strategic Disconnect:** Consumers may not connect the product to the licensor, meaning the computer maker gains no brand halo. If they *do* connect it and the toy-like console fails, the core computer brand suffers reputational damage.
4.  **Alternatives:** "Do nothing" preserves brand integrity and capital. A direct, focused pilot (even if smaller scale) retains control over quality and marketing messaging, which is critical when entering a hostile market.

**Pre-mortem:** The console launches, sells poorly due to weak marketing and strong competition. The licensee cuts losses and discontinues the line. The computer maker has earned pennies per unit while the market now associates their premium computer brand with a failed, cheap toy.

**Blocker:** The proposal explicitly accepts "small royalties" and "licensee may not market hard" as acceptable risks. This combination guarantees a low-upside, high-reputation-risk outcome. The economic model does not support the marketing spend required to overcome market dominance.

**Kill Criterion:** If the projected royalty per unit does not exceed the estimated marketing spend required per unit to achieve 5% market share within 18 months, the deal destroys value. The proposal admits this math likely doesn't work.

DECISION: NO-GO
