First call: GO. The proposal attacks a clear market gap: luxury quality at a non-luxury price, leveraging existing engineering efficiency to undercut entrenched European rivals.

Steelman: A distinct brand identity, decoupled from the economy parent, can capture value-conscious luxury buyers if the product delivers objective parity in refinement while maintaining a significant price advantage.

Alternatives considered:
1. Do nothing: Cede the high-margin luxury segment entirely to Europeans; remain capped at mid-market volumes.
2. Upgrade the main brand: Impossible to shed the "economy" stigma without confusing the core customer base and diluting the reliability reputation.
3. Joint venture: Slower execution, shared profits, and potential brand conflict.

Fit: Moves the north star from "volume leader in small cars" to "full-spectrum manufacturer," capturing higher margins per unit.

Pre-mortem: Failure occurs if the separate dealer network fails to convey luxury service standards, or if the single flagship model cannot sustain showroom traffic. However, these are execution risks, not fundamental flaws. The "thin line-up" is mitigated by focusing all marketing spend on one perfect product rather than diluting effort across a weak range.

Evidence & Math: The proposal admits heavy sunk costs in development. Canceling now guarantees a total loss of that capital. Launching offers a path to recoupment. The price undercut is the primary lever; if quality matches Europeans, the value proposition is mathematically superior for the consumer.

Blockers: NONE. The risks cited (brand perception, dealer cost, prestige deficit) are inherent to any market entry, not unique blockers. The separate brand structure specifically solves the perception issue. The sunk cost of development makes "do nothing" financially irrational compared to a calculated launch.

Risks: Brand rejection, dealer network profitability, single-model dependency. These become kill criteria: if year-one sales do not meet 60% of forecast, or if dealer satisfaction scores drop below industry average, the program must be reviewed for contraction.

The strongest attack—that buyers won't pay luxury prices to an economy maker—is defeated by the structural separation of the brand and the compelling price-to-quality ratio. History shows value disruptors succeed when the quality gap is closed.

DECISION: GO
