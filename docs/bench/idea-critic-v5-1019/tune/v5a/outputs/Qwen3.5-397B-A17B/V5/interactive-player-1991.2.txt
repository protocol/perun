First call: NO-GO. The $1,000 price point creates an immediate market mismatch against cheaper, evolving alternatives.

**Steelman:** A dedicated $1,000 hub is the only way to guarantee high-fidelity interactive multimedia in the living room before PCs mature.

**Attack:**
1.  **Price vs. Value:** At $1,000, the device costs significantly more than game consoles and approaches low-end PC prices. Unlike a PC, it lacks general utility; unlike a console, it lacks a proven mass-market library. The "middle" position is a value void, not a niche.
2.  **Substitution Threat:** Personal computers are rapidly gaining multimedia capabilities. A PC at a similar price point offers productivity, gaming, and future-proofing. The dedicated player offers only a single, unproven use case.
3.  **Content Chicken-and-Egg:** The proposal admits the interactive title library will be small. High hardware costs deter software developers; limited software deters hardware buyers. Without a killer app to justify the premium, the cycle cannot start.
4.  **Consumer Confusion:** If buyers do not understand the product's purpose, the high price becomes a barrier rather than a signal of quality. Marketing education costs will be immense for a single-function device.

**Alternatives:**
*   **Do Nothing:** Wait for PC multimedia standards to solidify or console makers to add disc drives (cheaper, lower risk).
*   **License Technology:** Sell the disc format/player tech to existing console manufacturers to leverage their distribution and lower price points.
*   **Lower Spec/Lower Price:** Target a sub-$500 price point, sacrificing some fidelity for market penetration.

**Blockers:**
*   **Economic Mismatch:** The $1,000 price exceeds the perceived value of a single-function media player when a multi-function PC is accessible at a comparable cost.
*   **Content Deficit:** The admitted small library cannot support the hardware cost, creating a negative feedback loop.

**Kill Criterion:** If fewer than 50 interactive titles are available at launch, sales will not cover marketing spend required to explain the product.

The proposal fails because it attempts to sell a specialized, expensive solution to a problem that general-purpose computers are about to solve more cheaply, without a content library strong enough to force adoption.

DECISION: NO-GO
