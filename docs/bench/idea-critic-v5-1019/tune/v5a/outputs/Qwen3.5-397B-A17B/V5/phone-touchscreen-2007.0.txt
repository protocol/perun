First call: GO. The claim is that a computer and music player maker can disrupt the phone market by controlling the user experience, despite lacking phone industry experience.

Steelman: By integrating a superior music player and full web browser into a touchscreen device without a physical keyboard, and securing a revenue-sharing partnership with a single carrier that cedes design control, the company can create a premium product that redefines the category and offsets the lack of third-party apps initially.

Skeptic: The premise that carriers will cede control is historically false; however, the revenue-share model aligns incentives differently. The lack of a keyboard contradicts current business norms, but touch interfaces remove mechanical constraints. The risk of no third-party apps is mitigated by shipping robust first-party software (web, email, music).

Better-way: Doing nothing allows music player erosion. Licensing the software to existing handset makers would dilute the experience and fail to secure the revenue share. A multi-carrier launch would force design compromises. The proposed exclusive, high-control path is the only one that preserves the core value proposition.

Fit: This moves the north star of "integrating hardware and software for superior user experience" into the largest adjacent market.

Pre-mortem: Failure occurs if the single carrier undermarkets the device or if the price point proves too high for consumers to accept a device without a keyboard. However, the revenue share mitigates carrier apathy.

Evidence: The company's dominance in music players proves demand for integrated media. Carrier frustration with low-margin handset subsidies creates an opening for a revenue-share model.

Blockers: NONE. The stated risks (no experience, premium price, exclusivity, no apps) are execution challenges, not factual contradictions. The proposal addresses the carrier control issue via the revenue-share incentive.

Risks: Market rejection of touchscreen-only input; limited initial distribution due to exclusivity.

Kill criterion: If pre-orders or first-quarter sales do not exceed the company's best-selling music player units within 12 months, pivot to a multi-carrier strategy or license the OS.

DECISION: GO
