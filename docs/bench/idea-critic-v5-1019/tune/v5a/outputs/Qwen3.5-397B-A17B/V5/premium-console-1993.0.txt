First call: NO-GO. The proposed $700 price point is prohibitive for a mass-market entertainment device in 1993, especially against competitors selling at a loss.

**Steelman:** Licensing shifts capital risk to manufacturers while securing royalty streams, allowing rapid market saturation with high-end tech.

**Attack:**
1.  **Price vs. Value:** At $700, this console costs nearly double the competition. Early adopters pay premiums, but not 100%+ over established 16-bit systems. The "high-end graphics" advantage diminishes if the user base is too small to attract third-party game developers.
2.  **Incentive Misalignment:** Competitors subsidize hardware to maximize game sales (the real profit center). Here, licensees *must* profit on hardware sales, forcing the high price. This creates a ceiling on volume. Low volume means fewer game sales, reducing the royalty stream—the startup's primary revenue.
3.  **Market Dynamics:** Entering a crowded market with a premium-priced, licensed fragmentations strategy is historically fragile. Manufacturers may hesitate to commit to a startup's unproven architecture when they can build their own or partner with incumbents.
4.  **Alternatives:** The "do nothing" or "wait" option preserves capital. A better way is to manufacture a single first-party unit, subsidize it to hit a $400–$500 price point, and lose money initially to secure market share, recouping via game royalties later. This aligns with the proven industry model.

**Pre-mortem:** The project fails because the high price limits install base to <100k units. Developers ignore the platform due to low sales potential. Licensees, stuck with unsold inventory at $700, exit the program. The startup earns zero royalties and collapses.

**Blockers:**
*   **Price Blocker:** The $700 price is a factual barrier to the critical mass needed for the royalty model to work. The proposal admits competitors sell at a loss; this plan demands profit on hardware, guaranteeing a non-competitive price.
*   **Incentive Blocker:** The licensing model prevents the price aggression required to compete.

**Risks:** Manufacturer apathy, developer abandonment, brand irrelevance.

**Kill Criterion:** If pre-orders from licensees do not cover tooling costs within 6 months, or if the final retail price cannot be brought under $500, the project must stop.

The proposal fails the basic math of the console business: volume drives software revenue, and price kills volume.

DECISION: NO-GO
