First call: GO. The strategic necessity of securing a proven hit-making engine outweighs the premium, given the internal unit's failure and the imminent loss of the partner to a rival.

**Steelman:** Acquiring the studio is the only viable path to retain its creative talent and IP pipeline, preventing a competitor from gaining them while instantly revitalizing the buyer's struggling animation division.

**Attack & Options:**
The $7.4B price tag is steep for one film/year, but the alternative ("do nothing") risks zero output from the internal unit and a strengthened rival. A "partial partnership" is dead; talks already broke down over economics. The only real alternative is letting the deal die, which guarantees continued underperformance.

**Fit & Pre-mortem:**
This aligns with the north star of dominating family entertainment. If it fails, it will be due to culture clash or the departure of key creative leaders post-acquisition. However, the proposal explicitly addresses the leadership risk by placing studio heads in charge of the conglomerate's unit. The founder becoming a major shareholder aligns incentives rather than creating conflict; their wealth is now tied to the combined entity's success.

**Evidence & Blockers:**
The facts state the internal unit is failing and the partnership is ending. There is no factual blocker: the price is high, but not impossible for a conglomerate, and the stock deal preserves cash. The risks (culture, dependence) are manageable execution challenges, not deal-breakers. The "dependence on few leaders" is inherent to the asset's value; buying the studio *is* buying those leaders.

**Decision:**
Blockers: NONE. The high price is a cost, not a failure condition.
Risks: Culture integration, key person retention. These are mitigated by the proposed leadership structure and equity alignment.

The cost of delay is the permanent loss of the studio to a rival and continued failure of the internal unit. The proposal offers a definitive solution to an existential threat.

DECISION: GO
