First call: NO-GO. The proposal attempts to solve a product-market fit problem (social engagement) with distribution force (integration) and financial incentives (bonuses), despite three prior failures and a dominant incumbent with entrenched network effects.

Steelman: Integrating social layers into high-utility tools (search, mail) combined with unique video capabilities could leverage existing traffic to overcome cold-start problems where standalone apps failed.

However, the plan fails the blocker test. The core assumption—that forced integration converts search utility into social retention—contradicts the company's own history of three failed attempts using similar distribution advantages. The proposal offers no evidence that "circles" or "hangouts" are sufficient to break the incumbent's lock-in, treating differentiation as a substitute for critical mass. Tying executive bonuses to adoption creates a perverse incentive to prioritize vanity metrics (sign-ups via forced prompts) over genuine engagement, likely exacerbating the risk of annoying existing users and degrading core product utility.

The "do nothing" or "niche tool" alternative is superior: building video hangouts or circles as standalone utilities for specific professional or interest groups avoids antagonizing the core user base and tests value without betting the company's primary revenue drivers. A cheaper, safer path is to launch video features within existing enterprise products where the company already has trust, rather than a consumer social gamble.

The pre-mortem is clear: The launch achieves high initial sign-ups due to forced integration, but daily active usage plummets because users do not find their network there, leading to public perception of the platform as a "ghost town" and resentment toward the core search/mail products. The cost of delay is low; the incumbent is not threatening the core search business, so rushing a flawed social strategy risks the crown jewel for a low-probability moonshot.

Blockers: (1) Historical evidence of three failures suggests the core hypothesis (distribution equals social success) is false; (2) Bonus structures incentivize metric manipulation over product value; (3) No pilot exists to prove "circles" overcome network effects before full integration.

Risks: User churn from core products, brand damage, wasted engineering cycles.

DECISION: NO-GO
