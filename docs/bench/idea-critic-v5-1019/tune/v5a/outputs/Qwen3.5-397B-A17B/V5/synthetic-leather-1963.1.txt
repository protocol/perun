First call: NO-GO. The proposal asks to spend heavily on a product that is more expensive and potentially inferior (comfort) to the incumbent, with no stated advantage other than durability, which may not be the primary driver for premium fashion.

Steelman: A durable, leather-like synthetic offers a consistent, high-performance alternative for shoe manufacturers, justifying a premium launch despite higher costs.

However, the attack reveals fatal flaws in the current plan:
1.  **Economic Disadvantage:** The synthetic costs *more* than leather. In 1963, leather is a known, trusted commodity. Asking manufacturers to pay more for an unproven material that consumers might find less comfortable is a non-starter without a compelling functional breakthrough (e.g., waterproofing, weight) not cited here.
2.  **Adoption Friction:** Manufacturers are unwilling to retool factories. Without a subsidy or a drastic cost reduction, the "spend heavily on marketing" budget is misallocated; the barrier is production integration, not awareness.
3.  **Consumer Risk:** If wearers find it less comfortable, returns will destroy the brand. Durability does not compensate for discomfort in premium footwear.
4.  **Fashion Volatility:** Betting on a single material aesthetic in a trend-driven market is risky.

The proposal fails the "beat do nothing" test. Doing nothing avoids the cost of marketing a product that is currently more expensive and less comfortable than the status quo. The risks are not merely potential; they are inherent to the product's current value proposition. There is no "kill criterion" or pilot phase suggested; it is a blind, heavy spend.

To proceed, the company must first prove that manufacturers will adopt it *despite* the cost and retooling friction, and that consumers prefer it over leather. As proposed, this is a capital destruction event.

Blockers: Higher cost than incumbent; lower comfort profile; manufacturer resistance to retooling.
Risks: Fashion trends shifting; total market rejection.

DECISION: NO-GO
