First call: GO. The proposal addresses a critical vulnerability: commoditization. If buyers cannot distinguish the product, price becomes the only differentiator, inviting clone makers to erode margins. The "Intel Inside" model (implied by the 1991 context and description) shifts power from the immediate buyer (computer makers) to the end-user, creating pull demand that forces computer makers to comply or lose sales.

Steelman: By funding co-op advertising, the chip maker buys leverage over computer makers' messaging at a discount, turning them into distributors of the brand while making the component a primary purchase criterion for consumers.

Alternatives considered:
1. Do nothing: Clones capture market share via confusion; margins collapse.
2. Price cuts: Triggers a race to the bottom, hurting profitability without solving the identification problem.
3. Direct sales to consumers: Impossible given the supply chain structure (sales only to computer makers).

Fit: Aligns with the north star of defending margin and market leadership against clones.

Pre-mortem: Failure occurs if computer makers unite to reject the branding requirements or if consumers ignore the logo. However, the financial incentive (subsidized advertising) aligns interests; rejecting free money to maintain ad autonomy is economically irrational for struggling PC makers. The risk of consumer indifference is real but unproven; the cost of inaction (commoditization) is certain.

Evidence: The proposal cites the specific mechanism of confusion (similar part numbers) and the unprecedented nature of the tactic. Unprecedented does not mean invalid; it often signals a blue-ocean opportunity to redefine category rules.

Blockers: NONE. The risks cited (unprecedented nature, budget share, resentment, lagging metrics) are execution challenges, not factual contradictions. They are mitigated by the structure of the deal (pay-for-performance via ad inclusion) and the desperate need to differentiate.

Risks: High budget concentration; potential channel friction. These become kill criteria: if computer maker participation drops below a threshold or if brand awareness does not lift sales within two quarters, the program must pivot or stop.

The proposal solves the root cause of the threat. The risks are manageable compared to the certainty of failure without action.

DECISION: GO
