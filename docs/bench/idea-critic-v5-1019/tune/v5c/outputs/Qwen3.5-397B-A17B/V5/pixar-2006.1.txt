First call: GO. The strategic necessity of securing a proven hit-making engine outweighs the premium price, given the internal unit's failure and the imminent risk of a competitor acquiring the target.

**Steelman:** Acquiring the studio is the only viable path to immediately restore animation dominance, as internal rehabilitation is too slow and risky compared to integrating a validated creative leadership team.

**Options:**
1.  **Do nothing:** High risk of internal decline and competitor empowerment; guarantees loss of market share.
2.  **New Partnership:** Talks already failed on economics; leverage is now with the studio.
3.  **Acquisition:** High upfront cost ($7.4B) but secures exclusive IP, talent, and immediate pipeline stability.

**Fit:** Aligns with the north star of market leadership in family entertainment. The internal unit's underperformance validates the need for external injection.

**Pre-mortem:** Failure occurs if the creative leaders depart post-acquisition or if the stock swap dilutes existing shareholders without immediate box-office returns. This is a one-way door; delay costs the partnership entirely.

**Evidence & Calculation:** The $7.4B price is steep for one film/year, but the value lies in the back catalog, brand equity, and the *option value* of future hits which the internal unit cannot currently generate. The "risk" of the founder becoming a major shareholder is actually an alignment mechanism, tying their wealth directly to the conglomerate's success.

**Blockers:** NONE. The high price is a function of scarcity and proven success, not a mathematical impossibility. The cultural clash is a management execution risk, not a deal breaker.

**Risks:** Integration friction, key person dependency, stock dilution. These are managed by placing creative leaders in charge (as proposed) and structuring earn-outs or retention packages.

**Kill Criteria:** If the creative leadership team refuses to stay under the new structure, the deal thesis collapses. However, the proposal explicitly addresses this by putting them in charge.

The cost of inaction (losing the studio to a rival, continuing internal failures) exceeds the financial risk of the premium acquisition.

DECISION: GO
