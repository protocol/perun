First call: GO. The $35 price point creates a unique category distinct from general-purpose laptops, targeting education specifically.

Steelman: A ultra-low-cost, single-board computer enables schools to teach programming fundamentals at a fraction of existing hardware costs, relying on user-supplied peripherals to maintain the price floor.

Alternatives:
1. Do nothing: Students continue using expensive, locked-down devices unsuited for hardware-level learning.
2. Cheap tablets: Good for consumption, poor for coding instruction; higher cost, less repairability.
3. Used laptops: High maintenance overhead, inconsistent specs, hidden total cost of ownership.

Fit: Directly addresses the charity's north star of rekindling programming interest by removing cost barriers.

Pre-mortem: Failure occurs if the "bring your own peripheral" requirement proves too high a friction point for non-technical schools, or if supply chain inexperience leads to fatal stockouts. However, these are execution risks, not fundamental flaws. The low price acts as a strong mitigator for adoption friction.

Evidence: The proposal cites a specific, aggressive price target ($35) unachievable by standard laptops in 2012. The risk of sell-outs indicates demand may exceed supply, a positive signal. Lack of manufacturing experience is a valid operational concern but does not invalidate the product-market fit; it suggests a need for strong distribution partners, which are already planned.

Blockers: NONE. No fact presented proves the model cannot work. The peripheral requirement is a trade-off for cost, not a dealbreaker.
Risks: Supply chain inexperience, peripheral friction, potential demand surge. These become kill criteria: monitor sell-through rates and support ticket volume regarding peripheral compatibility.

The alternative of waiting for cheaper laptops ignores the specific educational niche this device fills. The low cost justifies the operational learning curve.

DECISION: GO
