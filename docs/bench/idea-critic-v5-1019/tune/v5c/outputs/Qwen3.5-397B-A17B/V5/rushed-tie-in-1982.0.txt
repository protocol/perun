First call: NO-GO. The timeline and production volume create an unacceptable risk of total capital loss given the constraints.

**Steelman:** A single expert can distill the film's core mechanic into a minimal viable product in five weeks, and the brand's heat guarantees sell-through of a large initial run before Christmas.

**Attack:**
1.  **Capacity vs. Scope:** Five weeks for one programmer to deliver a commercial-grade title based on a complex film property is historically anomalous. Normal development cycles are months or years. Compressing this inevitably sacrifices quality, debugging, and playtesting.
2.  **Inventory Risk:** Ordering a "very large" production run before a single unit is tested or sold violates basic inventory management. If the game is poor (likely due to the rush), the company is left with massive unsold inventory compounding the already "very high" license fee.
3.  **Market Reality:** A hit film does not automatically guarantee a hit game, especially one perceived as a rushed cash-grab. The brand protects the movie, not necessarily a low-quality adaptation.
4.  **Alternatives:** A smaller initial run reduces downside exposure. A delayed launch ensures quality. Doing nothing preserves capital. The proposal beats none of these on safety.

**Blockers:**
*   **Timeline Feasibility:** The stated fact that "five weeks is far shorter than normal development" directly contradicts the assumption that a quality product can be finished in that time by one person. This is a known constraint, not a theoretical risk.
*   **Financial Exposure:** Combining a sunk high license fee with a high-variable cost (large production run) based on an unproven (and likely flawed) product creates a scenario where the downside is catastrophic and the upside is merely "good."

**Risks:** Poor quality, brand damage, total inventory write-off. These are not mere possibilities; they are the probable outcome of the stated plan. The proposal offers no kill criteria, no pilot run, and no quality gate. It demands full commitment based on hope.

The plan fails the basic test of balancing risk and reward. It bets the entire project on a miracle delivery schedule and immediate market saturation without verification.

DECISION: NO-GO
