First call: GO. The request comes from the dominant market leader's largest customer, addressing a verified demand surge where current capacity cannot meet growth. The core claim—that scaling up reduces cost per seat—is mathematically sound for high-growth subsonic travel.

Steelman: Building a massive, convertible wide-body jet now secures the primary customer, locks out competitors, and hedges against both continued subsonic growth and future freight needs, despite high upfront costs.

Alternatives considered:
1. Do nothing: Cedes the growth market to competitors and fails the key customer.
2. Build a slightly larger narrow-body: Fails to achieve the requested 2.5x efficiency gain.
3. Wait for supersonic tech: Speculative timeline; ignores immediate subsonic demand surge.

Fit: Aligns with the vision of market dominance and serving the largest partner. The raised cockpit design specifically addresses the "freighter conversion" option, adding long-term asset value.

Pre-mortem: Failure would result from cost overruns bankrupting the firm before delivery, or the engine failing certification. However, the "one airline ordered" risk is mitigated by that airline being the *largest* customer; their commitment validates the immediate market. The supersonic threat is a known uncertainty, but the convertible design ensures the asset retains utility even if subsonic passenger demand plateaus.

Evidence: The proposal cites a specific customer request and a clear efficiency target (2.5x size). The engine risk is acknowledged but assigned to a supplier, distributing the technical burden.

Blockers: NONE. The financial scale is unprecedented, but the potential loss of the largest customer and market leadership is a greater existential threat. The risks are executional, not fundamental flaws in the concept.

Risks: High capital expenditure, unproven engine technology, supersonic disruption. These are managed via the convertible design (freighter fallback) and the strategic necessity of the move.

Kill criteria: If the sole ordering airline cancels before the factory is operational, or if the engine supplier fails to deliver a certified prototype within the agreed timeline, the project must be paused.

DECISION: GO
