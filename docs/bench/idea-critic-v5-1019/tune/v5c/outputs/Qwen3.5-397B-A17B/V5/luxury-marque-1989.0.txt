First call: GO. The proposal addresses a hard ceiling on brand perception and margin by creating a distinct entity, a standard operator move when the core brand cannot stretch further.

Steelman: A separate luxury brand with a dedicated network and an all-new flagship is the only viable path to bypass the "economy" stigma and compete directly with European incumbents on quality, while using price as a wedge.

Alternatives:
1. Do nothing: Remain capped at mid-price, surrendering margin growth and ceding the entire luxury segment to Europeans.
2. Badge engineering: Upgrading current models fails immediately; the economy reputation contaminates any logo-swapped luxury attempt.
3. Joint venture: Dilutes control and brand identity; unlikely to yield the specific engineering culture required to match European refinement.

Fit: Moves the north star from "volume reliability" to "total market coverage." Assumes the engineering team can deliver subjective refinement matching decades of European heritage.

Pre-mortem: Failure occurs if the separate dealers cannot reach critical mass before cash runs out, or if the single flagship model is perceived as a "rich man's economy car" despite the new badge. This is a one-way door with high sunk costs; delay allows competitors to solidify the segment further.

Evidence: The proposal admits heavy development costs are already sunk (sunk cost fallacy risk if stopped now) and acknowledges the reputation gap. The price-undercutting strategy is the only variable within direct control.

Blockers: NONE. The risks cited (brand perception, dealer cost, thin lineup) are inherent to the strategy, not factual contradictions. The "economy" reputation is precisely why a *separate* brand is proposed; if the brand were shared, this would be a blocker. The thin lineup is a necessary constraint for a launch flagship.

Risks: Brand rejection, dealer profitability, single-model dependency. These become kill criteria: if Year 1 sales do not hit X% of forecast or dealer churn exceeds Y%, pivot or stop.

The claim survives the attack because the separation of brand and network directly neutralizes the primary objection (reputation), and the sunk costs demand completion rather than abandonment.

DECISION: GO
