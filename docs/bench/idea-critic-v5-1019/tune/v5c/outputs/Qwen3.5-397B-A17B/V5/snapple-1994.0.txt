First call: NO-GO. The plan to immediately migrate a cult brand with a "quirky" independent distributor network into a mass-market supermarket system and corporate advertising model ignores the specific assets driving its value: exclusivity and perceived authenticity.

Steelman: The acquisition leverages the buyer's scale to accelerate growth by fixing distribution gaps and amplifying reach, assuming the brand's core appeal is flavor and availability rather than its niche status.

Attack: The proposal's central mechanic—applying the sports drink's mass-market playbook to a premium tea brand—is a category error. The "cult image" is not a byproduct of marketing spend but a result of scarcity and the independent distributor relationships. Moving to supermarket shelves directly attacks the brand's premium positioning and alienates the loyalists who drove the "strong growth." Furthermore, replacing the independent network with corporate distribution risks immediate channel conflict and loss of shelf presence during the transition, exactly when competition from large soft-drink companies is intensifying. The plan assumes the brand is a smaller version of the sports drink; evidence suggests it is a different asset class entirely.

Alternatives:
1. Do nothing: Avoids $1.7B cash outlay and integration risk; preserves status quo but misses growth.
2. Acquire and ring-fence: Keep independent distributors and niche marketing intact; slower growth but preserves brand equity.
3. Proposed plan: High cash cost, high risk of destroying the very loyalty being purchased.

Pre-mortem: One year post-acquisition, sales plateau as core fans abandon the "sold out" brand, while mass-market consumers reject the price point now displayed alongside generic competitors. The independent distributors defect to rival startups.

Blockers: The plan's own logic contradicts its goal. It seeks to buy "loyalty" and "cult image" while explicitly proposing actions (mass distribution, corporate ads) that historically erode those specific traits. The stated fact that the brand relies on a "small, quirky network" makes the immediate shift to "supermarket-focused distribution" a self-defeating strategy.

Risks: Integration failure, brand dilution, competitor exploitation of the transition chaos.

Kill criteria: If post-merger surveys show a >15% drop in "brand uniqueness" perception within 6 months, or if independent distributor retention falls below 50% in Q1, the integration strategy must pivot immediately to a ring-fenced model.

DECISION: NO-GO
