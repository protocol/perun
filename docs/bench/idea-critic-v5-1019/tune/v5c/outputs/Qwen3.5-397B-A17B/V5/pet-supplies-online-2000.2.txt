First call: NO-GO. The plan relies on selling products below cost to acquire customers while simultaneously undertaking massive, cash-intensive expansions (IPO, TV ads, acquisition, warehouses) in a fragile capital market environment.

Steelman: The company aims to dominate market share rapidly by leveraging public capital to subsidize customer acquisition and infrastructure, betting that scale will eventually justify the losses and secure a monopoly position.

Attack:
1.  **Financial Suicide:** Selling below cost (negative gross margin) on heavy, expensive-to-ship items creates a hole that deepens with every sale. An IPO in early 2000 faces imminent market closure (dot-com crash); if capital markets freeze, the company cannot fund the operating losses or the expansion, leading to immediate insolvency.
2.  **Misallocated Capital:** Using scarce IPO proceeds for a national TV campaign and buying a competitor while bleeding money on every order is fiscally irresponsible. It burns cash on low-reversibility bets (warehouses, acquisitions) before proving unit economics.
3.  **Better Way:** The "do nothing" or "consolidate" alternative is superior. Focus on fixing unit economics, raising prices or cutting shipping costs, and surviving the impending capital crunch. Acquiring a competitor with similar flawed economics doubles the liability.

Pre-mortem: Six months post-IPO, the stock crashes as the market closes. The company cannot raise follow-on funding. Massive warehouse lease obligations and the cost of the acquired competitor's losses drain the remaining cash. The TV ad spend fails to convert profitable customers due to the negative margin structure. The company enters bankruptcy with significant debt.

Blockers:
*   **Negative Unit Economics:** Selling below cost on heavy items guarantees cash burn that scales with revenue. No amount of volume fixes a negative margin without infinite capital, which is not guaranteed given the risk of capital markets closing.
*   **Timing:** Executing a capital-intensive expansion in early 2000 ignores the high probability of an imminent market crash, making the IPO proceeds uncertain and the valuation likely unsustainable.

Risks: Competitor spending, execution difficulty. These are secondary to the fundamental mathematical impossibility of sustaining negative margins without guaranteed perpetual capital.

DECISION: NO-GO
