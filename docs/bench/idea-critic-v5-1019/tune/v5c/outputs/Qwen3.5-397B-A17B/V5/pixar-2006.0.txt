Steelman: Acquiring the studio secures a proven hit-making engine to replace a failing internal unit, preventing a rival from gaining the asset and immediately revitalizing the conglomerate's animation slate under proven leadership.

Options:
1. **Buy:** High cost ($7.4B), high integration risk, immediate talent retention via equity, but creates a powerful internal bloc (the founder) that could dictate strategy or destabilize governance.
2. **Do Nothing:** Partnership expires; rival likely signs the studio. Conglomerate's hand-drawn unit continues underperforming. Revenue downside is certain; upside is zero.
3. **Renegotiate Partnership:** Lower capital outlay than acquisition. However, talks already failed over economics, and leverage is now low as expiration nears.

Fit: Aligns with the north star of dominating family entertainment, but the execution method (outright purchase at a premium with stock) threatens financial stability and control.

Pre-mortem: The deal closes, but culture clash drives key creatives to leave within 18 months. The founder, now a top shareholder, blocks necessary cost-cutting or strategic pivots, holding the conglomerate hostage. The stock price drops as the $7.4B valuation proves unsustainable against one film a year.

Evidence: The proposal admits the price is "very high" relative to output (one film/year). It explicitly states the founder becomes a "major shareholder," introducing a confirmed governance risk, not just a theoretical one. The hand-drawn unit's recent failures confirm the need for change, but not necessarily this specific costly fix.

Blockers:
1. **Valuation Mismatch:** $7.4B for one film annually lacks a clear path to ROI without assuming unrealistic per-film gross increases.
2. **Governance Conflict:** Making the seller a major buyer shareholder creates an inherent conflict of interest that cannot be mitigated post-close.

Risks: Culture clash, dependence on specific leaders.

Kill Criteria: If the studio fails to deliver a box-office hit within two years post-acquisition, or if the founder's shareholding blocks strategic decisions, the acquisition is a failure.
