First call: NO-GO. Breaking compatibility with a massive installed base while relying on unproven compiler technology and slow emulation presents an existential market risk that outweighs the theoretical performance gains of a clean-slate design.

**Steelman:** A joint venture can leapfrog incremental 64-bit extensions by the incumbent through a radically simpler hardware design, shifting complexity to compilers to achieve superior parallelism, accepting short-term emulation penalties for long-term dominance.

**Attack:** The proposal fails the "fit" and "evidence" checks. The dominant architecture's moat is not just technical but ecological; a huge installed base means customers will reject slow emulation regardless of peak theoretical speed. The risk that "compilers will be hard to write" is not a future uncertainty but a known historical bottleneck; betting the entire architecture on compiler writers solving an NP-hard scheduling problem perfectly is a single point of failure. Furthermore, the "do nothing" or "extend existing" alternative offers immediate 64-bit capability with zero porting cost, beating this proposal on reversibility and time-to-market. The pre-mortem is clear: the project launches, emulation is 10x slower than native code for key applications, compiler optimization fails to extract promised parallelism, and the market chooses the incumbent's compatible 64-bit extension.

**Blockers:**
1. **Market Reality:** The stated "huge installed base" combined with "slow emulation" creates a guaranteed adoption barrier; no enterprise will migrate critical legacy software to a slower environment without a compelling, immediate native ecosystem, which this proposal lacks at launch.
2. **Technical Dependency:** The core value proposition relies entirely on compilers solving a problem explicitly admitted as "hard to write," making the hardware's potential unreachable without a software miracle.

**Risks:** Rivals extending the dominant architecture (highly probable given the incentive); developer resistance to new instruction sets.

**Kill Criteria:** If a prototype compiler cannot schedule instructions to beat the incumbent's projected 64-bit extension on standard benchmarks within 12 months, or if emulation overhead exceeds 20% for target workloads, the project must pivot to compatibility or terminate.

The proposal asks to bet the company on breaking the very network effect that defines the industry, relying on unproven software to fix a hardware handicap. The safer, cheaper path is to license or extend the dominant architecture.

DECISION: NO-GO
