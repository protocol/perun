First call: NO-GO. The proposed $700 price point is prohibitively high compared to existing consoles, and the licensing model prevents the aggressive hardware subsidization required to compete against established rivals who sell at a loss.

Steelman: Licensing spreads manufacturing risk and capitalizes on multiple partners' distribution networks to rapidly establish a new standard.

However, the attack on the core economics is fatal. Established competitors operate on a "razor-and-blades" model, selling hardware at a loss to secure long-term game royalties. This proposal requires licensees to make a profit on every unit sold while paying royalties to the start-up. This double-margin structure (licensee profit + start-up royalty) forces the $700 retail price. In a market with strong incumbents and price-sensitive early adopters, a console costing double the competition cannot gain the critical mass necessary to attract game developers. Without a large installed base, the software royalty stream—the actual value driver—will never materialize.

Alternatives:
1. Do nothing: Avoids certain capital destruction.
2. Self-fund/Subsidize: The start-up absorbs hardware losses to lower the price, betting entirely on software margins (high risk, but the only viable path to market share).
3. Partner for manufacturing only: Retain control of pricing and subsidies while outsourcing production, rather than licensing the entire business model.

Pre-mortem: The console launches at $700, sells a fraction of the required units, third-party developers ignore the tiny user base, licensees cancel production lines within 18 months due to poor sales, and the brand is tainted as a luxury failure.

Blockers:
1. Price Disadvantage: $700 is unsupported by market willingness to pay given competitor pricing.
2. Flawed Incentives: Licensees cannot compete on price while mandated to earn hardware profit and pay royalties.
3. Market Dynamics: The "sell hardware at a loss" strategy is an industry standard for a reason; ignoring it guarantees failure against entrenched players.

Risks: Execution delay, game library quality (these are secondary to the pricing blocker).

The proposal's own facts confirm the target users are early adopters, but even they face a price barrier too high when functional alternatives exist at half the cost. The licensing model structurally prevents the pricing strategy needed to win.

DECISION: NO-GO
