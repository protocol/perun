First call: NO-GO. The initial four-city test demonstrated weak demand; repeating the same fundamental approach (product usage) in a single city with a name change and free distribution ignores the core signal that the market does not perceive a need.

Steelman: Renaming and gifting samples to offices will uncover a latent use-case (memo-taking) that retail packaging obscured, validating national rollout via direct feedback rather than sales data.

Options:
1. **Proposal:** Rename + free office samples. Cost: Moderate (printing + logistics). Risk: High (confuses branding, samples diverted). Reversibility: Low (brand dilution).
2. **Do Nothing:** Halt rollout. Cost: Zero. Risk: Lost opportunity. Reversibility: High.
3. **Pivot Usage:** Market explicitly as a "bookmark" or "temporary tag" without renaming. Cost: Low. Risk: Niche appeal. Reversibility: High.

Fit: The strategy assumes the product failed due to naming/packaging, not utility. However, the "disappointing sales" across four cities suggests a fundamental lack of perceived value, not a messaging error. Giving away the product to a specific demographic (offices) assumes they have a problem the product solves, which the general market rejected.

Pre-mortem: The office rollout yields 80% positive feedback on the "clever idea," but conversion to paid sales remains near zero because recipients view the free samples as novelties, not necessities. The company burns cash on a national rollout based on vanity metrics (liking the free item) rather than purchase intent. The "rename" fractures any residual brand equity from the first test.

Evidence: The only hard fact is "disappointing sales" in four cities. There is no evidence that a name change alters the utility curve or that office workers have a distinct need compared to the general public. The assumption that "most recipients say they would buy it" is a leading question bias; saying one would buy a free item is not evidence of willingness to pay.

Blockers:
1. **Contradictory Logic:** The plan relies on "purchase intent" surveys after free distribution to justify a paid rollout, ignoring that free uptake rarely correlates with paid conversion, especially after a failed market test.
2. **Resource Misallocation:** A national rollout requires a large budget, yet the proposal seeks to validate demand with a low-fidelity signal (verbal confirmation of free goods).

Risks: Brand confusion from renaming; sunk cost in sample production; false positive validation.

DECISION: NO-GO
