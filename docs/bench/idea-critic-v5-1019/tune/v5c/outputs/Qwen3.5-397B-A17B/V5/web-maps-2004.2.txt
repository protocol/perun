First call: GO. The shift from desktop to browser, combined with satellite imagery and routing, creates a step-change in accessibility and utility that dominates the status quo, despite known risks.

**Steelman:** A free, browser-based map with satellite views and routing will become the default global reference tool because frictionless access outweighs technical limitations and licensing costs, enabling scale that justifies indirect monetization.

**Options:**
1.  **Do nothing:** Retain desktop niche; cede web future to incumbents. High risk of irrelevance.
2.  **License only:** Sell data to others. Low upside, caps growth.
3.  **Proposal (Go):** Build free web app. Highest cost/risk, but only path to mass adoption and platform dominance.

**Fit:** Aligns with the acquirer's core mission to organize information and make it universally accessible. Browser slowness is a transient technical hurdle, not a strategic blocker.

**Pre-mortem:** Failure occurs if licensing costs bankrupt the project before ad revenue scales, or if browser performance remains unusable for panning/zooming. However, the "one-way door" is actually reversible (shut down servers), whereas missing the web transition is irreversible. Cost of delay is total loss of the mapping category to competitors.

**Evidence & Blockers:**
*   *Blocker Check:* No factual blocker exists in the proposal. High costs and competition are *risks*, not blockers, as they are inherent to high-value markets. The lack of a direct revenue model is standard for platform plays aiming for scale first (indirect monetization via search/ads is implied by the acquirer's nature).
*   *Kill Criteria:* If browser frame rates cannot exceed 10fps for map tiles within 12 months, or if licensing costs exceed $X million without user growth traction, pivot or stop.

**Risks:** Licensing expense, browser performance, competitive entrenchment. These are execution challenges, not reasons to abort. The potential upside (global utility layer) dwarfs the sunk costs of development. The "slow browser" objection is a temporary engineering constraint, solvable via AJAX/tile optimization (techniques emerging now).

**Verdict:** The proposal attacks the right friction (download/install) and adds the killer feature (satellite). Risks are manageable; the opportunity is existential.

DECISION: GO
