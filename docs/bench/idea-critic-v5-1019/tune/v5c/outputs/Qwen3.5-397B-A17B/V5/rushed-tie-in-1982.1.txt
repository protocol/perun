First call: NO-GO. The compressed timeline and massive inventory commitment create an unacceptable risk profile given the high sunk cost of the license.

Steelman: A rapid, single-developer sprint can capture immediate holiday market share before competitors, leveraging the film's peak popularity to offset quality compromises with brand recognition.

However, the attack on this claim reveals fatal flaws. The five-week timeline is not just "short"; it is functionally incompatible with creating a stable, engaging product that justifies a "very large" production run. Normal development cycles exist to mitigate bugs and ensure gameplay loops are fun; skipping this guarantees a poor-quality product. In the software and entertainment industry, reputation damage from a broken launch can permanently tarnish the brand, negating the value of the film license.

Furthermore, the inventory strategy is economically unsound. Ordering a "very large" run based on an unproven, rushed product assumes demand will remain high regardless of quality. This ignores the "better way": a smaller, initial production run to test market reception and quality, allowing for a reorder if successful. The current proposal forces an all-or-nothing bet where the downside (massive unsold inventory of a broken game) vastly outweighs the upside (selling out a mediocre game).

The risks are not merely theoretical; they are structural. The combination of extreme time pressure, single-point failure (one programmer), and irreversible capital commitment (large manufacturing run) creates a scenario where failure is the most probable outcome. The high license fee should dictate caution to protect the investment, not recklessness to rush it.

Blockers: The proposed timeline makes quality assurance impossible, directly contradicting the requirement for a sellable product. The inventory commitment lacks a fallback, turning a business risk into a potential catastrophe.
Risks: Brand damage, total loss of manufacturing capital, failure to recoup license fees.

The proposal fails to account for the necessity of quality control and demand validation. It demands an irreversible commitment based on hope rather than evidence.
