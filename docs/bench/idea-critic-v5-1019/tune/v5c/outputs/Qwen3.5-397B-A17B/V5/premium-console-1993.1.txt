First call: NO-GO. The proposed $700 price point is prohibitively high compared to existing consoles, and the licensing model creates a fundamental economic conflict.

Steelman: Licensing to multiple manufacturers could rapidly expand market reach and share hardware risk, allowing the start-up to focus on software royalties while partners compete on production efficiency.

However, the attack on this claim reveals fatal flaws in the current facts:
1.  **Economic Misalignment:** Established competitors sell hardware at a loss to subsidize game sales. Licensees in this proposal must profit on hardware *and* pay royalties. This double margin requirement forces the $700 price, making the console uncompetitive against subsidized rivals.
2.  **Channel Conflict:** Multiple licensees building the same design will compete on price, eroding the very hardware margins they need to survive. This encourages cost-cutting that damages the "high-end" brand promise.
3.  **Market Timing:** Entering a crowded market with a premium-priced device, without the ecosystem leverage of an incumbent or the loss-leader pricing strategy of a challenger, offers no clear path to adoption.

Alternatives:
*   **Do nothing:** Avoids certain failure in a hostile market.
*   **Single Partner/Manufacture Own:** Build one high-quality unit (possibly at a loss) to control price and brand, recouping costs via software. This aligns with industry standards.
*   **Niche High-End:** Target a much smaller, specialized market where $700 is acceptable, abandoning the "standard for next generation" mass-market goal.

Pre-mortem: The venture fails because licensees cannot lower prices to compete without going bankrupt, retailers refuse to stock an overpriced console, and gamers choose established, cheaper ecosystems. The "standard" is never set because the hardware never reaches critical mass.

Blockers:
*   The requirement for licensee hardware profit + royalty directly causes the non-competitive $700 price.
*   The market already has strong competitors using a loss-leader model this proposal cannot match.

Risks:
*   Fragmented quality control across licensees.
*   Lack of unified marketing push.

The proposal's own facts confirm the target users (gamers) have cheaper alternatives, and the economic structure prevents price competition. This is a blocker, not a risk.

DECISION: NO-GO
