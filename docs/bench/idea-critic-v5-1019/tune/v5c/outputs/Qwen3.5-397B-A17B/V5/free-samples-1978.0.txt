First call: NO-GO. The initial four-city test demonstrated weak market demand, and distributing free samples to a single city's offices does not address the fundamental lack of perceived need while incurring unrecoverable costs.

**Steelman:** Renaming and targeted sampling will uncover a hidden niche of office workers who value re-positionable notes, validating a national rollout through expressed intent rather than past sales.

**Attack:**
1.  **Evidence Contradiction:** The proposal admits the first test showed "little demand." A rename is a cosmetic fix that cannot solve a functional mismatch if users "may not see a need."
2.  **Metric Flaw:** The success criterion is recipients *saying* they would buy. Stated intent in a free-sample scenario is notoriously unreliable and historically overestimates actual conversion, especially for a product already failing to sell.
3.  **Better Way:** The "do nothing" or "kill project" option preserves capital. A cheaper alternative is a paid pilot in one city with the new name to test actual willingness to pay, not just sample acceptance.
4.  **Pre-mortem:** The project fails because the company spends money on samples and a rebrand, gathers false-positive verbal interest, executes a costly national rollout, and faces the same disappointing sales as the first test, now with higher sunk costs.
5.  **Blocker:** The core claim relies on the assumption that "renaming" fixes "no perceived need." The existing evidence (disappointing sales, little demand) directly contradicts the likelihood of success. The proposed metric (verbal intent) is insufficient to overturn the hard data of zero/low sales.

**Blockers:**
*   Existing data shows little demand; no evidence suggests a name change alters utility or necessity.
*   Success metric (stated intent) is decoupled from revenue and prone to false positives.

**Risks:**
*   Wasted budget on samples and rebranding.
*   False confidence leading to larger losses in national rollout.

The proposal attempts to validate a failing product with a low-fidelity metric (verbal intent) while ignoring hard sales data. It does not prove the product is needed, only that people will accept free items.

DECISION: NO-GO
