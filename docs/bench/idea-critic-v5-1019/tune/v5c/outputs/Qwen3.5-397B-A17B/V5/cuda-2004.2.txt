First call: NO-GO. The proposal commits significant capital to build a market that does not exist, raising costs on every unit sold in a hyper-competitive core business without any committed revenue to offset the risk.

Steelman: Embedding general-purpose compute capability in every consumer chip now creates an unavoidable industry standard for parallel computing, locking out rivals and capturing a future trillion-dollar scientific market before it forms.

Options:
1. **Do nothing:** Maintain cost leadership in gaming; wait for clear demand signals before investing.
2. **Targeted acceleration:** Build the toolchain only for high-margin workstation cards where scientists already pay premiums, avoiding mass-market cost bloat.
3. **Proposed plan:** Subsidize R&D via every gaming card sold.
The proposed plan fails to beat "do nothing" or "targeted acceleration" because it imposes a cost penalty on the entire volume base to serve a niche with zero willingness to pay today.

Fit: This contradicts the immediate survival need to win the core gaming architecture race. Diverting engineering focus to unproven compiler tech risks losing the primary revenue engine that funds all R&D.

Pre-mortem: The program burns cash for three years; the compiler remains buggy because no real users stress-test it; the added silicon area reduces gaming performance or raises prices slightly, causing gamers to switch to the rival's faster/cheaper chips. The company loses its core market share, goes bankrupt, and the "future" market is captured by a competitor who waited for clarity. This is a one-way door with massive downside; the cost of delay is negligible since the market isn't there yet.

Evidence: The proposal admits "no large paying market exists" and "academic users are experimenting with hacked APIs." There is no evidence of demand elasticity or customer commitment.

Blockers:
1. **Unit economics failure:** Raising unit costs on 100% of volume to serve 0% of paying customers violates basic margin discipline without a signed anchor tenant.
2. **Strategic distraction:** Engineering resources are finite; splitting focus between a life-or-death gaming rivalry and a speculative compiler project invites defeat in the core business.

Risks: Niche demand remains niche; software adoption fails; rival undercuts price/perf.

Kill criteria: None can be set because the baseline premise (market existence) is false.

DECISION: NO-GO
