First call: GO. The current product is functionally inadequate for the business market, which represents the only viable path to significant scale. Staying static guarantees irrelevance.

Steelman: A dedicated business machine with essential usability features (lowercase, 80-column) is required to capture the enterprise sector, necessitating a controlled transition from the hobbyist-focused predecessor despite execution risks.

Alternatives considered:
1. Do nothing: Retain the limited device. Result: Cedes the business market entirely to competitors; long-term revenue ceiling remains low.
2. Incremental update: Add features to the existing chassis. Result: Likely insufficient to overcome architectural limitations; confuses branding.
3. The proposal: Build a successor. Result: High execution risk, but the only path to market leadership.

Fit: Aligns with the strategic necessity to evolve from a niche hobbyist toy to a universal business tool.

Pre-mortem: The project fails if the complex new design ships late or with critical reliability bugs, causing business buyers to reject the unproven hardware while alienating the existing user base by killing the original too soon. This is a one-way door; once the old line is cut, there is no retreat. Cost of delay is total loss of the 1980-1981 business adoption window.

Evidence: The premise rests on the verified fact that business users explicitly require lowercase and 80-column displays, which the current unit lacks. No data suggests these users will accept the current machine.

Blockers: NONE. The risks cited (complexity, schedule, migration friction, reliability) are execution challenges, not factual contradictions. They do not prove the plan fails as proposed; they define the difficulty of the work. The fear of hurting revenue by phasing out the current product is invalid if the current product cannot grow revenue further due to feature deficits.

Risks: Schedule slippage, hardware defects, software ecosystem lag, channel confusion. These become kill criteria: if a working prototype with full reliability is not demonstrated within 6 months, or if key software partners do not commit before launch, the project must pivot or stop.

The absence of a pilot is not a blocker given the clear market signal; the "pilot" is the rigorous engineering validation required before mass production.

DECISION: GO
