First call: NO-GO. The proposal commits significant capital to build a market that does not exist, directly threatening the company's survival in its only proven revenue stream (gaming) during a fierce competitive war.

**Steelman:** Embedding general-purpose compute capabilities in every consumer chip now creates an inevitable, dominant platform for scientific computing, securing a monopoly before rivals can react.

**Attack:**
1.  **Financial Suicide:** Raising unit costs on *every* chip to serve a non-existent market reduces margins on the core gaming business. In a "race with its main rival," this hands the competitor a price or performance advantage in the only segment that currently pays bills.
2.  **Misaligned Incentives:** Academic users experimenting with "hacked APIs" represent curiosity, not committed demand. Building a full compiler and toolchain requires massive engineering hours diverted from the core graphics race, with no guarantee these researchers will convert to paying enterprise customers.
3.  **Better Way:** The "do nothing" or "wait and see" approach preserves cash and engineering focus. If a market emerges, the company can add dedicated hardware later when customers are willing to pay a premium, rather than subsidizing silicon for everyone now.
4.  **Pre-mortem:** Two years later, the company has lost significant gaming market share due to higher costs/lower performance, the scientific toolchain is unfinished due to staffing shortages, and the "niche" remains academic with no revenue. The company is acquired or bankrupted by the rival who focused on gaming.

**Blockers:**
*   **Fact:** No large paying market exists; demand is purely experimental.
*   **Fact:** Core revenue is 100% dependent on gaming/workstation graphics.
*   **Fact:** A direct rival exists in a "core race."
*   **Conclusion:** The proposal requires sacrificing the known, vital business for a hypothetical one without a single committed customer. This violates basic capital allocation logic for a public company in a competitive hardware war.

**Risks:** Engineering distraction, cost overrun, competitor gaining ground, total market failure. These are not just risks; given the lack of revenue, they are probable outcomes.

DECISION: NO-GO
