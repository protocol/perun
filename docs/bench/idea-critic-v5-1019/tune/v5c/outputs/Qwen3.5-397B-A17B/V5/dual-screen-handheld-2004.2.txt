First call: GO. The proposal avoids a direct hardware war against a superior graphics rival by pivoting to a novel interaction model (dual-screen/touch) and a lower price point, aiming to expand the market rather than fight for the existing core.

Steelman: By offering unique gameplay impossible on the rival's single-screen device at a lower cost, the company can attract non-traditional gamers and leverage its strong first-party software library to define a new category, rendering the rival's graphical superiority less relevant.

Options:
1. **Proposal**: Differentiate via touch/dual-screen, lower price, broaden audience. Cost: Moderate (new form factor). Risk: Developer confusion, gimmick perception. Reversibility: Low once manufactured.
2. **Match Rival**: Build stronger graphics/media handheld. Cost: High (R&D race). Risk: Entering competitor's stronghold, margin erosion. Reversibility: Low.
3. **Do Nothing**: Keep old handheld only. Cost: Low. Risk: Losing relevance as market shifts to media/graphics. Reversibility: High.
The proposal beats "do nothing" by addressing the threat proactively and beats "matching the rival" by avoiding a costly spec war it might lose.

Fit: Assumes the goal is market expansion and longevity over winning a spec-sheet battle. This aligns with leveraging software IP over hardware commoditization.

Pre-mortem: Failure occurs if third-party developers refuse to support the unique architecture, leaving a software drought, or if the touch interface is deemed too unintuitive for mass adoption. However, keeping the older model on sale mitigates the risk of alienating the core base during the transition.

Evidence: The proposal relies on the assumption that "gameplay innovation" outweighs "graphical fidelity" for a broader demographic. While touch was unproven in 2004, the lower price point reduces the barrier to entry for skeptics.

Blockers: NONE. No factual contradiction exists in the proposal; the risks (developer confusion, gimmick label) are execution challenges, not fundamental flaws. The strategy explicitly accepts these risks to gain a unique market position.

Risks: Developer adoption lag; consumer confusion. These become kill criteria: if major first-party titles do not demonstrate the dual-screen value within 6 months of launch, or if sales to non-core demographics remain below 20% after one year, the strategy must pivot.

DECISION: GO
