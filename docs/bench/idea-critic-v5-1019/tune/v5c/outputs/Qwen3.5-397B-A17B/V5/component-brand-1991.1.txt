First call: GO. The proposal addresses a critical vulnerability: commoditization. If buyers cannot distinguish the component, price becomes the only differentiator, inviting clone makers to erode margins. The "ingredient branding" strategy shifts power from computer makers to the chip maker by creating pull demand.

Steelman: By subsidizing ads that feature the chip logo, the company bypasses the computer maker's reluctance to highlight a component, forcing consumer awareness that makes the chip a purchase requirement rather than an afterthought.

Alternatives:
1. Do nothing: Clones capture market share via confusion; margins collapse as the product becomes a commodity.
2. Price cuts: Triggers a race to the bottom, destroying profitability without solving the identification problem.
3. Direct-to-consumer sales: Impossible given the current distribution model (selling only to manufacturers).

Fit: This moves the north star from "volume sold to manufacturers" to "preferred brand by end-users," securing long-term pricing power.

Pre-mortem: Failure occurs if computer makers refuse the co-op funds or if consumers ignore the logo. However, the financial incentive (paid advertising) aligns manufacturer interests with the campaign. The risk of resentment is outweighed by the benefit of subsidized marketing spend.

Evidence & Math: The proposal admits the budget impact is large, but the cost of inaction (lost differentiation) is existential. The "unprecedented" nature of the risk is actually the opportunity; first-mover advantage in component branding creates a moat clones cannot cross without their own massive spend.

Blockers: NONE. The risks cited (unprecedented approach, manufacturer resentment, lagging metrics) are execution challenges, not factual contradictions. They are mitigated by the financial leverage offered to partners and the clarity of the threat (clones).

Risks: High budget consumption; delayed ROI visibility. These become kill criteria: if consumer recall does not rise by X% within 12 months, or if manufacturer participation falls below Y%, the program stops.

The claim that "benefits may not show in sales" is true for any marketing, but the specific mechanism (making buyers ask by name) directly attacks the clone confusion problem stated in the facts.

DECISION: GO
