First call: NO-GO. The proposal targets a mass market ("many new home users") with a product requiring hardware ("high-end PC") that the target market explicitly lacks, while facing immediate obsolescence from the company's own roadmap.

Steelman: A high-fidelity, cartoon-based shell could drastically reduce anxiety for novice users if they possessed the hardware to run it, providing a bridge until the next OS release.

Attack:
1.  **Market Mismatch:** The core constraint is fatal. "New home users" in 1995 predominantly own mid-to-low range PCs. Requiring a "high-end PC" excludes the very demographic intended to solve the "hard to use" problem. The addressable market is too small to sustain a $100 SKU.
2.  **Cannibalization & Obsolescence:** A "major new operating-system release" is imminent. Selling a separate $100 shell months before a built-in interface update guarantees massive customer dissatisfaction and returns. It trains users on a paradigm the company itself is about to abandon.
3.  **Adoption Friction:** Experienced users (who influence purchases) will reject the "patronizing" aesthetic, while the "few third-party add-ons" means the rooms remain empty, reducing utility immediately after novelty wears off.

Alternatives:
*   **Do Nothing:** Wait for the upcoming OS release to integrate usability features natively. Cost: $0. Risk: Short-term friction for novices. Reversibility: N/A.
*   **Software Only (Low Spec):** Build a simplified launcher without high-end graphics. Cost: Low. Risk: Less engaging. Reversibility: High.
*   **The Proposal:** Cost: High dev/marketing. Risk: Total market mismatch, channel conflict, brand damage. Reversibility: Low (once shipped).

Fit: This contradicts the strategic need to unify the user base ahead of the major OS launch. It fragments the experience and dates the brand.

Pre-mortem: Six months post-launch, sales are negligible because customers cannot run the software. Those who bought high-end PCs feel cheated when the new OS arrives with similar features built-in. The project is canceled, writing off development costs.

Blockers:
1.  **Hardware Dissonance:** Target users do not own the required hardware.
2.  **Strategic Collision:** Imminent native OS release renders the product redundant before ROI is possible.

Risks: Patronizing perception; lack of ecosystem.

The hardware requirement alone invalidates the business case for a mass-market solution, and the timing ensures commercial failure.

DECISION: NO-GO
