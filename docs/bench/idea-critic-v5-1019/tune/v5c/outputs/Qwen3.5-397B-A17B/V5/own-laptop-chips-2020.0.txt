First call: GO. The company already possesses the core competency in silicon design for its mobile devices, suggesting a high probability of successful technology transfer to laptops, provided the software compatibility risk is managed.

**Steelman:** Leveraging existing mobile silicon expertise to build custom laptop processors will yield superior performance-per-watt and deeper hardware-software integration, creating a unique market advantage that off-the-shelf supplier chips cannot match.

**Attack & Analysis:**
The primary blocker claim is software compatibility. However, this is an execution challenge, not a fundamental impossibility. The industry has successfully navigated architecture shifts before (e.g., PowerPC to Intel, Intel to ARM) via translation layers and developer incentives. The "unproven" nature of the chips is a temporary state resolved by testing; it is not a permanent disqualifier.

The cost of engineering time is significant but represents an investment in long-term margin expansion and product differentiation, not a sunk loss. Ending the supplier relationship is a strategic choice to capture value internally, not a failure mode. The risk of buyer confusion is mitigated by clear marketing focusing on performance benefits rather than technical specifications.

**Alternatives:**
1.  **Do nothing:** Continue relying on supplier roadmaps, accepting commodity performance and limited differentiation.
2.  **Hybrid approach:** Split lines, which increases complexity and dilutes the supply chain leverage without capturing the full benefit of custom silicon.
3.  **Custom Silicon (Proposed):** High upfront cost, high long-term reward, full control over the product roadmap.

**Pre-mortem:** If this fails, it will be because the translation layer for legacy software was too slow, causing user churn. This is avoidable with aggressive developer tooling and a phased rollout.

**Blockers:** NONE. The proposal acknowledges the risks (software, cost, transition) but does not present a factual contradiction proving failure. The risks are manageable execution hurdles, not show-stoppers. The company's existing success with mobile chips proves the design capability exists.

**Risks:** Software ecosystem friction, initial performance gaps in pro workflows, supply chain ramp-up delays. These become kill criteria: if legacy app performance drops below 80% of native speed after 12 months, or if developer adoption lags by >30% against targets, the strategy must pivot.

The strategic upside of vertical integration outweighs the transitional friction. The company has the unique asset (mobile chip design) required to make this viable.

DECISION: GO
