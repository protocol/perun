First call: NO-GO. The proposal targets a market segment (home users) with a product requiring hardware (high-end PC) that the segment largely does not possess, while facing immediate obsolescence from the company's own roadmap.

Steelman: A specialized, intuitive interface could accelerate adoption for novices if hardware constraints were irrelevant and no superior native solution existed.

Alternatives:
1. Do nothing: Wait for the upcoming major OS release to address usability natively, avoiding fragmentation.
2. Improve documentation/tutorial layers for the current OS: Lower cost, no hardware barrier, reversible.
3. The proposal: High cost, high hardware barrier, creates a dead-end product line.

Fit: This contradicts the strategic reality of an imminent major OS release. Investing engineering and marketing capital in a standalone shell that will likely be rendered redundant or incompatible within months is a misallocation of resources. It cannibalizes attention from the core platform transition.

Pre-mortem: The product launches, but sales flatline because 80% of target homes cannot run it. Early adopters feel insulted by the "cartoon" aesthetic. Six months later, the new native OS release includes improved usability features, making this standalone shell obsolete. Inventory is liquidated at a loss, and the brand is associated with an expensive, unnecessary add-on.

Evidence & Calculation: The proposal admits the need for a "high-end PC" while acknowledging "many new home users find computers hard to use." In 1995, high-end PCs are a minority in homes. The addressable market is effectively zero. Furthermore, the "major new operating-system release... coming within months" acts as a hard blocker; releasing a competing interface now guarantees channel confusion and technical debt.

Blockers:
1. Hardware mismatch: The target audience lacks the required hardware to run the software.
2. Strategic collision: An internal, superior, and native solution is imminent, making this product a stranded asset.
3. Value proposition failure: The cost ($100) plus hardware upgrade cost exceeds the value of a temporary interface layer.

Risks: User alienation, channel conflict, brand dilution. These are secondary to the hard blockers.

DECISION: NO-GO
