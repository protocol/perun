First call: NO-GO. Buying a failing hardware unit to solve a software ecosystem deficit contradicts basic market dynamics; developers follow users, not owned factories.

**Steelman:** Vertical integration is the only lever left to prevent the sole remaining hardware partner from defecting to the rival platform, preserving the OS's foothold long enough to court developers.

**Options:**
1.  **Do nothing:** The manufacturer defects; the OS dies immediately.
2.  **Subsidize the partner:** Pay them to stay without owning them. Lower capex, but no control over product quality or roadmap; they may still leave once subsidies end.
3.  **Buy the division (Proposal):** High capex (€5.4B) and integration risk, but guarantees the hardware channel stays open and allows direct control over the user experience.

**Fit:** The goal is OS survival. However, the proposal assumes owning a shrinking hardware business fixes an app shortage. History shows apps follow market share, not ownership structures. If sales are already falling, owning the decline accelerates cash burn without guaranteeing developer interest.

**Pre-mortem:** Two years post-acquisition, the company is stuck with a bloated, unprofitable hardware division, alienated other handset partners who fled to the rival, and the app gap remains because total user base continued to shrink. The €5.4B is gone, drained by operational losses.

**Evidence & Math:** The handset maker's sales are *already falling*. Paying a premium (€5.4B) for a depreciating asset to solve a "chicken-and-egg" app problem is structurally unsound. Developers will not build for a platform with low single-digit share just because one manufacturer is owned; they need total market volume. The cost of integrating a large, likely unionized workforce into a software culture is a massive distraction from the core need: making the OS attractive.

**Blockers:**
1.  **False Premise:** The proposal claims hardware ownership fixes an app-ecosystem problem. The facts state sales are falling and the rival has the apps. Owning a losing hardware business does not create demand; it merely monetizes the decline.
2.  **Strategic Contradiction:** Other handset makers will view this as competition, not partnership, likely accelerating their exit to the rival platform, shrinking the total OS footprint further.

**Risks:** Integration failure, continued cash burn, partner alienation. These are secondary to the fundamental flaw: you cannot buy your way out of an ecosystem deficit by acquiring a failing supply chain.

DECISION: NO-GO
