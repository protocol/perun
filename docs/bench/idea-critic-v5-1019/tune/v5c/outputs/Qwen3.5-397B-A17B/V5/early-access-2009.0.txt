First call: NO-GO. A solo developer selling an unfinished product in 2009 without marketing or support capacity invites immediate reputational damage and refund churn that halts development.

Steelman: Releasing early monetizes passion to fund completion, leveraging community feedback to prioritize high-value features while generating cash flow.

Alternatives:
1. Do nothing: Finish privately; risk running out of money or building unwanted features.
2. Free private beta: Gather feedback without financial transaction friction or public judgment.
3. Seek publisher/micro-grant: Secure funds to finish before facing the market.

Fit: Assumes "survival via revenue" is the north star. However, the stated facts (solo, no support, rough state) contradict the requirement for a sustainable early access model.

Pre-mortem: The game launches; players encounter bugs and lack of content. With no support bandwidth, negative reviews accumulate instantly. In 2009, "Early Access" is not an established, trusted category (unlike post-2013); it is seen as "broken software." Sales flatline, refunds spike, and the developer burns out trying to fix critical issues instead of building new content.

Door/Delay: Two-way door (can stop selling), but reputation damage is sticky. Cost of delay: Potential loss of cash flow needed to eat/code.

Evidence Check: The proposal admits "buyers may judge by rough state" and "solo dev cannot support large audience." These are not risks; they are stated facts that directly block the core mechanism (selling to many). The market context (2009) lacks the infrastructure for successful indie early access.

Blockers:
1. Capacity mismatch: The plan requires supporting a "large audience" (implied by public sale) but explicitly states the developer "cannot support a large audience."
2. Market timing: In 2009, selling unfinished games publicly typically results in being labeled "vaporware" or "scam" without an established trust framework like modern Early Access.
3. Contradictory facts: The proposal acknowledges players may stop buying upon seeing the state, yet relies on them continuing to buy to fund development.

Risks: Reputation loss, burnout, refund exhaustion.

Kill Criteria: If a free private beta of 50 users does not yield 20% retention and specific feature requests within 2 weeks, do not proceed to public sale.

DECISION: NO-GO
