First call: NO-GO. The proposal states a critical negative as fact—"existing software must keep running"—but offers no evidence that the phone-based design approach can execute this legacy laptop software without performance collapse or incompatibility. A design method optimized for mobile power efficiency and thermal constraints is not inherently capable of sustaining laptop-class workloads or binary compatibility with a mature desktop ecosystem.

Steelman: Leveraging existing silicon IP to unify the product architecture will yield superior long-term margins and integration, provided the performance gap is closed.

However, the attack reveals a fatal blocker under the "Kill Check." The proposal explicitly admits the laptop chips are "unproven against the supplier's" while simultaneously demanding existing software compatibility. In the laptop market, performance parity is not a risk; it is a prerequisite for entry. If the new chips cannot match the supplier's performance while running legacy software, the product fails immediately. The proposal treats this uncertainty as a manageable risk rather than a validated capability. Furthermore, ending the supplier relationship before proving the replacement creates a single point of failure with no fallback.

The "better way" analysis shows that a phased approach (hybrid lineup) or a performance-first validation pilot is superior to a blanket line migration. Moving the entire line based on an unproven design approach violates the principle of reversible doors; once the supplier relationship is severed and buyers are confused by underpowered machines, the brand damage is irreversible.

The facts provided indicate the core mechanism for success (software compatibility + performance parity) is assumed, not demonstrated. The proposal asks to bet the laptop division on a hypothesis that contradicts the stated constraint of maintaining software utility on unproven hardware.

Blockers: The proposal states the chips are unproven against the incumbent while requiring full software compatibility, creating a high probability of product failure upon launch.
Risks: Engineering distraction, buyer confusion, supplier retaliation.

DECISION: NO-GO
