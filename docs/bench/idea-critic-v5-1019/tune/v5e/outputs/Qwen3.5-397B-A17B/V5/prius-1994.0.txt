Steelman: Being first establishes a decade-long moat in hybrid technology, forcing competitors to play catch-up while securing early adopter loyalty before regulations inevitably tighten.

However, the attack on this steelman reveals fatal flaws based strictly on the provided facts. The "first mover" advantage collapses without a demand signal; if consumers will not pay a premium for fuel efficiency when gas is cheap, the product sits unsold. The novelty of the power-split transmission and NiMH batteries introduces extreme reliability risks. In the automotive sector, warranty exposure from unproven high-voltage components in mass production can bankrupt a program faster than being late to market.

Alternatives:
1. Do nothing: Preserves capital and avoids warranty catastrophe while waiting for fuel prices to rise or competitors to validate the tech.
2. Limited pilot: Test the powertrain in a low-volume fleet to prove durability before committing to a 1997 mass-production schedule.
3. Wait for regulation: Align development with impending mandates to guarantee a market.

The proposal fails the blocker test. The combination of "no near-term demand signal" and "unproven battery durability" constitutes a concrete failure scenario: mass-producing a costly, potentially unreliable vehicle that the market does not yet want. This is not a calculated risk; it is a gamble with undefined downsides and no immediate upside. The absence of a pilot program or a specific trigger (like a fuel price threshold or regulatory date) means the company is betting on a future that may not arrive in time to recoup the investment.

The pre-mortem is clear: The car launches in 1997, suffers early battery failures leading to massive recalls, and sales flatline because the fuel savings do not offset the purchase price. The brand reputation takes a decade to recover.

Blockers: No demand signal at current fuel prices; unproven durability creates unlimited warranty liability; no pilot phase to de-risk the novel transmission.
Risks: Competitor leapfrogging if they wait and learn from these failures; sunk cost if regulations are delayed.

DECISION: NO-GO
