First call: NO-GO. The proposal admits a fundamental technical contradiction: combining two incompatible networks where one is data-limited, requiring the acquirer to run legacy equipment indefinitely. In 2005, the industry trajectory is decisively toward high-speed data (3G/4G evolution), not maintaining siloed voice-only infrastructure. Acquiring a "high-value" base that relies on technology the buyer cannot efficiently scale or integrate creates a structural liability, not an asset.

Steelman: The merger secures a sticky, high-margin enterprise segment (construction/business) via a unique push-to-talk feature, justifying the $36 billion premium and integration headache as a defensive moat against competitors lacking this specific utility.

Attack: The "moat" is built on rotting pilings. Running incompatible legacy networks fractures operational efficiency, doubling maintenance costs while delaying the unified data roadmap essential for future revenue. The $36 billion valuation likely prices in synergies that technical incompatibility makes impossible to realize. Goodwill from such a massive premium becomes an immediate impairment risk if integration stalls or the legacy tech becomes unsupportable. The "high-value" customers are hostage to a dying technology; migrating them to the buyer's modern stack risks churn, while keeping them on legacy drains capital.

Alternatives:
1. Do nothing: Preserve capital for organic data network expansion; avoid integration chaos.
2. Partner/License: Contract for the push-to-talk service without acquiring the incompatible network infrastructure.
3. Acquire only assets: Buy the customer list and software IP, leaving the legacy network behind.

Pre-mortem: Two years post-merger, integration costs have blown out by 40%, the legacy network suffers outages due to parts scarcity, and enterprise customers defect to agile competitors offering modern data-enabled push-to-talk. The $36 billion price tag anchors the stock price down as goodwill is written off.

Blockers:
1. Technical incompatibility prevents the core synergies required to justify the $36 billion price.
2. Strategy misalignment: Investing heavily in legacy, data-limited tech contradicts the 2005 market shift toward mobile data.

Risks: Customer churn during migration; regulatory scrutiny on market consolidation.

The plan fails the basic test of strategic fit and technical feasibility as proposed. The cost of running dual incompatible networks negates the value of the acquired customer base.

DECISION: NO-GO
