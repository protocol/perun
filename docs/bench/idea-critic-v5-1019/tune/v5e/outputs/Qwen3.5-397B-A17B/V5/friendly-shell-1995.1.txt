Attack: The proposal fails on its own evidence. It requires hardware the target demographic does not possess ("needs more memory than most home PCs have"). Launching a $100 product for high-end machines ignores the installed base. Furthermore, the "major new operating-system release... coming within months" renders this standalone shell obsolete before it gains traction. Experienced users will reject the patronizing interface, and the lack of third-party add-ons limits utility. The cost of delay is low because the competitor's OS update likely addresses usability natively, making this a redundant, expensive stopgap.

Blockers:
1. Hardware mismatch: The product requires high-end PCs, but most home users have low-end machines (stated fact).
2. Obsolescence risk: A major OS update with a new interface arrives in months, likely cannibalizing this shell (stated fact).
3. No stated edge: The proposal offers no advantage over the upcoming native OS update for the few who can run it.

Risks:
1. Patronizing tone alienating power users.
2. Lack of ecosystem (add-ons).
3. High price point ($100) for a shell.

The proposal's own facts confirm the target audience cannot run the software, and the timing guarantees immediate competition from a platform-level solution. There is no viable path to profitability or adoption as described.
