First call: NO-GO. The proposal identifies a real need (hands-free computing) but fails to state an edge: the listed facts (high price, privacy objections, no clear everyday use, social friction, short battery) indicate the product does not beat existing alternatives (smartphones) for the target mass market, nor does it offer a defensible niche beyond a limited early adopter trial that contradicts the "everyday consumer" positioning.

Steelman: An invitation-only launch at $1,500 allows the company to iterate on hardware and social norms with a controlled group before a general release, mitigating risk while establishing a foothold.

Attack: The steelman collapses under the proposal's own stated facts. The price ($1,500) is prohibitive for an "everyday" device without a killer app, and the explicit admission of "no clear everyday use for most people" confirms the value proposition is undefined for the general public. Privacy objections and social acceptance issues are not merely risks; they are active blockers to the core function (recording in public) required for the device to deliver value. A short battery life further degrades utility below the threshold of a primary consumer device. The "invitation-only" phase does not solve the fundamental lack of product-market fit for the broader goal; it merely delays the inevitable rejection by the general public.

Alternatives:
1. Do nothing: Avoids brand damage from a failed consumer launch and high R&D sunk costs.
2. Enterprise-only pivot: Target specific industrial use cases where price and privacy are less sensitive, and utility is high (e.g., remote assistance), skipping the "everyday consumer" claim entirely.
3. Delay general release: Indefinitely pause the public rollout until battery, price, and use-case clarity are resolved.

Blockers: The proposal states there is "no clear everyday use for most people" while positioning it as an "everyday consumer product." This is a direct contradiction that invalidates the core business case. The high price and privacy concerns are cited as facts that remove the route to mass adoption.

Risks: Social stigma, battery degradation, regulatory backlash on privacy.

The plan fails as proposed because the stated facts confirm the product does not solve a problem worth $1,500 for the average user, and the identified barriers are intrinsic to the current design, not solvable by an invitation list.

DECISION: NO-GO
