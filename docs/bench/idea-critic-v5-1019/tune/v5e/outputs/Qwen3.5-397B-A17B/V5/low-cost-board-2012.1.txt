First call: NO-GO. The proposal identifies a need but fails to state an edge over existing cheap laptops and tablets; need alone is not a strategy.

Steelman: A $35 bare-board computer creates a mandatory "tinkering tax" (requiring users to source peripherals) that filters for serious learners and undercuts tablets on price, creating a new category rather than competing on convenience.

**Blockers:**
1. **No Stated Edge:** The proposal admits ordinary laptops/tablets are already cheap. It offers no fact explaining why a $35 board + ~$50 peripherals beats a $100 used laptop for schools. Without a proven cost or pedagogical advantage, the plan fails as proposed.
2. **Capability Gap:** The charity explicitly lacks manufacturing or support experience. Selling hardware through distributors without internal capacity to handle defects or supply chain shocks is a factual route to failure, not a risk to be managed later.

**Risks:**
- Peripheral friction may deter the target audience entirely.
- Small batch sell-outs could kill momentum before scaling.
- Lack of brand trust in a hardware venture.

**Analysis:**
The "better way" check reveals that "doing nothing" (using existing cheap laptops) currently offers a superior user experience (all-in-one) at a comparable total cost. The proposal's core mechanism relies on users wanting to build their own setup, but the facts provided do not confirm this demand exists among the target schools. The lack of manufacturing experience is a hard constraint; entering hardware without it is not a bet, it's a blind leap. The risks raised (peripherals, batch size) are symptoms of the missing edge: if the product were clearly superior, these would be solvable logistics. As stated, the numbers do not work against the alternative.

DECISION: NO-GO
