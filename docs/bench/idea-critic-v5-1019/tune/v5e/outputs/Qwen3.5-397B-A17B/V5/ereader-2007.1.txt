First call: NO-GO. The proposal lacks a stated edge; it enters a hardware market where the company has no experience, facing known failures of prior devices, publisher resistance, and margin compression, without explaining why this specific attempt succeeds where others failed.

Steelman: A dedicated, cellular-enabled device removes friction for book buyers and locks them into the store, creating a ecosystem moat that outweighs hardware inexperience.

Attack:
1. **Options:** "Do nothing" avoids capital risk and publisher conflict. Licensing existing hardware is cheaper and faster than designing from scratch. The proposal must beat "do nothing" on stated facts; it currently relies on hope, not evidence.
2. **Fit:** The company's north star is selling books, not manufacturing electronics. Diverting focus to hardware design contradicts core competencies unless the hardware is merely a loss-leader conduit, which is not explicitly stated as the strategy.
3. **Pre-mortem:** Assume failure. Cause: The device is too expensive due to low-volume hardware production costs, or publishers withhold titles fearing piracy, leaving the store empty. The cellular cost creates a fixed burden without guaranteed usage.
4. **Evidence:** The proposal admits earlier e-readers sold poorly and publishers fear piracy. These are stated facts acting as blockers, not just risks. The plan offers no mechanism to overcome publisher fear or the "hardware inexperience" deficit other than trying.
5. **Blocker Test:**
   - *Blocker:* Stated fact: "Company has never designed consumer hardware." Stated fact: "Earlier dedicated e-readers sold poorly." The proposal offers no evidence (e.g., proprietary screen tech, existing manufacturer partnership) to negate these.
   - *Kill Check:* The risk of "cannibalizing print sales" is acknowledged but not mitigated. If print margins are higher, this strategy destroys value unless digital volume compensates, which is unproven.

The proposal asks for a high-cost, low-reversibility bet (hardware design) based on assumptions that contradict current evidence (past failures, publisher fear). It fails the "must beat do nothing" test because it introduces massive new risks without a demonstrated unique advantage.

DECISION: NO-GO
