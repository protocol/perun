First call: NO-GO. The proposal lacks a stated edge proving this specific massive scale beats existing fleets or future supersonic alternatives; "larger size" is a feature, not a strategic advantage against the stated risk of obsolescence.

Steelman: Building a 2.5x larger wide-body with a raised cockpit creates a dominant, convertible freighter platform that locks in the largest customer and defines the next era of subsonic travel before supersonic jets mature.

However, the blocker test fails immediately. The proposal admits the development and factory outlay is "far larger than any the company has made" while relying on an "unproven" engine and a single customer order. This violates the fundamental rule of capital allocation: you cannot bet the entire company on a single unproven technology and a single buyer when a disruptive substitute (supersonic) is expected to render the asset obsolete. The risk of supersonic replacement is not a mere "risk" to be managed; it is a direct contradiction to the plan's long-term viability. If supersonic succeeds, this massive factory becomes a stranded asset. If the engine fails, the plane cannot fly. There is no cheaper, staged alternative presented, such as stretching the current airframe or waiting for engine maturity.

The "raised cockpit" for freighter conversion is a clever feature, but it does not solve the core economic flaw: the bet requires perfect execution of three unknowns (massive funding, new engine, subsonic dominance) to succeed, while only one needs to fail to bankrupt the firm. The proposal offers no kill criteria, no pilot, and no evidence that the single airline's demand represents the broader market rather than an outlier. Without a second anchor customer or a proven engine, the math does not work. The cost of delay (waiting for engine data or more orders) is far lower than the cost of immediate, total commitment.

Blockers: 
1. Single customer order cannot justify a company-betting factory outlay.
2. Unproven engine creates a single point of failure for the entire program.
3. Expected supersonic substitution threatens to make the asset obsolete before ROI.

Risks: Execution complexity, supply chain strain, market misreading.

DECISION: NO-GO
