First call: NO-GO. The proposal lacks a stated edge; "easier tools" and "cheaper discs" are generic advantages that do not overcome the massive switching costs of loyal customers and incumbent exclusive franchises without a killer application or proven developer momentum.

Steelman: A new entrant can disrupt the duopoly by shifting the economic model from expensive cartridges to cheap CDs, enabling full-motion video and 3D graphics that incumbents cannot match, thereby attracting third-party developers priced out of the current market.

Attack: The claim fails on three verified fronts. First, the "no experience" risk is fatal; platform holding requires curated hit games, not just hardware. Without a first-party studio system or a signed "system seller," the hardware is a blank slate with no consumer pull. Second, the timing is catastrophic; launching simultaneously with an incumbent's 3D console invites a direct feature war against a brand with established trust, likely resulting in a race to the bottom on price without the cash reserves to sustain negative hardware margins. Third, the developer pitch is theoretical; "easier tools" do not compensate for the lack of an installed user base. Developers follow users, not manufacturing savings.

Alternatives: Doing nothing preserves capital. Partnering as a peripheral manufacturer for existing consoles leverages the partner's user base without the platform risk. A cheaper, staged entry (e.g., a dedicated multimedia player) tests the optical drive technology without the console ecosystem burden.

Pre-mortem: The console launches, sells poorly due to a weak game library, developers abandon the platform after the first quarter, and the company writes off hundreds of millions in R&D and marketing. The "cheaper disc" advantage becomes irrelevant when no one buys the player.

Blockers:
1. No first-party content strategy to drive initial hardware sales.
2. Direct competition with an incumbent's 3D launch without a unique value proposition beyond format.
3. Negative margin model requires massive scale that cannot be achieved without the very games the platform lacks.

Risks: Developer apathy, brand dilution, cash burn.

The proposal relies on assumptions that the market will shift to CDs before seeing the content, and that developers will bet on a zero-share platform. These are unproven. The absence of a flagship title or a committed developer lineup is a structural failure, not a manageable risk.

DECISION: NO-GO
