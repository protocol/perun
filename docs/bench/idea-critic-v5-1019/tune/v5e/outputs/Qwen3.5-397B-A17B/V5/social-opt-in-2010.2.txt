First call: NO-GO. The proposal lacks a stated edge; it assumes existing email users want a social network and that automatic enrollment converts them, without evidence that this beats dedicated social platforms or respects user intent.

Steelman: Leveraging the massive existing user base and default visibility creates immediate network effects, overcoming the cold-start problem that kills most social launches, while integrating communication and socializing in one place.

However, the attack reveals critical flaws. The "edge" relies on coercive growth (automatic enrollment) rather than product merit. This directly triggers the stated risks: privacy disregard and regulatory attention are not mere possibilities but probable outcomes of changing default privacy settings without explicit consent. The proposal admits the launch is "hard to reverse" once connections are exposed, making it a one-way door with high reputational liability. Furthermore, the risk of low ongoing use suggests a fundamental mismatch between the user's mental model for email (task-oriented, private) and social networking (leisure, public/semi-public). A cheaper, safer alternative exists: opt-in invitations or a standalone pilot that respects current privacy defaults, testing demand without burning trust or inviting regulatory scrutiny. The current plan fails because its primary growth mechanism (auto-enrollment) is the very cause of its greatest existential risks (privacy/regulation), and the proposal offers no mitigation other than hoping the risks don't materialize. The lack of explicit user demand for this specific integration, combined with the irreversible nature of the privacy shift, constitutes a blocker.

Blockers: The growth strategy (auto-enrollment) creates immediate regulatory and trust risks that the proposal admits are hard to reverse; no evidence shows users want social features in email.
Risks: Low engagement due to context mismatch; reputational damage from perceived privacy violations.

DECISION: NO-GO
