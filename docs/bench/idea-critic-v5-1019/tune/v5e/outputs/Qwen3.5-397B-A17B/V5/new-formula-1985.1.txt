First call: NO-GO. The proposal lacks a stated edge: beating a rival in blind taste tests does not prove that replacing a heritage brand will increase total market share or profit, given the untested risk of severing emotional bonds.

Steelman: Replacing the formula with a scientifically superior-tasting product will recapture drinkers lost to sweeter competitors and maximize sales volume.

Attack: The evidence relies entirely on blind taste tests, which isolate flavor from brand identity. The proposal explicitly admits testing did not inform participants that the original would be removed. This creates a fatal gap between "preferred taste" and "preferred product." Consumers often reject changes to heritage brands even when the new version is objectively "better" by metric, because the value proposition includes nostalgia and consistency, not just sweetness.

Options:
1. **Do nothing:** Retain share erosion but preserve brand equity and bottler stability.
2. **Line extension:** Launch the sweeter formula as a new variant (e.g., "New Cola") alongside the original. This captures the taste-preference segment without alienating loyalists.
3. **Proposed plan:** Total replacement. Highest upside if correct, but catastrophic downside if the emotional attachment hypothesis is true.

Fit: The goal is to stop share loss. However, destroying the core asset (the original formula) to chase a taste metric contradicts the brand's north star of heritage and trust unless the data proves customers *want* the change, not just the taste.

Pre-mortem: The launch triggers a public backlash. Loyal consumers feel betrayed because their favorite product was stolen, not just improved. Competitors leverage the "authenticity" angle. Sales plummet as boycotts organize.

Evidence: The proposal's own facts highlight the missing variable: "Testing did not tell participants the original would be removed." Therefore, the data cannot support the decision to withdraw the original. The risk of "emotional attachment" is not a hypothetical; it is a known variable explicitly excluded from the validation data.

Blockers: The core claim (that replacement increases share) rests on data that intentionally omitted the primary driver of consumer behavior in this category: the reaction to removal. Without a test measuring the impact of withdrawal, the ROI calculation is invalid.

Risks: Total brand alienation, bottler revolt, permanent loss of the "classic" segment.

Kill criteria: None exist because the plan has not been validated against the condition of removal.

DECISION: NO-GO
