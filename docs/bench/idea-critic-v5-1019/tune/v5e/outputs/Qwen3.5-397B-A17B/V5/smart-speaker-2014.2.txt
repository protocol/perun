First call: NO-GO. The proposal lacks a stated edge; "first cylindrical speaker" is a form factor novelty, not a defensible advantage over existing phone assistants or established hi-fi systems, especially given the company's recent hardware failure and the high friction of an always-on microphone in 2014.

Steelman: A dedicated, always-listening hub creates a new "ambient computing" category that bypasses the poor phone experience, leveraging membership pricing to secure early adopters despite privacy concerns.

Attack:
1. **Fit & Edge:** The company's core competency is retail logistics, not consumer hardware or voice AI. The previous phone failure suggests a systemic inability to execute hardware launches. No evidence suggests voice recognition is reliable enough to overcome the "creepy" factor of an always-on mic in the home.
2. **Alternatives:** "Do nothing" preserves capital. Partnering with an existing hardware maker to bundle the assistant is cheaper and reverses the hardware risk. Waiting one year allows voice tech to mature and privacy norms to shift.
3. **Pre-mortem:** The product fails because parents unplug it due to privacy fears, or it returns useless results due to background noise, reinforcing the brand's "hardware incompetence" narrative. The invitation-only model limits volume too much to gather meaningful data before bad reviews kill demand.
4. **Evidence Gap:** The proposal assumes membership pricing ($99) offsets hardware risk, but no data shows members want a dedicated speaker. The "always-listening" feature is a blocker in 2014 without a proven trust mechanism.

Blockers:
- **Proven Incompetence:** The recent poor phone sales indicate a failure in hardware execution; repeating this with a more complex IoT device is financially reckless.
- **No Stated Edge:** A cylinder shape and invitation-only sales do not solve the core problems of voice accuracy or privacy acceptance.
- **Category Risk:** Creating a new category requires dominant technology or brand trust; this company currently has neither in hardware.

Risks: Privacy backlash, voice failure rates, low adoption. These are not just risks; given the 2014 context and company history, they are probable outcomes.

Kill Criteria: If the company cannot demonstrate a 50% improvement in voice accuracy over phone assistants or a clear path to privacy trust, the project must stop.

DECISION: NO-GO
