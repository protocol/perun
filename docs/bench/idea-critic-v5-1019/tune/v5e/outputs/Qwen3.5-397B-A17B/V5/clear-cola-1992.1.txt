First call: NO-GO. The proposal identifies a need (slowing growth) and a positive test signal, but fails to state a defensible edge against the specific risks raised that would guarantee nationwide success. A positive test market is not an edge; it is a leading indicator that often fails to predict repeat purchase behavior for novelty items.

Steelman: A clear cola can capture a distinct "purity" segment and reverse sales stagnation without eroding the core brand's equity if positioned correctly.

Attack: The central failure mode is the conflict between consumer expectations and product reality. Cola flavor is psychologically anchored to dark color; disconnecting them creates cognitive dissonance that drives initial trial but kills repeat purchases. The proposal admits "novelty buyers may not repeat" yet plans a "large marketing campaign" nationwide. This scales customer acquisition costs against a product likely lacking retention, burning capital on one-time buyers. Furthermore, launching as a "major brand extension" maximizes cannibalization risk of the flagship without a proven mechanism to prevent it. Test markets notoriously overstate demand for novelties due to the "newness" effect, which fades post-launch. The plan offers no specific kill criteria, pilot cap, or phased rollout to validate repeat rates before committing to a nationwide spend. It bets the brand's extension equity on an unproven psychological shift.

Alternatives:
1. Do nothing: Preserve margin and brand focus; risk continued slow decline in soft drinks.
2. Limited regional rollout: Validate repeat purchase rates and cannibalization metrics with capped spend before national commitment.
3. Sub-brand launch: Isolate risk to protect the flagship's equity if the concept fails.

The proposal fails because it treats a "positive response" in a controlled test as proof of sustainable demand, ignoring the specific risk that the product's core attribute (clarity) undermines its functional promise (cola taste) over time. Without evidence that repeat rates exceed the threshold required to sustain a national campaign, the plan is a bet on novelty, not a brand extension. The risks listed are not merely possibilities; they are structural flaws in the product-category fit that a large campaign cannot fix.

Blockers: None of the stated facts prove repeat purchase viability; the plan relies on an unverified assumption that "purity" overrides flavor-color expectation long-term.
Risks: High cannibalization, low retention, wasted marketing spend, competitor copying of a failed concept.

DECISION: NO-GO
