First call: NO-GO. The proposal offers no stated edge over extending the dominant architecture; it trades immediate compatibility for uncertain compiler gains.

Steelman: A clean-slate 64-bit design with compiler-scheduled parallelism yields superior performance that emulation overhead cannot negate, justifying the break from compatibility.

Attack: The claim fails the blocker test on three verified facts within the proposal. First, the "huge" installed base combined with "slow" emulation creates an immediate adoption valley of death; users will not accept degraded performance for legacy software. Second, the admission that compilers will be "hard to write" contradicts the core mechanism's success, as the architecture relies entirely on compiler efficiency to schedule parallel instructions. If the tooling lags, the hardware is useless. Third, the risk that rivals extend the dominant architecture offers a cheaper, reversible path to 64-bit computing without breaking compatibility or requiring new compilers. This alternative reaches the same upside (64-bit addressing) with significantly lower risk and cost.

The proposal admits its own fatal flaws: high migration friction, unproven tooling, and a viable competitor path that preserves the ecosystem. The "edge" is theoretical performance, but the cost is total ecosystem abandonment. A sound operator does not bet the company on a harder compiler and slower emulation when a compatible extension is possible. The risks listed are not merely potential; they are structural blockers inherent to the design choice.

Blockers: 
1. Emulation slowness alienates the huge installed base immediately.
2. Core value depends on compilers explicitly admitted as "hard to write."
3. Competitors can achieve 64-bit goals via extension, rendering the break unnecessary.

Risks: Market rejection, tooling delays, competitor dominance.

Kill criteria check: The proposal lists risks that function as blockers because the plan offers no mitigation for the "slow emulation" or "hard compiler" realities. The alternative (extending the current arch) beats this plan on cost and risk while delivering the primary benefit.
