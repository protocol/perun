First call: NO-GO. While the taste data provides a functional edge, the proposal ignores the stated risk that the brand's value derives from heritage and continuity, not just flavor. A blind taste test cannot measure the loss of identity caused by removing the original.

Steelman: Replacing the formula maximizes immediate consumption utility based on superior sensory data, aiming to halt share loss by offering the objectively better-tasting product.

However, the plan fails the blocker test. The proposal explicitly states that testing did not inform participants of the original's removal. This invalidates the data for this specific strategic move. Consumers may prefer the sweeter taste in isolation but reject it as a replacement for a cultural icon. The "emotional attachment" risk is not a hypothetical uncertainty; it is a verified market reality for century-old brands that the current evidence fails to address. By withdrawing the original entirely, the plan creates a one-way door with no fallback, betting the entire franchise on a metric (blind taste) that excludes the primary purchase driver (brand equity).

The cheaper, safer alternative is a line extension: launch the sweeter formula as a new variant while retaining the original. This captures the taste-preference segment without alienating the heritage base. The current plan demands total replacement, which contradicts the evidence that the brand's strength lies in its history. The absence of a fallback product and the deceptive nature of the testing (hiding the discontinuation) constitute a fatal flaw in the decision logic. The data supports a new product, not the destruction of the old one.

Blockers: The testing methodology excluded the core variable (discontinuation), rendering the results inapplicable to the proposed action. Total withdrawal removes the asset (heritage) that differentiates the brand from the rival.
Risks: Consumer backlash, bottler revolt, permanent brand damage.

DECISION: NO-GO
