First call: NO-GO. The proposal relies on a "cheapest" positioning that contradicts the core buyer need (family safety) and ignores cheaper, proven alternatives (scooters, used cars) without a stated edge in reliability or total cost of ownership.

**Steelman:** A dedicated high-volume factory enables a price point low enough to convert scooter owners, creating a new mass market segment despite safety concerns.

**Attack:**
1.  **Fit & Edge:** The "cheapest" label is a liability, not an edge, for families prioritizing safety. The proposal admits safety doubts; without a stated mechanism to prove safety *despite* low cost, the brand positioning actively repels the target demographic. Need alone is not an edge.
2.  **Alternatives:** Scooters and used cars are explicitly cheaper. The proposal offers no evidence that the target user values a *new* unsafe car over a *used* safe one. A cheaper alternative (used market) reaches the same upside (mobility) with lower risk, blocking this plan as proposed.
3.  **Blockers:** The land protests threaten the dedicated factory model, which is central to the cost structure. If the factory delays, the $2,500 price collapses. This is a factual conflict between the stated site risk and the required volume/cost model.
4.  **Pre-mortem:** The car launches as "the unsafe cheap car," sales stall among safety-conscious families, and the brand becomes synonymous with compromise rather than ascent.

**Kill Check:** The proposal names "safety doubts" and "cheaper alternatives" as risks. These are not mere risks; they are blockers because the plan offers no counter-evidence (e.g., crash test data, total-cost comparison) to neutralize them. The "cheapest" marketing amplifies the safety fear.

**Verdict:** The plan fails the blocker test. It attempts to sell a safety-critical product to families while highlighting cost-cutting and lacking a safety proof point. The existence of cheaper, safer used cars invalidates the "cheapest new car" strategy as a sufficient differentiator.

DECISION: NO-GO
