First call: NO-GO. The proposal lacks a stated edge proving this mandatory client beats existing retail distribution given the severe constraints of 2003.

Steelman: A mandatory client solves update/cheat issues and creates a future store platform, outweighing retail friction.

Attack:
1. **Alternatives:** "Do nothing" retains retail revenue while solving updates via optional web patches (common in 2003). A "cheaper alternative" is an optional launcher, avoiding the blocker of alienating users without broadband. The proposal fails to explain why *mandatory* adoption is superior to optional tools given the "home broadband is not universal" fact.
2. **Fit:** In 2003, forcing online accounts contradicts the dominant offline PC gaming market. The "north star" of fighting cheating cannot justify cutting off the majority of players who rely on boxed copies and dial-up.
3. **Evidence & Math:** The proposal admits "home broadband is not universal." In 2003, this meant a significant portion of the customer base could not practically use a mandatory update/anti-cheat client. The math fails: if 40-60% of users cannot connect reliably, mandatory enforcement destroys the core revenue stream (retail boxes) before the store upside materializes.
4. **Pre-mortem:** The client launches with bugs; players unable to download patches or verify cheats return boxed games en masse. Retailers, seeing high return rates and customer anger over "always-on" requirements for single-player games, drop the publisher. Competitors copy the *optional* features without the mandatory barrier, stealing market share.
5. **Blockers:** The fact that "home broadband is not universal" is a hard blocker for a *mandatory* online client in 2003. The proposal offers no workaround for offline users. The risk of retailers dropping the games is immediate and tangible, while the store revenue is hypothetical and distant.

The proposal attempts to solve real problems (updates, cheating) but chooses a mechanism (mandatory online) that contradicts the infrastructure reality of the time. It sacrifices confirmed retail sales for unproven digital convenience.

Blockers: Universal broadband absence in 2003 makes mandatory online access impossible for a large customer segment; retail partner retaliation is immediate.
Risks: Early bugs, competitor copying, user backlash.

DECISION: NO-GO
