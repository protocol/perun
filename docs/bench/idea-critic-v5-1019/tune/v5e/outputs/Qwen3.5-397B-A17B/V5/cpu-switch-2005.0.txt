First call: NO-GO. The proposal lacks a stated edge; it admits translated software runs slower, developers must rebuild, and brand distinctiveness will weaken. Need alone is not an edge.

Steelman: Switching architectures is the only path to competitive thin laptops, and a translation layer mitigates immediate software incompatibility.

Alternatives:
1. Do nothing: Retain distinctiveness and stability but lose the thin laptop market to competitors with better chips.
2. Hybrid approach: Introduce the new architecture only in a new "thin" line while keeping the old line for legacy users, splitting the risk.
3. Full switch (proposed): High execution risk, potential sales freeze, and brand dilution.

Fit: Assumes the north star is "market leadership in form factor" over "ecosystem stability." If the goal is distinctiveness, this fails.

Pre-mortem: The transition drags past two years; the translation layer proves too slow for key pro apps; sales freeze upon announcement without immediate new inventory; the brand becomes "just another PC maker" without gaining performance leadership.

Evidence: The proposal itself provides the blockers: slower performance, developer friction, and brand weakening. The "painful earlier transition" confirms execution risk. No evidence suggests the new chips offer a performance-per-watt advantage large enough to overcome the translation overhead and brand loss.

Blockers:
1. Performance deficit: Translated apps run slower on the new architecture than native apps on the old, removing the primary user benefit.
2. Brand erosion: The plan explicitly weakens the brand's distinctiveness without a compensating unique feature.
3. Sales freeze: Announcing early risks current revenue with no guarantee of timely replacement stock.

Risks: Developer abandonment, timeline slippage, supply chain dependency on the dominant maker.

Kill criteria: If native performance on the new chip does not exceed old native performance by >40% to offset translation overhead, the move fails. If the brand cannot articulate a new distinctiveness beyond "thin," the move fails.

The proposal admits to negative outcomes (slower apps, weaker brand) as certainties, not risks. A plan that guarantees a worse user experience and diluted brand identity while relying on a painful transition has no stated edge to justify the cost.

DECISION: NO-GO
