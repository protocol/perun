First call: NO-GO. The proposal breaks compatibility with a huge installed base and relies on unproven compiler technology and slow emulation to bridge the gap, offering no stated edge over rivals extending the dominant architecture.

Steelman: A clean-slate 64-bit design could theoretically outperform patched legacy systems if compilers perfectly schedule parallel instructions and emulation overhead is negligible.

Attack: The claim fails on three verified premises within the text. First, "emulated programs may run slowly" directly contradicts the requirement to serve the "huge" existing base; if performance degrades, users will not adopt. Second, "compilers... will be hard to write" introduces a critical execution risk where the hardware's core value proposition (compiler-scheduled parallelism) may never be realized. Third, the alternative—"rivals may extend the dominant architecture"—offers 64-bit gains without breaking compatibility or requiring new compilers, beating this plan on cost and risk.

The proposal admits the installed base is huge but offers no mechanism to migrate it except slow emulation. In computing, backward compatibility is usually the primary driver of adoption; breaking it requires a performance advantage so massive it overrides migration costs. The proposal states no such advantage, only theoretical potential dependent on solving two known hard problems (compiler complexity and emulation speed).

Blockers:
1. The plan relies on "slow" emulation for a "huge" software base, which negates the user value proposition immediately.
2. An alternative exists (extending dominant architecture) that achieves the 64-bit goal without the compatibility penalty or compiler risk.

Risks:
1. Compiler development delays.
2. Market rejection due to software incompatibility.

The risks listed in the proposal are not merely possible downsides; they are fundamental flaws in the business logic as presented. The "edge" is absent; the plan trades a guaranteed massive ecosystem for a speculative performance gain hindered by its own migration strategy. Without a stated fact proving emulation can be fast or compilers easy, the math does not work.

DECISION: NO-GO
