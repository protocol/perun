First call: NO-GO. The proposal lacks a stated edge; "hard to use" is a need, not an advantage over existing solutions, and the high hardware requirement excludes the majority of the target market.

Steelman: A cartoon house shell makes computing intuitive for novices by leveraging spatial metaphors, justifying a $100 premium despite hardware constraints.

Attack: The plan fails on three verified facts within the proposal. First, the hardware mismatch is a blocker: requiring a "high-end PC" when "most home PCs" lack the memory means the product cannot reach its intended audience at launch. Second, the timing is fatal: a "major new operating-system release" arriving in months will likely incorporate interface improvements, rendering this standalone shell obsolete before it gains traction. Third, the ecosystem risk is immediate: "few third-party add-ons" suggests low developer interest, while the "patronizing" perception among experienced users (who influence purchases) creates a negative adoption loop.

Alternatives:
1. Do nothing: Wait for the upcoming OS release to address usability natively, avoiding fragmentation and hardware incompatibility.
2. Integrate, don't isolate: License the cartoon interface technology to the OS vendor for the upcoming release rather than selling a conflicting standalone layer.
3. Low-end adaptation: Build a text-based or simplified 2D interface that runs on current hardware, sacrificing visual flair for reach.

Pre-mortem: The product launches, users discover their machines cannot run it, and those who can find it childish. The imminent OS update provides a free, integrated alternative. Sales collapse; the brand suffers.

Blockers:
1. Hardware incompatibility: The target market ("many new home users") predominantly owns machines that cannot run the software.
2. Obsolescence window: The "months" until the next OS release is shorter than the typical adoption cycle for a new shell.
3. No distribution edge: High cost ($100) + high hardware barrier + low add-on support offers no reason to buy over waiting.

Risks: User rejection (patronizing), developer apathy. These are secondary to the hard blockers.

The proposal commits to a high-cost, high-barrier product in a shrinking window of opportunity without a mechanism to overcome the hardware deficit or the looming OS competition.

DECISION: NO-GO
