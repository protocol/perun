First call: NO-GO. The proposal attempts to enter a market defined as "collapsed" with a product category ("video games") that retailers actively refuse to stock, relying on unproven rebranding and financial guarantees to overcome deep structural aversion.

Steelman: By masking the console as an "entertainment system" with non-game toys and assuming retailer risk via stock buybacks, the company can bypass the "video game" stigma and secure shelf space where pure consoles cannot.

However, the attack reveals fatal flaws in the current facts. The "collapsed" market status is not merely a perception issue but a result of massive oversupply and consumer distrust; a lockout chip restricting third-party software directly contradicts the need for a robust library to rebuild consumer confidence, while simultaneously creating a bottleneck that slows content availability. The take-back promise, intended to coax retailers, creates an uncapped liability on a product with zero proven demand in this region; if the rebranding fails to fool consumers or retailers, the company faces catastrophic inventory returns without revenue. Furthermore, cheap home computers offer superior utility for similar prices; a toy-bundled console with restricted software cannot compete on value or longevity against programmable machines. The rebranding assumes retailers are fooled by semantics rather than driven by sell-through velocity; if the "entertainment system" does not move, the refusal to stock "video games" will simply expand to include "entertainment systems," rendering the buyback promise a trigger for massive losses rather than a safety net. The lack of a track record outside Japan compounds this, as there is no brand equity to sustain the high price point required to cover the robot, gun, and buyback costs.

Blockers:
1. The take-back promise creates unlimited downside liability on a product with no verified demand, threatening insolvency if the rebrand fails.
2. Restricting third-party publishers via lockout chips starves the new platform of necessary software volume to revive a dead category.
3. Retailers' refusal is based on sales data, not just naming; semantic masking does not solve the lack of consumer pull.

Risks: Consumer rejection of the "toy" bundle as gimmicky; home computer price wars eroding margins.

DECISION: NO-GO
