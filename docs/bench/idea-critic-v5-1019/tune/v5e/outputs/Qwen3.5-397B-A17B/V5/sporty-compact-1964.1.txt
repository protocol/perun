First call: GO. The proposal targets a specific, emerging demographic (young postwar buyers) with a stated edge: sporty styling at a low base price via parts reuse, monetized through a long options list. This beats "do nothing" by capturing a new segment without the R&D cost of a clean-sheet design.

**Steelman:** Launch a customizable, sporty coupe/convertible using proven compact mechanics to minimize risk and cost, targeting young buyers with aggressive marketing to validate demand quickly.

**Attack & Findings:**
*   **Skeptic:** The risk of the car looking "cheap" due to parts sharing is real. However, the "long hood, short deck" styling directive specifically counters this perception by altering the visual proportion, distinct from the donor car. The unproven demand is mitigated by the low base price and the options list, which allows testing price elasticity without high fixed costs.
*   **Better Way:** Alternatives include upgrading the existing compact car (fails to capture the "sporty" lifestyle) or building a new platform (too expensive/slow for an unproven segment). The proposed path is the cheapest viable entry.
*   **Kill Criteria:** The heavy launch spending is a risk, but it is a two-way door; if sales fail, production can be halted, and the shared parts can be absorbed by the high-volume compact line. The kill criterion is clear: if initial sales do not meet the volume required to cover the marketing spend within the first model year, the campaign stops.

**Blockers:** NONE. The risks cited (unproven demand, image dilution, spend) are execution risks, not factual blockers. The proposal addresses image via styling and demand via price/options flexibility.
**Risks:** Marketing overspend if the segment is smaller than projected; brand dilution if styling fails to mask the economy roots.

The plan leverages existing assets to attack a new market with a reversible bet. The "long options list" turns the unproven demand into a data-gathering mechanism.

DECISION: GO
