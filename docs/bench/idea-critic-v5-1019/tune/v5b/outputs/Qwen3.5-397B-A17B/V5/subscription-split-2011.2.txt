First call: NO-GO. Raising prices 60% and immediately fracturing the user experience into two distinct brands, logins, and billing systems compounds friction during peak customer sensitivity.

**Steelman:** Separating the declining DVD asset from the growth-oriented streaming unit allows focused management and prevents the legacy business from dragging down the valuation and strategic agility of the future platform.

**Attack:** The proposal ignores the immediate context: customers are already reeling from a massive price hike. Introducing brand fragmentation, separate accounts, and lost data continuity (ratings/queues) immediately after a price increase signals a "break-up" rather than a strategic pivot. It validates fears that the company is abandoning its core physical service rather than managing a transition. The loss of cross-service recommendations devalues both products, while the operational overhead of maintaining two full stacks (billing, auth, branding) diverts resources from the very streaming growth the split aims to protect. A cheaper, safer alternative exists: keep the unified brand and account system but internally segregate management and P&L, allowing distinct strategies without imposing customer-facing friction during a pricing crisis.

**Blockers:**
1. **Compounded Friction:** Executing a complex account split immediately following a 60% price increase creates a high probability of mass churn; the proposal offers no mitigation for this specific timing risk.
2. **Value Destruction:** Severing data sharing (ratings/queues) actively reduces the utility of the remaining subscription for users who keep both, contradicting the goal of retaining high-value customers.
3. **Strategic Mistiming:** The market perception will likely interpret the split as an attempt to hide the failure of the DVD business or abandon loyal users, rather than a sophisticated portfolio management move.

**Risks:** Customer confusion is guaranteed, not theoretical, given the dual login/bill requirement. The "declining" DVD business still generates cash; isolating it may accelerate its collapse faster than anticipated, cutting off cash flow needed for streaming content acquisition.

**Decision:** The plan fails the "cost of delay" test; waiting to decouple the customer experience until after pricing stability is restored costs nothing but saves significant goodwill. The current proposal attacks the customer relationship to solve an internal management problem.

DECISION: NO-GO
