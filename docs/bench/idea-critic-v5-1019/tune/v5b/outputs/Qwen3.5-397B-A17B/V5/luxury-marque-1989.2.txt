First call: GO. A separate brand with a clean-sheet flagship priced below incumbents directly attacks the prestige barrier while leveraging underlying cost advantages.

Steelman: Creating a distinct luxury marque with its own distribution isolates the premium offering from the parent's economy reputation, allowing it to compete on merit and value rather than legacy badge equity.

Options:
1. Do nothing: Remain capped at mid-market, ceding all margin-rich luxury volume to Europeans.
2. Badge engineering: Upscale current models. High risk of brand contamination; unlikely to convince luxury buyers.
3. Proposed path: High upfront capex and channel cost, but offers the only viable route to segment entry without dragging down the core brand's value perception.

Fit: Moves the north star from "volume leader" to "full-spectrum manufacturer." Assumes the market values engineering quality over heritage if the price/value ratio is compelling.

Pre-mortem: Failure occurs if the separate dealer network fails to deliver a luxury purchase experience, or if the single-model lineup is perceived as insufficient for a luxury brand. However, these are execution risks, not fundamental flaws. The "economy" stigma is neutralized by the separate brand identity.

Evidence: The proposal admits high development costs are already sunk. The pricing strategy (below European leaders) creates an immediate value wedge. The risk of buyer hesitation is real but is the exact problem the separate brand structure is designed to solve.

Blockers: NONE. The risks cited (prestige, cost, thin lineup) are inherent to any new luxury entrant, not unique failures of this specific plan. The separate brand mechanism specifically addresses the primary objection of brand dilution.

Risks: Dealer network profitability during ramp-up; consumer skepticism despite separation. These become kill criteria: monitor initial sales velocity against burn rate; if brand perception surveys do not decouple from the parent within 18 months, pivot marketing or halt expansion.

The plan survives the attack because it correctly identifies that reputation cannot be stretched, only bypassed. The sunk cost argument actually favors completion to realize any return on the engineering spend.

DECISION: GO
