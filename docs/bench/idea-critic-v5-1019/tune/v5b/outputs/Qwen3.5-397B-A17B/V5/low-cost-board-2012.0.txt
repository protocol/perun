First call: GO. A $35 price point creates a new category for education, distinct from shared laptops or expensive tablets, directly addressing the charity's north star of rekindling student interest through ownership and experimentation.

Steelman: A dedicated, ultra-low-cost device removes the barrier of sharing expensive school IT resources, allowing every student to take a computer home, break it, and learn without fear of damaging institutional property.

Alternatives:
1. Do nothing: Students continue sharing limited lab resources; interest wanes due to lack of personal access.
2. Bulk cheap laptops: Higher cost ($200+), fragile, high theft risk, and less conducive to low-level hardware tinkering.
3. Software simulators: Lacks the tangible engagement of physical hardware and requires existing computers to run.

Fit: Aligns perfectly with the goal of democratizing access. The lack of manufacturing experience is mitigated by using licensed distributors and a simple, single-board design.

Pre-mortem: Failure occurs if the "hidden costs" of peripherals (display, keyboard, power) exceed the board's cost, making the total package unaffordable. However, many households already possess spare monitors and USB peripherals, whereas they do not possess spare laptops.

Evidence: The $35 price is the core claim. If verified, it is an order of magnitude cheaper than the nearest alternative (laptops). The risk of selling out is a sign of demand, not a flaw, manageable by scaling production. The lack of support experience is a valid operational risk but not a strategic blocker; documentation and community forums can scale support better than a small internal team.

Blockers: NONE. The proposal's facts do not contradict the core claim that a $35 entry point unlocks a new market segment.
Risks: Peripheral availability, supply chain scaling, support overhead. These are managed via kill criteria: if attach-rate data shows <40% of buyers cannot source peripherals within 3 months, pivot to bundled kits.

The objection that "laptops are cheap" is factually weak relative to the $35 target; even "cheap" laptops are 5-10x the cost. The proposal survives the attack because the price differential creates a unique value proposition that alternatives cannot match on cost or durability.

DECISION: GO
