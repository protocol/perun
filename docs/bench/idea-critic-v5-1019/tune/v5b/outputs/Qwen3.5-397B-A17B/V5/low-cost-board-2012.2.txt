First call: GO. The $35 price point creates a new category distinct from general-purpose laptops, targeting education specifically.

**Steelman:** A ultra-low-cost, single-board computer enables schools to teach programming fundamentals at a fraction of current hardware costs, creating a scalable educational platform despite requiring peripheral sourcing by buyers.

**Options:**
1.  **Do nothing:** Students continue using expensive, locked-down devices or no devices; programming access remains limited.
2.  **Subsidized Laptops:** Higher cost ($200+), complex logistics, faster obsolescence, less hands-on hardware learning.
3.  **Software-only:** Requires existing hardware, failing the "low-cost computer" goal for under-resourced schools.
The proposal beats "do nothing" by enabling access where none exists; it beats laptops on cost and educational transparency.

**Fit:** Aligns with the charity's north star of rekindling student interest in programming through accessible, tangible tools.

**Pre-mortem:** Failure occurs if schools cannot source cheap displays/keyboards, rendering the board useless. However, legacy PC monitors and keyboards are widely available in schools or homes, mitigating this. Sell-outs indicate demand, not failure. Lack of manufacturing experience is a risk, but licensing distributors transfers operational burden.

**Evidence:** The $35 price is the core fact. Ordinary laptops are "cheap" relative to enterprise gear but not for 1:1 student deployment in budget-constrained schools. The requirement for users to add peripherals reduces the unit cost and complexity for the charity.

**Blockers:** NONE. The risks cited (peripherals, batch size, inexperience, competition) are execution challenges, not fundamental flaws. Peripherals are a known constraint users can solve; small batches allow iterative learning; distributors handle manufacturing logistics; existing tablets do not offer the same programmable, open hardware experience at this price.

**Risks:** Supply chain hiccups, peripheral fragmentation. These become kill criteria: if >20% of buyers cannot boot within 30 days due to peripheral incompatibility, pivot to bundled kits.

**Kill Criteria:** Monitor sell-through rate and boot-success reports from first 1,000 units. If sell-out takes >6 months, demand is insufficient.

**Strongest Attack Survived:** The claim that "ordinary laptops are already cheap" was tested. At $35 vs ~$200 lowest-end laptop, the 80% cost reduction creates a uniquely accessible tier for mass education, surviving the competition argument.

DECISION: GO
