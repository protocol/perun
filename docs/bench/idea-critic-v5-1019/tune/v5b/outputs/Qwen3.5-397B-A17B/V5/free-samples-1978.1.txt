First call: NO-GO. The initial four-city test demonstrated weak demand, and repeating the exercise with a name change and free samples in a single city ignores the core evidence that the market need is unproven.

**Steelman:** Renaming and targeted sampling might uncover a specific use case (internal office organization) missed in the general consumer test, validating a niche before national spend.

**Attack:**
1.  **Evidence Contradiction:** The proposal admits the first test showed "little demand." A name change is a superficial variable; it does not create utility where none was perceived. The fundamental risk is not awareness (solved by samples) but utility.
2.  **Better Way:** Before spending on printing, renaming, and logistics for a city-wide sample drop, the company must verify if "office workers see a need." A low-cost qualitative check (interviews in 10 offices) costs near zero compared to manufacturing and distributing free units. If they don't see a need after a conversation, they won't after a free note.
3.  **Kill Criteria:** The proposal's success metric is "most recipients say they would buy." Stated intent from free recipients is a notoriously poor predictor of actual sales (the "say-do" gap). This metric invites a false positive. A real pilot requires actual payment, even if nominal.
4.  **Cost vs. Reversibility:** While a single city is cheaper than a national rollout, it is still a sunk cost if the underlying value proposition is flawed. The "large marketing budget" risk for national rollout is irrelevant if the local test is structurally biased to produce false hope.

**Blockers:**
*   **Metric Validity:** The proposed success metric (stated intent) cannot reliably predict sales, rendering the test results useless for a national go/no-go decision.
*   **Root Cause Ignored:** The plan treats a potential product-market fit failure as a branding/awareness failure without evidence.

**Risks:** Wasting resources on a second failed test; scaling a product with no genuine demand based on polite feedback.

The plan fails because it measures the wrong thing (intent vs. payment) and addresses the wrong problem (branding vs. utility).

DECISION: NO-GO
