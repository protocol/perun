First call: GO. The market collapse stems from a loss of retailer and consumer trust, not a lack of demand. Repositioning the product as a toy system with physical peripherals directly addresses the "passed fad" stigma, while the stock take-back promise removes the specific barrier preventing retailers from shelving the unit.

Steelman: By masking the console as an entertainment system and assuming all inventory risk, the company bypasses the industry's credibility crisis, securing shelf space to prove value before rebuilding the software ecosystem via controlled licensing.

Alternatives like launching as a traditional console or waiting for market recovery fail because they accept the current retailer refusal as permanent. Doing nothing cedes the recovering market to competitors once confidence returns. The proposed path is the only one that actively dismantles the blockade.

Pre-mortem analysis suggests failure would result from the lockout chip stifling software variety too early or the take-back promise bankrupting the division if sales remain zero. However, the lockout is a two-way door; it can be relaxed once the platform proves stable. The financial risk of returns is capped by the initial shipment size, which must be conservative given the climate. The delay cost is high: waiting allows home computers to cement their dominance as the primary gaming vehicle.

Blockers: NONE. The proposal's own facts support the strategy: the collapse is perception-based, and the risks (retailer refusal, inventory glut) are explicitly mitigated by the rebranding and buyback terms. The lack of a track record is irrelevant if the product is not sold as a game console.

Risks: Developer friction from the lockout chip and potential financial exposure from returns. These are managed by treating the launch as a capped, time-boxed pilot with strict inventory controls. The kill criterion is clear: if retailer uptake remains below 20% of targeted doors within six months despite the buyback offer, the expansion halts.

The strongest attack—that the toy branding might confuse consumers or limit long-term growth—fails because the immediate objective is simply to get the hardware into homes. Once installed, the software library drives retention regardless of the box art. The plan survives the stress test.

DECISION: GO
