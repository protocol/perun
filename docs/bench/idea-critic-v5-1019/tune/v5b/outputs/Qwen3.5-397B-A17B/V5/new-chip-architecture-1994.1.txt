First call: NO-GO. The proposal bets the company on unproven compiler technology and slow emulation to displace a massive incumbent, while rivals can extend the existing architecture with native compatibility.

Steelman: A clean-slate 64-bit design with compiler-scheduled parallelism offers superior theoretical performance and efficiency, bypassing legacy constraints that limit the dominant architecture.

Options:
1. **Proposal:** High cost, extreme risk (compiler difficulty, emulation slowdown), irreversible break from the installed base.
2. **Extend Dominant Arch:** Lower cost, minimal risk, preserves compatibility, immediate 64-bit path.
3. **Do Nothing:** Maintain status quo; let rivals fracture the market or extend first.

Fit: Assumes the market values theoretical peak performance over immediate software compatibility. Given the "huge" installed base, this misaligns with customer reality.

Pre-mortem: The project fails because compiler writers cannot extract enough parallelism to beat extended rival chips, and emulation makes legacy apps unusable. Users reject the new hardware due to lack of native software. This is a one-way door with massive sunk costs; delay allows rivals to capture the 64-bit transition with backward compatibility.

Evidence: The proposal admits emulated programs "may run slowly" and compilers "will be hard to write." These are not risks; they are stated probable failures of the core mechanism. The "huge" installed base is a factual blocker to a compatibility-breaking move.

Blockers:
1. **Core Mechanism Failure:** The plan relies on compilers that are admitted to be "hard to write" and performance that is admitted to be potentially slow. Without proof these are solved, the product has no value proposition.
2. **Market Reality:** A "huge" installed base cannot be migrated via slow emulation. Rivals extending the dominant arch offer a strictly superior path for users (native speed + compatibility).

Risks: Execution difficulty, rival response, high price.

The proposal asks to bet the firm on a harder technical path (new compiler + emulation) that yields a worse user experience (slow legacy apps) against a competitor who can offer native speed and compatibility. The stated facts confirm the downsides while offering no evidence the upsides are achievable.

DECISION: NO-GO
