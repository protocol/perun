First call: NO-GO. The compressed timeline and massive inventory commitment create a catastrophic risk profile that outweighs the seasonal opportunity.

Steelman: A single elite programmer can leverage the film's brand momentum to deliver a functional, profitable cartridge within five weeks, justifying a large print run to maximize Christmas sales.

Attack:
1.  **Feasibility:** Five weeks is insufficient for design, coding, testing, and debugging a commercial title, even for one person. Normal development cycles are months long. Rushing guarantees bugs, poor gameplay, or incomplete features.
2.  **Inventory Risk:** Ordering a "very large" production run before verifying quality or market reception is financially reckless. If the game is flawed (likely due to the rush), the company is left with high-cost, unsellable inventory compounded by the already "very high" license fee.
3.  **Single Point of Failure:** Relying on one programmer creates a critical bottleneck. Illness, technical debt, or scope creep immediately misses the Christmas window, rendering the product useless for its intended season.
4.  **Alternatives:** A smaller initial run allows for quality verification and market testing. Delaying launch to ensure quality preserves brand reputation. Splitting the team reduces single-point failure risk.

Pre-mortem: The game ships late or bug-ridden. Retailers return unsold cartridges. The company absorbs the license fee loss plus manufacturing costs for dead stock. The brand is damaged by a low-quality product associated with a hit film.

Blockers:
-   **Time:** Five weeks is objectively inadequate for full-cycle commercial game development, making the "quality may be poor" risk a near-certainty.
-   **Commitment:** Locking in a large production run before a playable build exists violates basic operational hygiene for high-cost IP.

Risks:
-   Market rejection of a rushed product.
-   Total financial loss on license and manufacturing.

The proposal demands high capital expenditure based on an unverified, highly improbable delivery schedule. The cost of delay is lower than the cost of failure.

DECISION: NO-GO
