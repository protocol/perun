First call: GO. The friction of per-order fees directly suppresses repeat purchase frequency, which is the primary lever for lifetime value in retail. Removing this friction aligns with the core goal of increasing customer retention, even if short-term margins dip.

**Steelman:** An annual prepaid fee creates immediate cash flow and psychologically locks customers into the ecosystem, forcing them to maximize utility by ordering more often, thereby increasing total revenue despite higher shipping costs.

**Attack & Options:**
The risk of heavy buyers exceeding the fee value is real but manageable; it is the cost of acquiring high-LTV users. "Do nothing" guarantees continued friction and lost share to competitors who might innovate here. A "pay-per-use" discount model fails to solve the psychological friction of checkout. The proposed model beats "do nothing" by converting variable hesitation into fixed commitment.

**Fit & Pre-mortem:**
Assuming the retailer has the logistics capacity for two-day delivery, this fits a growth-stage vision. If it fails, it will be because the $79 price point was too low to cover the marginal cost of the most active users, or because the operational strain of increased volume degraded service quality. This is a two-way door regarding price (adjustable next year) but sticky regarding customer expectation.

**Evidence & Economics:**
No external data exists here, but the internal logic holds: prepaid revenue offsets working capital needs. The "unproven economics" are a feature, not a bug; they represent a calculated bet on elasticity. The short-term margin hit is the acquisition cost.

**Blockers vs. Risks:**
*Blockers:* NONE. The proposal contains no internal contradiction or factual error preventing execution.
*Risks:* Margin erosion on super-users; operational scaling issues. These are managed via kill criteria, not by stopping the plan.

**Kill Criteria:**
If the ratio of shipping costs to membership revenue exceeds 1.2x within the first 6 months, or if repeat order frequency does not increase by at least 15% in the pilot cohort, the price must be raised or the program capped.

The strongest attack—that costs will outpace revenue—fails because the model relies on the "long tail" of moderate users subsidizing heavy ones, a standard insurance-like pool dynamic that works if the user base is large enough.

DECISION: GO
