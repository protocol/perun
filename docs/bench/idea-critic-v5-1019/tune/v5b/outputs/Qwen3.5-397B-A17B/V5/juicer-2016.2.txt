First call: NO-GO. The unit economics and behavioral friction of a $699 connected juicer relying on proprietary, subscription-only inputs create an insurmountable barrier to mass adoption in a commoditized market.

**Steelman:** A premium, hassle-free nutrition ecosystem where device subsidies are recouped via high-margin recurring revenue, justified by convenience and freshness verification.

**Attack:**
1.  **Price Dissonance:** At $699, the device costs more than high-end blenders and entire kitchen suites. Consumers will not lock into a single-vendor ecosystem at this entry point without proven demand.
2.  **Recurring Cost Trap:** Proprietary bags invariably cost significantly more per serving than fresh produce. The "convenience" premium must outweigh a likely 300-400% markup on ingredients. In 2016, subscription fatigue for consumables is high; this model demands perfect retention to survive.
3.  **Unnecessary Complexity:** Wi-Fi connectivity and app-based freshness checks introduce failure points (connectivity loss, server downtime) for a mechanical task. If the internet dies, can the user still juice? If not, the product is broken by design.
4.  **Market Reality:** The "smart kitchen" market in early 2016 is littered with connected novelties that failed due to high price and low utility. Juicing is a declining or niche trend compared to whole-food consumption.

**Alternatives:**
*   **Do Nothing:** Avoid massive CAPEX on custom engineering and inventory risk for perishable goods.
*   **Sell Machine Only:** Allow users to input their own produce. This lowers the price point, removes the subscription barrier, and validates the hardware mechanism before betting the company on a logistics nightmare.
*   **Retail Partnership:** Sell bags in stores rather than forcing a subscription, reducing churn friction.

**Pre-mortem:** The company burns cash on custom bag manufacturing and inventory spoilage. Early adopters revolt over the per-juice cost compared to buying fruit. The Wi-Fi feature becomes a liability when servers lag. The high hardware price limits the install base, preventing the subscription volume needed to reach profitability. The company pivots to a generic blender or dissolves.

**Blockers:**
*   The business model relies on a subscription markup that likely exceeds consumer willingness to pay for juice versus whole fruit.
*   Mandatory internet connectivity for basic functionality creates an unacceptable single point of failure.
*   No evidence provided that the market will accept a $699 entry fee for a single-function appliance.

DECISION: NO-GO
