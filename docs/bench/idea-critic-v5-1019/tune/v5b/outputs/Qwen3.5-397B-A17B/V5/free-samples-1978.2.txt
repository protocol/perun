First call: NO-GO. The initial four-city test demonstrated little demand, indicating the core value proposition is unproven; repeating the experiment with a rename and free samples ignores the primary failure mode.

**Steelman:** Renaming and targeted sampling will uncover latent demand in offices that the initial broad test missed, validating a national rollout.

**Attack:**
1.  **Evidence Contradiction:** The proposal admits the first test showed "little demand." A rename is a cosmetic change that does not address the fundamental lack of utility ("office workers may not see a need"). If the product failed to sell when customers could choose to buy it, giving it away will not prove they *want* it, only that they will accept free goods.
2.  **Metric Flaw:** The success criterion is "most recipients say they would buy it." Stated intent from free-sample recipients is notoriously unreliable and inflates demand (social desirability bias). This metric ignores the actual behavior observed in the first test: non-purchase.
3.  **Better Way:** Before spending on a city-wide sample campaign, the company must identify *why* the first test failed. Is the adhesive too weak? Too strong? Is the use case unclear? A cheap, qualitative investigation into the initial failure is required before any further spend.
4.  **Pre-mortem:** The campaign runs, 80% of recipients say "yes, I'd buy this" when handed a free pad, but the national launch fails because conversion rates remain near zero. The company burns cash on samples and marketing for a product the market does not need.

**Blockers:**
*   **Fact:** Initial test showed little demand.
*   **Fact:** Success metric relies on unreliable stated intent rather than actual purchase behavior.
*   **Logic:** A rename does not fix a functional utility gap.

**Risks:**
*   Wasting budget on samples without solving the root cause of low demand.
*   False positive validation leading to a costly failed national rollout.

The proposal attempts to validate demand using a method (free samples + self-reporting) known to produce false positives, while ignoring hard evidence of non-purchase. It fails to address the core risk: the product may simply have no market fit.

DECISION: NO-GO
