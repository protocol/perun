First call: NO-GO. The proposal commits massive capital to a non-existent market while the company fights a survival-level race in its core gaming business.

Steelman: Embedding general-purpose compute capabilities in every consumer chip creates a foundational platform shift, turning graphics hardware into a universal parallel processor before competitors realize the opportunity, securing a decade-long monopoly in high-performance computing.

Alternatives:
1. Do nothing: Preserve margins and engineering focus for the immediate gaming war; wait for clear market demand before investing.
2. Software-only layer: Build tools for existing fixed-function hardware without altering silicon, minimizing unit cost risk.
3. Targeted high-end only: Restrict expensive programmable features to workstation cards where scientific users already pay premiums, avoiding mass-market cost bloat.

Fit: This contradicts the immediate "north star" of winning the gaming market share war. It assumes a future scientific market that currently has zero revenue potential, diverting resources from the core revenue engine.

Pre-mortem: The program burns cash for three years; the gaming division loses the architecture race due to distraction; the scientific tools remain unused by academics who prefer CPUs; the company runs out of capital or is acquired by the rival. This is a one-way door with high irreversible cost.

Evidence: The proposal admits "no large paying market exists" and users are "experimenting with hacked APIs." There are no committed customers. The cost increases on *every* unit sold, directly attacking the price-sensitive gaming margin.

Blockers:
1. Financial suicide: Raising unit costs on 100% of products for a feature with 0% current revenue contribution violates basic capital allocation during a competitive war.
2. Strategic distraction: Engineering bandwidth is finite; splitting focus between a life-or-death gaming rivalry and a speculative greenfield project guarantees failure in at least one, likely both.

Risks: Niche demand remains niche; compiler complexity stalls adoption; rivals undercut on price while this firm subsidizes unused silicon.

Kill criteria: None can save this as proposed because the fundamental economic premise (spend heavily now for no guaranteed return while ignoring core threats) is flawed. The lack of a pilot, cap, or phased approach makes this an all-in bet on a hypothesis with no validation.

DECISION: NO-GO
