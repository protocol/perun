First call: NO-GO. Buying a declining handset maker to solve an app-ecosystem deficit contradicts the core dynamic: developers follow users, not ownership structures.

**Steelman:** Acquiring the manufacturer secures the primary hardware outlet for the OS, preventing an immediate switch to the rival platform and allowing tight integration to improve user experience, which might slowly attract developers.

**Attack:**
1.  **Wrong Lever:** The proposal identifies the problem as an "app-ecosystem problem" but prescribes a hardware solution. Developers build for market share; owning a factory does not create demand. If smartphone sales are already falling, acquiring the division imports a shrinking customer base, accelerating the very developer exodus it hopes to stop.
2.  **Financial Drain:** €5.4 billion is a massive capital outlay for a business unit with falling sales. This cash would be better spent subsidizing devices, bribing developers, or marketing—direct incentives that address the ecosystem gap. Instead, capital is locked into low-margin hardware manufacturing and integration costs.
3.  **Competitor Reaction:** Other handset makers running this OS will view this acquisition as the platform owner becoming a direct competitor. Their rational response is to abandon this OS for the rival platform immediately to avoid strengthening a competitor, collapsing the remaining hardware diversity the OS relies on.
4.  **Integration Distraction:** Merging a large workforce and managing hardware supply chains distracts leadership from the only thing that matters: software quality and developer relations.

**Pre-mortem:** Two years post-acquisition, the handset division continues to lose money, other manufacturers have defected to the rival platform, the app gap widens, and the company is forced to sell the division at a loss, having wasted €5.4 billion and alienated partners.

**Blockers:**
*   **Strategic Mismatch:** The plan attempts to fix a network effect (apps) with vertical integration (hardware), which historical data suggests fails when market share is low.
*   **Partner Alienation:** The move logically forces other hardware partners to defect, shrinking the OS footprint further.
*   **Falling Asset:** Acquiring a business with declining sales contradicts the goal of growing the ecosystem.

**Risks:** Integration failure, continued sales decline, developer apathy.

The proposal fails the basic test: it spends huge capital to acquire a liability that accelerates the primary threat (partner defection) without solving the root cause (lack of apps).

DECISION: NO-GO
