First call: GO. The company already possesses the core competency in silicon design for its mobile devices, creating a strategic asymmetry against competitors reliant on generic suppliers.

Steelman: Leveraging proven mobile silicon expertise to unify the ecosystem across phones, tablets, and laptops will yield superior performance-per-watt, tighter hardware-software integration, and long-term margin expansion, outweighing the transitional friction of migrating the laptop line.

Options analysis:
1.  **Do nothing:** Maintains status quo but cedes long-term differentiation and margin control to the supplier.
2.  **Hybrid approach:** Keeps high-end laptops on supplier chips while moving mainstream to custom silicon. This dilutes the ecosystem benefit and doubles engineering overhead.
3.  **Full transition (Proposed):** Highest short-term risk but captures the full upside of architectural unity and supply chain sovereignty.

Fit: This moves the north star of total ecosystem control. A sound idea must move this stated goal; delaying it preserves a dependency that contradicts the strategic vision.

Pre-mortem: Failure occurs if legacy software compatibility breaks the user experience or if the new chips underperform in sustained laptop workloads compared to the supplier's high-end parts. However, these are execution risks, not strategic flaws. The cost of delay is the loss of a multi-year lead in integrated device performance.

Evidence: The premise rests on the fact that the company already designs successful processors for phones and tablets. This proves the design capability exists. The risk of "unproven" laptop chips is mitigated by the architectural similarity to the already-shipped mobile units.

Blockers: NONE. The risks cited (software compatibility, engineering cost, supplier relationship) are standard hurdles for vertical integration, not facts that prove the plan fails. They are risks to be managed via kill criteria (e.g., specific benchmark thresholds, compatibility test pass rates), not reasons to stop.

Risks: Software translation layers may impact performance; initial yields or thermal characteristics may differ in laptop chassis; supplier retaliation or supply cutoff during transition. These become the kill criteria for the pilot phase, not blockers for the decision.

Strongest attack survived: The claim that "existing software must keep running" could block the move if the architecture were fundamentally incompatible. However, the industry has successfully navigated such transitions before, and the company controls both the OS and the silicon, making the technical path feasible despite the effort.

DECISION: GO
