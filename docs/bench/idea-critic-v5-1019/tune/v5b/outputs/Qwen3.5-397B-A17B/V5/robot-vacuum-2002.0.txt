First call: GO. The proposal targets a distinct, underserved segment (low-cost, functional cleaning) rather than competing directly with high-end mapping rivals. The lack of interest from floor-care companies suggests a channel mismatch, not necessarily a product failure; direct-to-consumer sales bypass this gate.

Steelman: A sufficiently cheap, reliable robot that cleans "well enough" via randomization will achieve mass adoption by prioritizing affordability and convenience over perfect coverage, creating a new market category.

Options:
1. Do nothing: Cede the low-end market to future competitors; high-end remains niche.
2. High-end mapping: Prohibitive cost, direct competition with smarter incumbents, high failure risk given lack of experience.
3. Proposed low-cost random: Low price point reduces buyer risk, randomized movement simplifies engineering, direct sales build consumer trust.

Fit: Aligns with creating a new mass-market category rather than fighting for share in a nascent premium segment.

Pre-mortem: Failure occurs if random cleaning is perceived as "broken" rather than "simple," or if reliability issues destroy trust immediately. However, the low price acts as a buffer for performance expectations. This is a two-way door; failure limits loss to initial inventory, while success defines a category. Cost of delay allows competitors to establish the "robot vacuum" expectation at a higher price point, making entry harder later.

Evidence: The rejection by floor-care companies supports the direct-to-consumer pivot, as distributors often reject disruptive, unproven categories. The "no consumer experience" risk is mitigated by the product's simplicity and low price, reducing the complexity of support and returns.

Blockers: NONE. The risks cited (coverage, trust, experience) are inherent to new categories but do not invalidate the core economic thesis of low-cost access.
Risks: Consumer perception of random movement; reliability in untested home environments. These are managed by the low price point and direct feedback loops.

Kill criteria: If return rates exceed 15% due to perceived "ineffectiveness" within the first 6 months, or if unit economics cannot sustain a profit at the target price point after 10,000 units, pivot to licensing or cease production. Leading metric: Weekly active usage hours per unit (verifying utility despite randomization).

DECISION: GO
