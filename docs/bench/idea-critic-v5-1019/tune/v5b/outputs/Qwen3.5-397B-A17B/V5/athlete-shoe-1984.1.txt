First call: NO-GO. The proposal commits a large share of the marketing budget to an unproven asset with multiple single points of failure (injury, league ban, market rejection) and no stated fallback.

Steelman: A high-risk bet on a specific rookie's transcendence can disrupt the basketball category if the player becomes iconic and the rule-breaking creates massive free publicity.

Options:
1. Do nothing: Preserve capital, accept slow share loss. Safe but stagnant.
2. Sign established star: Higher cost, lower upside, proven sales. Beats "do nothing" on certainty.
3. Proposed Rookie Deal: Highest upside, highest risk. Does not beat "established star" on risk-adjusted return given the "large share of budget" constraint.

Fit: Assumes the goal is category disruption rather than steady growth. If the north star is market leadership, this fits; if it is profitability or stability, it fails.

Pre-mortem: The player suffers a career-ending injury in game 1; the league bans the shoes immediately, forcing a costly redesign; the signature line flops because rookies lack fan bases. The budget is exhausted with zero ROI. This is a one-way door; the money is gone.

Evidence: The proposal admits the player is unproven, injury is possible, fines are likely, and historical data shows rookie signatures rarely sell. No evidence suggests this specific player defies these trends.

Blockers:
1. Budget concentration: Committing a "large share" of the budget to a single unproven variable violates basic portfolio risk management.
2. Regulatory dependency: The plan relies on breaking rules, inviting immediate league intervention that could halt sales entirely.
3. Historical failure mode: The proposal explicitly states signature shoes for rookies "have rarely sold beyond a season," yet offers no reason why this case is different.

Risks: Injury, lack of popularity, rule enforcement. These are treated as certainties in the absence of contrary data.

Kill check: Does the risk concern the core claim (sales)? Yes. Do the facts support it happening? Yes (history says they fail). Is the commitment too large to stop? Yes (5-year deal, large budget share). Three yeses make this a blocker.

The plan lacks a capped pilot, a kill criterion, or a safer alternative. It bets the division on a hope contradicted by the proposal's own data.

DECISION: NO-GO
