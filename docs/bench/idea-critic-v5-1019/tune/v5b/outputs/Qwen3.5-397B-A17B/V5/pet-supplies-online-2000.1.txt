First call: NO-GO. The plan relies on selling goods below cost to buy growth while burning cash on TV and acquisitions, a model that collapses if capital markets tighten.

Steelman: Aggressively capture market share via below-cost pricing and mass media to achieve monopoly scale before competitors, assuming continuous access to venture capital and an eventual path to profitability through dominance.

Options:
1. **Proposal:** High burn, negative margins, massive debt/equity dilution. Risk: Catastrophic if funding stops. Reversibility: Low (warehouses, TV contracts).
2. **Do Nothing:** Maintain current growth, fix unit economics first. Risk: Slower share gain. Reversibility: High.
3. **Targeted Digital:** Shift from national TV to measurable online ads; stop below-cost selling. Risk: Slower brand awareness. Reversibility: High.

Fit: Assumes "growth at all costs" is the north star. However, selling below cost with negative gross margins contradicts basic solvency unless monopoly is guaranteed.

Pre-mortem: Six months post-IPO, the tech bubble bursts (consistent with early 2000 context). Capital markets freeze. The company cannot refinance debt or issue new equity. Burdened by expensive warehouses and long-term TV contracts, it runs out of cash. Because every sale loses money, revenue acceleration hastens bankruptcy. Competitors with better unit economics survive the downturn and acquire the failed assets for pennies.

Evidence: The proposal admits to "negative gross margins" and "high customer-acquisition cost." Selling below cost means scaling increases losses linearly. Heavy shipping costs exacerbate the bleed. Relying on an IPO in early 2000 to fund indefinite losses ignores the imminent volatility of the dot-com era.

Blockers:
1. **Negative Unit Economics:** Selling below cost with high shipping expenses means every new customer deepens the hole. No amount of scale fixes a negative margin without a realistic monopoly timeline, which is not guaranteed.
2. **Capital Market Timing:** Betting the entire operation on a successful IPO and subsequent continuous funding in early 2000 is a single point of failure given the sector's instability.

Risks: Competitor retaliation, execution complexity of integrating acquisitions, warehouse overcapacity.

The plan fails the basic test of solvency. It requires perpetual capital availability to sustain a model that loses money on every transaction.

DECISION: NO-GO
