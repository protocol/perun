First call: NO-GO. The $1,000 price point creates an immediate market mismatch against established game consoles and rapidly evolving personal computers, while the value proposition remains ambiguous to the average consumer.

**Steelman:** A dedicated $1,000 appliance successfully bridges the gap between passive TV viewing and complex PC multimedia by offering a simplified, high-fidelity interactive experience for the living room before PCs become user-friendly enough for this role.

**Attack:**
1.  **Price vs. Utility:** At $1,000, the device costs significantly more than game consoles yet offers less utility than a PC. Without a massive, exclusive library of "must-have" interactive titles, the hardware cannot justify the premium. The proposal admits the library will be small, which is fatal for a media platform.
2.  **Category Confusion:** Positioned between a console and a PC, the device risks appealing to neither. Console buyers seek affordable entertainment; PC buyers seek productivity and expanding multimedia capabilities. A standalone box for "interactive discs" lacks a clear use case if buyers "may not understand what the product is for."
3.  **Technological Obsolescence:** Personal computers are rapidly gaining native multimedia abilities. A dedicated, expensive hardware unit with a small content library cannot compete with a general-purpose computer that is becoming cheaper and more capable by the quarter.
4.  **Blocker:** The core economic model fails. The high hardware cost ($1,000) creates a high barrier to entry, which suppresses install base growth. A small install base discourages software developers from creating titles, keeping the library small. This negative feedback loop prevents the ecosystem from ever reaching critical mass.

**Alternatives:**
*   **Do Nothing:** Wait for PC prices to drop and multimedia standards to mature naturally.
*   **Cheaper Path:** Partner with existing console manufacturers to add a disc drive module at a lower price point ($300–$400).
*   **Software First:** Develop the interactive media format and license it to PC manufacturers rather than building expensive proprietary hardware.

**Conclusion:** The proposal relies on consumers paying a premium for a device with an undefined purpose and limited content, directly competing with rapidly improving general-purpose technology. The identified risks are not merely potential issues; they are structural flaws in the business case that guarantee market rejection.

DECISION: NO-GO
