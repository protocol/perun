First call: GO. A $35 price point creates a new category for education by removing the cost barrier of disposable hardware, whereas cheap laptops remain too expensive for mass deployment in schools.

Steelman: A ultra-low-cost, single-board computer enables schools to teach programming logic and hardware interaction at a price where breakage or loss is negligible, fostering experimentation that protected, expensive devices cannot.

Alternatives:
1. Do nothing: Programming remains theoretical or limited to well-funded districts; student engagement stagnates.
2. Used laptops: Higher failure rates, complex maintenance, and inability to expose hardware layers; cost still 3-4x higher.
3. Tablets: Closed ecosystems prevent low-level programming and hardware interfacing; consumption-focused rather than creation-focused.

Fit: Directly serves the charity's north star of rekindling interest by placing a programmable device in every student's hands. The lack of peripherals is a feature, forcing resourcefulness and reuse of existing school infrastructure.

Pre-mortem: Failure occurs if the supply chain cannot scale past the first batch or if the "bring your own peripheral" requirement proves too high a friction point for non-technical teachers. However, these are execution risks, not fundamental flaws. The one-way door is minimal; unsold units can be liquidated or donated with low loss.

Evidence: The $35 target is below the psychological threshold for institutional procurement approval in many regions. Existing cheap laptops ($150+) still require significant budget justification.

Blockers: NONE. The risks cited (peripherals, batch size, inexperience) are operational hurdles, not contradictions of the core value proposition. The charity's lack of experience is mitigated by using licensed distributors for sales and logistics.

Risks: Supply constraints during initial launch; potential user frustration with setup. These become kill criteria: if the second batch cannot be secured within 6 months, or if return rates due to setup complexity exceed 15%, the model must pivot to bundled kits.

The proposal beats "do nothing" and existing alternatives on the specific goal of ubiquitous, hackable access. The low price creates the volume necessary for a movement, not just a product line.

DECISION: GO
