First call: NO-GO. A 60% price hike followed immediately by a forced split into two brands, two logins, and two bills treats loyal customers as adversaries, inviting mass churn and reputational collapse.

**Steelman:** Separating a declining legacy asset (DVD) from a high-growth one (streaming) allows distinct strategies and prevents the legacy cost structure from dragging down the streaming valuation.

**Attack:** The execution ignores the immediate context: customers are already reeling from a massive price increase. Forcing them to manage separate accounts and losing cross-service recommendations feels like a punishment for loyalty, not a strategic pivot. The "confusion" risk is understated; this is perceived hostility. The loss of shared data blinds both sides to user preferences during a critical transition. A cheaper, safer alternative exists: keep one brand and one login, but offer tiered pricing or clearly separated billing line items within a single account. This achieves financial separation without severing the customer relationship.

**Pre-mortem:** Assume the split launches. Result: A social media firestorm over "greed," a spike in cancellations as users refuse to manage two identities, and a stock plunge driven by the narrative that the company has lost touch with its base. The door is one-way; re-merging brands after a public split is admitting failure.

**Blockers:**
1. **Timing:** Launching a complex, customer-hostile structural change weeks after a 60% price hike is a factual contradiction of "customer retention." The combined shock guarantees a backlash larger than the strategic benefit.
2. **Value Destruction:** Severing recommendations and forcing dual logins actively degrades the product experience for the remaining dual-users, accelerating the very decline the split aims to manage.

**Risks:** Brand fragmentation, operational overhead of two stacks, immediate revenue drop from churn.

**Kill Criteria:** If customer churn exceeds 5% in the first month or NPS drops below -20, halt the split and reintegrate accounts immediately.

The proposal fails because it solves an internal accounting problem by creating an external customer service disaster. The strategy of separation may be sound eventually, but this specific plan, at this specific time, in this specific manner, is self-sabotage.

DECISION: NO-GO
