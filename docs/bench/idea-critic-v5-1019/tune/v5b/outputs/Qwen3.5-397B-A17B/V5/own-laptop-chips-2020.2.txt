First call: GO. The company already possesses the core competency (chip design) and a proven architecture in its mobile line; extending this to laptops creates a unified ecosystem, improves margins, and differentiates the product, outweighing the transition costs.

**Steelman:** Leveraging existing mobile silicon expertise to replace third-party laptop processors will yield superior performance-per-watt and deeper hardware-software integration, securing long-term strategic independence despite short-term migration friction.

**Attack & Alternatives:**
The primary failure mode is software incompatibility breaking the user experience. However, "do nothing" cedes control to a supplier and limits differentiation. A "hybrid" approach (running both) increases complexity without solving the dependency. The "cheaper" alternative is staying with the supplier, but it caps upside and exposes the roadmap to external constraints. The proposal beats "do nothing" because the mobile architecture proves the design approach works; the gap is scale, not feasibility.

**Pre-mortem & Fit:**
If this fails, it is because the emulation layer for legacy software is too slow, alienating pro users. This is a two-way door for the first generation (can revert or support dual-architecture temporarily), but the cost of delay is high: every quarter without custom silicon is a quarter of margin and integration advantage given to competitors.

**Evidence & Blockers:**
The proposal states the company *already* designs processors for phones/tablets using the *same* approach. This validates the core technical claim. The risks (software compatibility, unproven scale, cost, supplier rupture) are execution challenges, not factual blockers. None of the raised risks constitute a "blocker" under the decision method: they are known variables with mitigations (emulation, phased rollout, capital allocation). No evidence suggests the target users reject the change; indeed, ecosystem unity is a stated goal of such manufacturers.

**Kill Criteria Check:**
Does the risk concern the core claim? Yes (software running). Do facts support it *will* happen? No, only that it *could*. Is the commitment irreversible? No, legacy support can bridge the gap. These are risks to manage, not reasons to stop.

**Decision:**
Blockers: NONE.
Risks: Software compatibility, transition cost, supplier fallout (managed via kill criteria: ship with robust emulation, cap engineering spend at X% of R&D, maintain supplier contract for 2 years as fallback).

DECISION: GO
