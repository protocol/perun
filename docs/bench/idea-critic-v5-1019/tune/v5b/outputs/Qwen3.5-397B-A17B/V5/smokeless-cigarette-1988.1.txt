First call: NO-GO. The proposal asks to launch and promote widely based on test markets that have not yet confirmed user acceptance of taste or usability, while facing immediate regulatory hostility toward health claims.

Steelman: A heated tobacco product offers reduced smoke and could retain smokers seeking a cleaner experience, justifying a launch if users accept the ritual and regulators allow the messaging.

Attack: The core claim—that wide promotion is viable now—collapses under its own stated facts. First, the "very large sum" already spent is a sunk cost; it does not prove market fit. Second, the specific risks identified (disliked taste, complicated lighting mechanism) are fundamental adoption barriers. If smokers reject the taste or find the carbon tip difficult to manage compared to ordinary cigarettes, no amount of marketing will sustain the product. Third, promoting it as a "cleaner way to smoke" invites direct regulatory intervention in 1988, likely halting the campaign or forcing costly litigation before scale is reached.

Alternatives:
1. Do nothing: Avoids regulatory scrutiny and brand damage from a failed launch; preserves capital.
2. Limited technical release: Target only current users who complained about smoke, without broad health claims, to validate taste/usability before scaling.
3. Proceed as proposed: High risk of total loss due to regulatory ban or consumer rejection.

Fit: A wide launch contradicts the precaution required when core utility (taste/ease) is unproven and the regulatory environment is hostile to health implications.

Pre-mortem: The product launches, regulators sue over "cleaner" claims within weeks, media highlights the awkward lighting process, smokers revert to standard cigarettes, and the company faces massive write-offs plus reputational harm.

Blockers:
1. Unverified core utility: The proposal admits smokers may dislike the taste and find it complicated; launching widely before resolving this is a factual failure of the business case.
2. Regulatory trap: Promoting health benefits ("cleaner") without explicit, pre-cleared regulatory approval in 1988 is a near-certain legal blocker.

Risks: High development cost is already lost; further investment without fixing taste/usability compounds the error.

The plan fails because it treats a prototype with known user-experience flaws and legal vulnerabilities as a mass-market product ready for wide promotion.

DECISION: NO-GO
