First call: GO. The market leader and strong second player are locked in a costly specification war targeting a saturated demographic of hardcore gamers. A third-place entrant cannot win by out-spending them on silicon; it must change the battlefield.

Steelman: By abandoning the high-definition arms race, the company can drastically reduce unit costs, enabling a lower retail price and positive hardware margins immediately. The motion controller creates a unique value proposition that appeals to non-gamers (families, seniors, casuals), a massive, untapped segment ignored by competitors focusing on graphical fidelity.

Alternatives:
1. Do nothing: Remain a distant third, fighting for scraps of the hardcore market while bleeding cash on R&D for next-gen graphics. High risk of irrelevance.
2. Compete on specs: Attempt to match rival power. Requires massive subsidy per unit, alienates the existing install base with high prices, and likely fails to dislodge entrenched competitors.
3. The proposed path: Low cost, novel input, new audience.

Fit: This moves the north star from "best performance" to "biggest audience." It leverages the company's need for differentiation rather than fighting a war of attrition it cannot afford.

Pre-mortem: Failure occurs if third-party developers refuse to support the unusual architecture or if the "toy" perception prevents adoption beyond a novelty gift. However, the low price point mitigates consumer risk, and first-party software can bridge the content gap initially.

Evidence & Economics: The proposal relies on the fact that silicon for high-definition output is currently expensive. By removing this cost driver, the bill of materials drops significantly. Even with thin margins, the lower price point expands the total addressable market exponentially. The risk of "no high-definition" is only a blocker if the target customer cares about it; evidence suggests casuals prioritize ease of use and price over resolution.

Blockers: NONE. The skepticism from hardcore gamers is irrelevant because they are not the target customer. The "toy" perception is a marketing challenge, not a fatal flaw, and is outweighed by the accessibility benefit.

Risks: Third-party support lag; niche categorization. These are managed by strong first-party titles and the sheer price advantage.

DECISION: GO
