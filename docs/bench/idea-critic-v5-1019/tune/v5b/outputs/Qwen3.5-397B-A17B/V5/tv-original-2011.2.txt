First call: NO-GO. The proposal commits a large share of the budget to an unproven format (straight-to-series, two seasons) with zero pilot de-risking, relying solely on internal data that has never predicted success for original prestige drama.

Steelman: The service's granular viewing data uniquely identifies a latent demand for this specific genre/director combination, making a traditional pilot an unnecessary cost and delay that competitors cannot match.

Options:
1. **Do nothing:** Continue licensing; costs rise, margin shrinks, but capital is preserved.
2. **Pilot first:** Commission one episode to test audience response and production quality before greenlighting the full order. Lower cost, higher learning, delays release.
3. **Proposed plan:** Commit 26 episodes immediately. Highest upside (market differentiation), highest risk (total budget loss if it fails).

Fit: Assumes the north star is "original content ownership" over "profitable licensing." If the goal is survival or steady growth, this bet is misaligned.

Pre-mortem: The show launches, viewership is tepid because the data measured interest in *actors* not *narrative*, or the "all-at-once" model confuses users accustomed to weekly anticipation. The massive sunk cost bankrupts the original content initiative for years. This is a one-way door; you cannot un-spend the budget.

Evidence: The proposal cites "viewing data" but offers no proof that this data correlates with willingness to pay for *original* subscription retention versus licensed content. No external benchmark exists for "online-only prestige series" in 2011.

Blockers:
1. **No pilot:** The proposal explicitly removes the industry-standard mechanism for de-risking high-cost creative projects.
2. **Two-season commitment:** Ordering 26 episodes without a single frame of footage violates basic capital allocation logic for an unproven vertical.
3. **Data gap:** Historical data on licensed content does not prove viability for original production; the causal link is assumed, not demonstrated.

Risks: Audience rejection, cost overrun, brand damage.

Kill check: Does the risk concern the core claim? Yes (audience will watch). Do facts support it happening? The lack of a pilot means we have *no* facts supporting success, only hope. Is the commitment too large? Yes, it is a "large share" of the budget.

This is not a calculated bet; it is a gamble with the company's primary content budget. A single-season order with a pilot (or a shorter first run) is a mandatory safer alternative that reaches the same strategic upside with survivable downside.

DECISION: NO-GO
