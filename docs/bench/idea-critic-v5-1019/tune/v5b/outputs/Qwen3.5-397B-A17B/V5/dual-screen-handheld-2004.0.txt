First call: GO. The proposal avoids a direct hardware arms race against a well-funded rival by leveraging asymmetric innovation (dual screens, touch) to expand the market rather than fighting for the existing core.

**Steelman:** By offering a distinct, lower-priced value proposition focused on novel interaction rather than raw graphical fidelity, the company can cultivate a blue ocean of casual and new users while maintaining legacy revenue streams.

**Attack & Options:**
The primary objection is that unusual hardware creates developer friction and that touch is unproven. However, matching the rival's graphics ("do nothing" or "follow") guarantees a costly battle where the rival holds the advantage in media features and funding. A cheaper, graphics-focused alternative fails to differentiate. The dual-screen approach forces software innovation that the rival cannot easily clone without redesigning their entire architecture.

**Fit & Pre-mortem:**
This fits a strategy of market expansion. If this fails, it will be because first-party software did not demonstrate the touch interface's value immediately, causing developers to ignore the second screen. The cost of delay is high; waiting allows the rival to cement mindshare as the "premium" choice.

**Evidence & Blockers:**
The proposal relies on the assumption that a lower price point offsets the perceived risk of new hardware. There are no factual blockers in the text: the risks (developer confusion, gimmick perception) are execution challenges, not impossibilities. The plan explicitly mitigates financial risk by keeping the older handheld on sale. The "gimmick" risk is neutralized if the initial software library proves utility. No alternative reaches the same upside (market broadening) at a comparable cost.

**Decision:**
Blockers: NONE. The risks are manageable through strong first-party title support and pricing strategy.
Risks: Developer adoption speed, consumer perception of novelty.
Kill Criteria: If first-month attach rate for touch-specific features is below 20%, pivot marketing to core legacy strengths.

The plan successfully sidesteps a losing specification war to define a new category.

DECISION: GO
