First call: NO-GO. The proposed $700 price point is prohibitive for a mass-market entertainment device in 1993, especially when competitors subsidize hardware to drive software sales.

Steelman: Licensing shifts capital risk to partners, allowing rapid market saturation without manufacturing overhead, while dual royalties (hardware + software) maximize revenue per unit.

Options analysis reveals fatal flaws. "Do nothing" preserves capital. The "standard model" (sell hardware at loss, profit on games) dominates because it lowers the consumer entry barrier, essential for network effects. The proposed licensing model forces partners to profit on hardware, locking the price at $700. Competitors selling at a loss will undercut this price by 50% or more, making the proposed console a niche luxury item rather than a standard.

Fit: The goal is to "set the standard." Standards require ubiquity, not high margins per unit. A $700 console cannot achieve the install base necessary to attract third-party game developers, creating a death spiral: no games because of low users, no users because of high price and no games.

Pre-mortem: The venture fails because licensees cannot move inventory at $700. They cut production, the platform lacks critical mass, and the start-up earns negligible royalties. The "one-way door" is signing exclusive licensing deals that prevent a later pivot to a subsidized first-party model.

Evidence: The proposal admits the price is "far above existing consoles" and competitors "sell hardware at a loss." In a price-sensitive consumer market, a 2x-3x price premium without a corresponding 10x value jump is a blocker. The math does not work: royalty revenue on zero volume is zero.

Blockers:
1. Price Disadvantage: $700 vs. subsidized competitor pricing (<$300) makes market penetration impossible for a "standard."
2. Misaligned Incentives: Licensees needing hardware profit prevents price wars required to gain share.
3. Ecosystem Failure: High hardware cost stifles the user base needed to monetize via game royalties.

Risks: Competitor retaliation, licensee abandonment. These are secondary to the pricing blocker.

Kill criteria were not met; the core economic model contradicts the stated goal of setting a standard.

DECISION: NO-GO
