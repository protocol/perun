First call: GO. The strategic necessity of owning the primary developer workflow outweighs the visible financial and cultural friction; waiting cedes the platform to a competitor or leaves it vulnerable to decay.

Steelman: Acquiring the dominant code host is the only viable path to secure the company's future relevance in software development, despite short-term losses and community skepticism, because the network effect creates an unassailable moat that no free alternative can quickly erode.

Options:
1. **Acquire:** High cost ($7.5B), high integration risk, but captures the entire developer ecosystem and neutralizes the free competitor's growth trajectory. Reversibility is low (stock spent), but the asset can be spun off if catastrophic.
2. **Do Nothing:** Zero cost, but risks the target stagnating without leadership or being acquired by a direct rival, permanently locking the company out of the developer channel.
3. **Build Competitor:** Prohibitively expensive and slow; impossible to displace the established network effect of the incumbent.

Fit: Aligns with the pivot from hostility to embrace; owning the platform validates the cultural shift more than mere sponsorship ever could.

Pre-mortem: Failure occurs if the buyer enforces strict proprietary controls immediately, triggering a mass exodus to the free alternative. This is a two-way door regarding operational tactics but a one-way door regarding the acquisition itself. The cost of delay is the potential consolidation of the free competitor or another rival buying the target.

Evidence: The proposal admits the target is dominant despite being loss-making and leaderless, proving the asset's intrinsic value lies in its community, not its current P&L. The "free competitor" is touted but not yet dominant, indicating a narrow window to act.

Blockers: NONE. The risks (defection, price, regulation) are significant but manageable through the proposed independent operation model. No fact presented proves the deal *cannot* work; they only prove it will be difficult.
Risks: Developer churn, cultural clash, antitrust delays. These become kill criteria: if active user growth turns negative within 12 months post-close due to policy changes, the integration strategy must pivot immediately.

The price is high, but the cost of missing the platform shift is existential. The independent operation clause directly mitigates the primary risk of developer defection.

DECISION: GO
