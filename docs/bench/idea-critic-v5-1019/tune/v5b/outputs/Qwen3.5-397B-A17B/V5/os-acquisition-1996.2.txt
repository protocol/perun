First call: NO-GO. The proposal commits hundreds of millions to an asset with negligible revenue, unproven migration viability, and a founder whose history suggests execution risk, while a cheaper licensing alternative exists.

**Steelman:** Acquiring this specific OS provides the only viable path to a modern, object-oriented core that internal teams failed to build, securing long-term differentiation despite short-term financial absurdity.

**Options:**
1.  **Acquire:** High cost, high integration risk, potential cultural toxicity, but offers full control and modern architecture.
2.  **License:** Lower upfront cost, faster deployment, but perpetuates dependency and limits differentiation.
3.  **Do Nothing:** Continued market share erosion and eventual irrelevance as the dated OS becomes unusable.

**Fit:** Assumes the north star is "survival through modernization." If the goal is mere continuity, licensing suffices; if it is revival, acquisition is required.

**Pre-mortem:** The integration fails because the acquired codebase cannot scale to the hardware maker's volume, or the founder's return triggers internal rebellion, stalling the transition. The door is one-way; the cash is gone. Delay costs another quarter of bleeding market share.

**Evidence:** The proposal admits the target sold few units and the price/revenue ratio is terrible. Internal failure twice proves internal capability is zero.

**Blockers:**
*   **Economic Mismatch:** Paying "hundreds of millions" for "few units" violates basic capital allocation unless the strategic value (the founder's vision + the code) is proven to be the *only* savior. The proposal treats this as a given, not a verified fact.
*   **Alternative Viability:** Licensing reaches the "modern OS" state cheaper. The proposal fails to demonstrate why licensing cannot achieve the same upside (survival) without the acquisition risk.

**Risks:** Migration complexity, founder volatility, cultural clash.

**Kill Criteria:** If a technical audit (within 30 days) proves the acquired code cannot run on existing hardware architectures, or if the founder refuses binding operational constraints, the deal terminates.

The economic disconnect and the existence of a cheaper alternative (licensing) that solves the immediate "dated OS" problem constitute a blocker. The proposal assumes acquisition is the only path without disproving the licensing alternative's sufficiency for survival.

DECISION: NO-GO
