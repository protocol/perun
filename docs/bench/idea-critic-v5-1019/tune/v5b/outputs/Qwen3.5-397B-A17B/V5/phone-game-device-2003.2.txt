First call: NO-GO. The proposal attempts to enter a dedicated market with a compromised hybrid device at a premium price without securing the essential content ecosystem.

Steelman: A converged device leveraging existing mobile distribution to disrupt the handheld market by offering phone utility alongside gaming.

Attack: The proposal fails on three verified facts provided in the text. First, the user experience is fundamentally broken: removing the battery to change games is an unacceptable friction point that violates basic usability standards for a gaming device. Second, the pricing strategy is inverted; charging a premium ($300) against a market leader with a lower price point and a massive existing library offers no value proposition to the consumer. Third, the "content moat" is ignored: without committed publishers, the hardware is useless, yet the proposal admits publishers are uncommitted.

Alternatives: "Do nothing" preserves capital. A "software-first" approach (games on existing leader hardware) or a dedicated, cheaper handheld without phone compromises would better test the market. The current plan beats neither the status quo nor simpler alternatives.

Fit: Entering a market led by a specialist requires either superior performance or lower cost. This proposal offers neither, while adding significant ergonomic and functional drawbacks (awkward holding, battery removal).

Pre-mortem: The device launches, reviewers pan the ergonomics and game-swapping mechanism, publishers refuse to port titles due to low install base, and the product sits unsold at $300 while the competitor continues to dominate with a $100 device and thousands of games.

Blockers:
1. **Functional Defect:** Changing games requires battery removal (stated fact), rendering the core gaming loop unusable.
2. **Economic Mismatch:** Price is "far above" the leader's while offering fewer games (stated fact).
3. **Content Void:** Publishers are "uncommitted" (stated fact), meaning no library at launch.

These are not risks; they are stated facts that guarantee failure as proposed. The kill check confirms these concern the core claim (that users will buy it), are supported by the proposal's own facts, and represent an irreversible capital commitment to a flawed design.

DECISION: NO-GO
