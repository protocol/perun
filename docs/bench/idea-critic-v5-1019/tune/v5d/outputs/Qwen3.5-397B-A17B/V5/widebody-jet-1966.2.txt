First call: NO-GO. The proposal lacks a stated edge proving this specific massive bet beats the emerging supersonic alternative or smaller, cheaper efficiency gains; it relies on a single customer's request against existential financial and technological risks.

Steelman: Building a ultra-high-capacity freighter-passenger hybrid now secures long-term dominance if subsonic travel grows and supersonic fails.

Options:
1. **Do nothing:** Avoid bankruptcy if supersonic wins or demand stalls; lose potential scale advantages.
2. **Upgrade current jets:** Cheaper, lower risk, immediate cost-per-seat reduction, but capped upside.
3. **Proposed giant jet:** Highest upside if right, but total company failure if wrong (one customer, unproven engine, supersonic threat).

Fit: Assumes subsonic growth continues and supersonic is a niche; contradicts the industry momentum toward speed.

Pre-mortem: The company goes bankrupt because the single airline cancels, the new engine fails certification, or supersonic jets render subsonic giants obsolete within five years. This is a one-way door; delay costs nothing compared to the loss of the entire firm.

Evidence: All critical data points (engine readiness, supersonic viability, broader market demand) are marked "unproven" or "expected." The financial outlay exceeds all prior history without diversified orders.

Blockers:
1. **Single Customer Dependency:** The entire business case rests on one airline's request; no broader market validation exists.
2. **Unproven Core Technology:** The high-bypass engine is not flight-ready, creating a critical path failure point.
3. **Existential Strategic Risk:** The industry expects supersonic replacement; betting everything on larger subsonic capacity contradicts this trajectory without proof it is wrong.

Risks: Cost overruns, factory delays, supersonic success.

The proposal commits the entire company to a single, unproven path based on one customer's wish while a disruptive technology (supersonic) looms. A sound operator does not bet the firm on a "maybe" without a diversified order book or proven technology. The lack of a second customer or a backup plan for engine failure makes this impossible to approve as proposed.

DECISION: NO-GO
