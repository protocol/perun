First call: NO-GO. The proposal breaks compatibility with a huge installed base and relies on unproven compiler technology and slow emulation to bridge the gap, offering no stated edge over rivals extending the dominant architecture to 64 bits.

Steelman: A clean-slate 64-bit design optimized for compiler-scheduled parallelism could theoretically outperform patched legacy architectures if compiler complexity is solved and emulation overhead is minimized.

Alternatives:
1. Do nothing: Wait for rivals to extend the dominant architecture; low risk, preserves compatibility, but cedes innovation leadership.
2. Extend dominant architecture: Add 64-bit capabilities to the existing instruction set; lower cost, immediate software compatibility, reduced execution risk.
3. Proposed joint venture: High cost, high risk, requires rewriting the software ecosystem via slow emulation.

Fit: Assuming the goal is market leadership, alienating the existing software base without a guaranteed performance leap contradicts North Star metrics of adoption and ecosystem health.

Pre-mortem: The project fails because compilers cannot efficiently schedule parallel instructions, causing real-world performance to lag behind extended legacy chips. Emulation proves too slow for critical applications, causing developers to reject the new platform. Rivals successfully extend the dominant architecture, capturing the 64-bit market with full backward compatibility. This is a one-way door with massive sunk costs; delay allows competitors to define the 64-bit standard.

Evidence: The proposal admits risks are "hard to write" (compilers) and "run slowly" (emulation). No data suggests these hurdles can be cleared faster than rivals can extend the current standard. The "huge" installed base is a fact that favors compatibility.

Blockers:
1. No stated edge: The proposal offers no evidence that the new architecture beats the "rivals extending dominant architecture" alternative on cost, time, or performance for the user.
2. Negative stated as true: Emulation is admitted to be slow and compilers hard to write; these directly remove the route to upside (performance/adoption) without a mitigation plan.

Risks: Total ecosystem rejection, compiler development stall, capital waste.

Kill criteria: If a working compiler cannot schedule instructions to beat extended legacy chips by >20% within 18 months, or if emulation speed is <50% of native legacy speed, pivot or stop.

DECISION: NO-GO
