First call: NO-GO. The proposal lacks a stated edge; cheap fuel, zero regulatory mandate, and no demand signal mean the market has no reason to prefer this over existing cars, while the company assumes massive unproven technical risk.

Steelman: Being first creates an insurmountable brand moat and locks in supply chains before regulators inevitably force the transition.

Attack: This reasoning fails because "inevitable" is not "now." Without a regulatory gun to the customer's head or a fuel price crisis, buyers will not pay a premium for unproven durability. The "first mover" advantage here is actually a "first payer" penalty for solving R&D problems that competitors can later license or copy once the technology is de-risked.

Alternatives:
1. Do nothing: Preserve capital, wait for regulators or fuel prices to create actual demand.
2. Limited fleet pilot: Test durability and warranty costs in controlled environments without a public mass-production commitment.
3. Competitor wait: Let others burn cash proving the nickel-metal-hydride viability.

Fit: This contradicts a sound North Star of profitable growth. It bets the corporate future on a hypothesis (demand exists) that the proposal's own facts refute (cheap fuel, no mandate).

Pre-mortem: The car launches in 1997. Early units suffer battery degradation in real-world cycles. Warranty claims explode because the durability data was theoretical. With fuel cheap, customers reject the higher sticker price. The program becomes a money-losing albatross that drags down the core business, while competitors watch and learn from the failure.

Evidence: The proposal admits battery durability is unproven and there is no near-term demand signal. These are not risks; they are known deficits in the business case. A plan cannot succeed if the core value proposition (fuel savings) is negated by market conditions (cheap fuel) and the product reliability is unknown.

Blockers:
1. No stated edge: The plan offers no advantage that beats current buyer incentives given cheap fuel.
2. Unproven core tech: Committing to mass production before proving battery durability violates basic engineering governance.

Risks: Warranty insolvency, brand damage from reliability failures, capital misallocation.

Kill criteria: None exist in the proposal to stop the bleed if early durability tests fail.

DECISION: NO-GO
