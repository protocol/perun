First call: NO-GO. The proposal states a need (integration) but offers no stated edge proving this specific plan beats the supplier's current performance or ecosystem advantage in 2020.

Steelman: Leveraging proven phone silicon architecture for laptops will unify the ecosystem and improve margins long-term.

Attack: The claim relies on the assumption that phone design approaches translate directly to laptop performance requirements without degradation. The proposal admits existing software must keep running, yet provides no evidence the new chips support the necessary instruction sets or thermal envelopes for legacy laptop workloads. The "unproven" status against the supplier is a factual gap, not just a risk; if the supplier's chips currently offer superior multi-core performance required for pro workflows, the switch degrades the product immediately.

Alternatives:
1. Do nothing: Maintain supplier relationship, ensuring software compatibility and performance benchmarks remain stable.
2. Hybrid approach: Introduce custom chips only in low-power models first, mitigating broad failure risk.
3. Proposed plan: High cost, high risk of software breakage, potential buyer confusion, and immediate loss of supplier leverage.

Fit: While vertical integration aligns with a long-term vision of control, executing a full transition in 2020 without a proven laptop-grade silicon track record contradicts the immediate need for software compatibility and market stability.

Pre-mortem: The transition fails because key professional software runs poorly or not at all on the new architecture, causing a mass exodus of core users to competitors before the ecosystem matures. This is a one-way door with massive delay costs; waiting allows the company to validate silicon performance against real laptop workloads first.

Blockers: The proposal explicitly states the chips are "unproven against the supplier's" and admits "existing software must keep running" without detailing the compatibility layer. Launching an unproven processor that risks breaking the existing software ecosystem is a factual failure condition as proposed.

Risks: Engineering distraction, buyer confusion, supplier retaliation.

Kill criteria: If beta testing shows >5% performance regression on top 20 professional apps, halt rollout.

DECISION: NO-GO
