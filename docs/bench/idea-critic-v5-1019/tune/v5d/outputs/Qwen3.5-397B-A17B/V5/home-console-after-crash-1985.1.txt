First call: NO-GO. The market is declared dead by the very channel required for distribution; entering as a "video game" console guarantees immediate rejection regardless of product quality.

Steelman: By rebranding the hardware as an "Entertainment System" and bundling it with non-game toys (robot, light gun), the company can bypass retailer prejudice against "video games" while using a lockout chip to prevent the software glut that caused the crash.

**Attack:**
1.  **Fit:** The proposal admits retailers view video games as a "passed fad." If the core mechanism (cartridges) is functionally identical to the failed generation, rebranding alone may not overcome the "toy" stigma if the software library inevitably reveals it as a game console.
2.  **Better Way:** "Do nothing" preserves capital while the market clears. Alternatively, licensing the technology to an established US toy manufacturer removes the "no track record" risk and leverages existing trust, rather than building a new distribution channel from zero.
3.  **Evidence & Math:** The "take-back promise" creates unlimited liability. If the rebranding fails and retailers reject the units, the company absorbs 100% of manufacturing and shipping costs for returned stock. With no existing US revenue stream, a moderate failure could bankrupt the division before the second holiday season.
4.  **Pre-mortem:** The venture fails because developers bypass the lockout chip or refuse to publish due to restrictions, leaving shelves empty. Retailers, seeing no software demand, exercise the return clause en masse. The "toy" bundles are perceived as cheap gimmicks masking a game console, confirming retailer biases.

**Blockers:**
*   **Financial Exposure:** The unconditional stock return policy combined with zero market validation creates a ruinous downside scenario that is not capped.
*   **Channel Denial:** The primary premise is that retailers refuse to stock video games; the proposal relies on these same retailers accepting a disguised version without proof of concept.

**Risks:** Developer revolt against the lockout chip; home computers undercutting the price/performance ratio; the "toy" positioning limiting the audience to young children only.

The financial risk of the buy-back clause is a fatal flaw in a collapsed market. The plan attempts to solve a perception problem with a positioning tweak while accepting infinite financial liability.

DECISION: NO-GO
