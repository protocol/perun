First call: NO-GO. The proposal lacks a stated edge; pricing parity with a dominant leader while offering a fraction of the apps and an unproven OS is a guaranteed loss, not a differentiated play.

Steelman: Leveraging the acquired OS creates a unique ecosystem that, if seeded aggressively now, could eventually rival the leader through exclusivity and integration.

Attack: The "small app catalog" is a fatal blocker at parity pricing. Users buy tablets for software, not hardware specs alone. Without a killer app or significant price advantage, "heavy advertising" cannot overcome the network effect of the incumbent's library. The leadership transition adds execution risk to an already fragile go-to-market strategy. A cheaper, staged alternative exists: launch at a loss-leader price to force adoption, or delay until the app gap narrows. The current plan attempts to buy relevance with marketing spend while ignoring the product deficit.

Fit: Assuming the north star is ecosystem survival, this approach burns cash without solving the core chicken-and-egg problem for developers.

Pre-mortem: The device launches, ads run, shoppers compare app stores, see the gap, and return the unit. Inventory piles up; the OS is declared dead on arrival. This is a one-way door for brand reputation.

Evidence: The proposal admits the catalog is a "small fraction" and the OS has "almost no market share." These are stated facts that contradict the viability of a parity launch.

Blockers:
1. Parity pricing with inferior utility (app count) ensures zero organic demand.
2. No stated mechanism to attract developers before users arrive.

Risks: Leadership distraction, marketing inefficiency, brand damage.

Kill Criteria: If developer sign-ups do not triple within 30 days of outreach, halt advertising spend immediately. Leading metric: Apps per active user vs. competitor.

The plan fails because it asks users to pay the same for less, relying on ads to mask a structural deficit.

DECISION: NO-GO
