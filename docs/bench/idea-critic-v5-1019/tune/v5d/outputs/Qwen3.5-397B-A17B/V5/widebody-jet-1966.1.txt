First call: NO-GO. The proposal lacks a stated edge proving this specific massive scale beats existing fleets or future supersonic options, relying on a single customer's request rather than broad market validation.

Steelman: Building a 2.5x larger wide-body jet with a freighter-convertible cockpit captures dominant market share and lowers seat costs before supersonic travel renders subsonic fleets obsolete.

**Blockers:**
1.  **Financial Suicide:** The development and factory outlay exceeds all prior company history. With only one airline ordered, the revenue base cannot support the debt load if other carriers do not immediately follow.
2.  **Technological Fragility:** The design depends on an unproven high-bypass engine. Failure here grounds the entire fleet, creating a single point of failure for the company's existence.
3.  **Strategic Obsolescence:** The premise assumes subsonic jets remain relevant. If supersonic transport succeeds as predicted, this massive capital commitment becomes a stranded asset of outdated technology.

**Risks:**
*   Market demand may not justify the capacity, leaving the airline with empty seats despite lower cost-per-seat metrics.
*   Conversion to freighter is a future hope, not a current revenue stream, failing to de-risk the initial passenger gamble.

**Analysis:**
The proposal bets the entire company on three unverified assumptions: that supersonic travel will fail, that an untested engine will work, and that one customer's need reflects global demand. The "edge" is purely theoretical cost savings, while the downside is total insolvency. A cheaper, staged alternative exists: upgrade current narrow-bodies or develop a twin-engine wide-body with lower capital exposure. Committing to a new factory and four engines based on a single letter of intent violates basic capital allocation discipline. The risk of being wrong (supersonic wins, engine fails, or demand stalls) outweighs the reward of being right.

DECISION: NO-GO
