First call: NO-GO. The market has explicitly collapsed; retailers refuse to stock the category, and the proposal relies on those same retailers accepting a disguised version of the rejected product without evidence that styling alone overrides the "passed fad" stigma.

Steelman: Repositioning the console as an "entertainment system" with toys and a buy-back guarantee bypasses retailer rejection and rebuilds consumer trust through quality control.

Attack:
1.  **Retailer Reality:** If retailers view video games as toxic inventory, a "toy robot" bundle does not change the core perception of the hardware. The buy-back promise mitigates financial risk but does not solve the shelf-space refusal driven by reputational damage.
2.  **Developer Hostility:** The lockout chip restricts supply to ensure quality, but alienates developers who just survived a crash caused by market saturation. This may starve the system of software, rendering the hardware useless regardless of marketing.
3.  **Financial Exposure:** The take-back promise creates unlimited liability. If the "entertainment" angle fails to trick the market, the company faces massive returns with no secondary market to absorb them.
4.  **Competition:** Home computers offer more utility for a similar price. A toy-bundled console cannot compete on value, only on novelty, which is insufficient to revive a dead category.

Alternatives:
*   **Do Nothing:** Avoids total loss in a hostile market.
*   **Licensing Only:** License the technology to an established US toy manufacturer to leverage their distribution, removing the "foreign console" stigma entirely.
*   **Arcade First:** Re-establish brand presence via arcades before attempting home penetration.

Pre-mortem: The launch fails because retailers accept the buy-back terms but place the units in backrooms, never displaying them. The toy bundle is seen as gimmicky, and the lack of third-party games due to the lockout chip leads to consumer boredom within months. The company is flooded with returns, bankrupting the North American division.

Blockers: The fundamental refusal of retailers to stock *any* video-game-related hardware is a factual barrier that styling cannot overcome without a proven distribution partner. The financial risk of the buy-back clause is uncapped and unjustified by current demand.

Risks: Developer revolt, gimmick perception, unlimited return liability.

DECISION: NO-GO
