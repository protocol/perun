First call: GO. The stated edge is the $35 price point, which undercuts ordinary laptops and tablets significantly, creating a unique access layer for students despite the need for peripherals.

Steelman: A ultra-low-cost, single-board computer distributed via established partners can bypass the charity's lack of manufacturing experience while achieving massive scale through price sensitivity.

Options:
1. **Proposal:** $35 unit via distributors. Low cost, high volume potential, reversible via production caps.
2. **Do nothing:** Status quo maintains high hardware barriers; student interest continues to wane.
3. **Refurbished Laptops:** Higher upfront cost, heavier logistics, shorter lifespan per unit dollar.
The proposal beats "do nothing" by lowering the entry barrier by an order of magnitude.

Fit: Aligns with the north star of rekindling interest; cost is the primary constraint for schools, which this addresses directly.

Pre-mortem: Failure occurs if the "bring your own peripherals" requirement creates a hidden cost exceeding the board's savings, or if supply chain inexperience leads to a sell-out that stalls momentum before it begins. This is a two-way door; production can be scaled or halted based on initial uptake. Delay costs another cohort of students losing interest due to lack of access.

Evidence: The $35 figure is the core fact. If valid, it creates a new market segment. The reliance on licensed distributors mitigates the charity's lack of sales infrastructure.

Blockers: NONE. The risks cited (peripherals, batch size, inexperience, competition) are execution challenges or market dynamics, not factual contradictions. Ordinary laptops are "cheap" relative to high-end gear but not relative to $35; the proposal defines a new bottom tier. The lack of experience is offset by the distributor model.

Risks: Supply shortage creating negative PR; peripheral costs negating value proposition for the poorest students. These become kill criteria: if the first batch sells out in <48 hours without scaled production ready, or if total system cost exceeds $100, the strategy must pivot.

The plan survives the attack because the price advantage is a structural edge that existing alternatives do not match, and the distribution model compensates for organizational inexperience.

DECISION: GO
