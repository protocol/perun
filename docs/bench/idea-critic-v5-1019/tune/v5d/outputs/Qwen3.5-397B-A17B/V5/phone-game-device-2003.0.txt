First call: NO-GO. The proposal lacks a stated edge; it enters a dominated market with higher price, inferior ergonomics, and a fragmented library compared to the incumbent.

Steelman: A converged device leverages the maker's distribution to offer casual gamers a "good enough" console experience alongside communication, creating a new category rather than fighting the dedicated leader directly.

Options:
1. Do nothing: Avoids brand damage from a clumsy hybrid; preserves capital for pure mobile innovation.
2. Cheaper alternative: License games to existing handhelds or launch a software store later when hardware matures.
3. Proposed: High-cost hardware with immediate friction (battery removal) and no publisher buy-in.

Fit: Assumes the north star is "market share in gaming," but the strategy contradicts the core competency of seamless mobile communication. A device awkward for calls undermines the primary value proposition of a phone.

Pre-mortem: The device fails because users refuse to power down to swap games, publishers reject the small install base, and the price point confines it to a niche that prefers the superior dedicated competitor. This is a two-way door (can exit the market), but the cost of delay is low since the gaming library gap cannot be closed instantly.

Evidence: The proposal admits critical flaws: battery removal for card swaps (fatal usability), awkward form factor, price premium over the leader, and uncommitted publishers. These are not risks; they are stated facts that invalidate the user experience and economic model. No evidence suggests users value convergence enough to tolerate these specific degradations.

Blockers:
1. Usability failure: Removing the battery to change games is a fundamental interaction flaw that no amount of marketing can fix.
2. Economic disadvantage: Higher price with fewer games than the market leader offers no rational reason for adoption.
3. Supply chain risk: Uncommitted publishers mean a launch with no software, rendering the hardware useless.

Risks: Brand dilution, inventory write-downs.

The plan commits real money to a product with known, fatal usability defects and no competitive advantage. A capped pilot is impossible when the core interaction model is broken by design.

DECISION: NO-GO
