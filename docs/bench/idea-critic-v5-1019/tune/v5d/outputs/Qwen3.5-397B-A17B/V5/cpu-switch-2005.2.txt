First call: GO. The stated edge is clear: the current supplier cannot deliver cool, efficient chips for thin laptops, while the dominant architecture can. Without this move, the product line loses relevance in the emerging portable market.

Steelman: Migrating to the dominant architecture is the only viable path to competitive thin-and-light hardware, and a translation layer mitigates the immediate software compatibility risk enough to bridge the gap.

Alternatives considered:
1.  **Do nothing:** Guarantees product stagnation as thermal limits cap performance; loses the thin laptop segment entirely.
2.  **Wait for current supplier:** Roadmap evidence shows they are already lagging; waiting cedes market share permanently.
3.  **Dual architecture:** Increases engineering complexity and cost without solving the core thermal constraint of the legacy line.

Fit: Aligns with the necessity of delivering superior hardware forms (thin/light) which the current strategy cannot support.

Pre-mortem: Failure occurs if the translation layer is too slow for key professional apps, causing developer abandonment before native ports arrive. This is a two-way door for software (can update apps later) but a one-way door for hardware manufacturing once tooled. Delay costs immediate loss of the "thin" category to competitors.

Evidence: The proposal states the supplier's roadmap lags and lacks efficiency. This is a hard constraint. The risks (sales freeze, rebuild effort, speed loss, brand dilution) are execution challenges, not fundamental blockers. The "painful earlier transition" is a warning to manage communication, not a reason to avoid the inevitable.

Blockers: NONE. No fact presented proves the translation layer is impossible or that the dominant architecture cannot meet thermal goals.
Risks: Sales freeze (mitigated by tight announcement control), developer friction (mitigated by translation layer and clear migration path), performance overhead (acceptable trade-off for hardware viability).

The plan addresses the critical hardware deficit. The risks are manageable execution details, not reasons to halt. The cost of delay is the loss of the product category itself.

DECISION: GO
