First call: NO-GO. Falling sales and a dominant rival ecosystem indicate the core problem is app availability, not hardware ownership; buying a shrinking asset rarely reverses network effects.

Steelman: Vertical integration secures the primary distribution channel, prevents the manufacturer from defecting to the rival, and allows tight hardware-software optimization to improve user experience, potentially slowing the decline while the ecosystem matures.

Alternatives:
1. Do nothing: Risk the manufacturer switching to the rival immediately, accelerating the platform's irrelevance.
2. Subsidize developers: Directly attack the app gap without the heavy capital expenditure and operational drag of manufacturing.
3. License to multiple vendors: Prioritize market share growth over control, accepting the risk of one defecting.

Fit: Assumes the strategy is "survival via control." However, controlling a declining hardware business does not inherently solve the "chicken-and-egg" app problem. Developers follow users; users follow apps. Ownership of a low-share handset maker does not instantly create users.

Pre-mortem: The acquisition closes, but the handset division continues to lose money due to falling sales. Integration distractions slow software updates. Competitors intensify marketing against the now-vertically-integrated threat. The app gap remains because total installed base growth is too slow. The €5.4 billion is sunk, and the company eventually exits the hardware business anyway, having delayed the inevitable while burning cash.

Evidence & Calculation: The proposal admits smartphone sales are falling and the rival has "most" apps. History shows that without a critical mass of users, developer interest cannot be bought via hardware ownership alone. The cost (€5.4B) is high relative to the declining revenue trajectory implied by "falling sales."

Blockers:
1. The core constraint is the app ecosystem (a network effect), but the solution purchases a hardware supply chain. The proposal offers no mechanism by which ownership converts falling sales into developer momentum.
2. The market leader's advantage is described as dominant; catching up requires exponential growth, yet the target asset is shrinking.

Risks: Massive integration overhead, alienating other handset partners, and burning cash on a depreciating asset.

Kill Criteria: If total platform active devices do not grow by 20% within 18 months post-acquisition, divest the hardware division.

DECISION: NO-GO
