First call: GO. The stated edge is clear: a garage-fit, car-like van built on an existing compact platform via factory conversion offers a unique product at a fraction of the cost of a clean-sheet design, leveraging a recent government loan to fund the pivot.

Steelman: Create a new "minivan" category by adapting a proven compact FWD platform into a garage-compatible passenger van, utilizing a converted facility to minimize capital expenditure while the company is cash-constrained but solvent via government backing.

Options:
1. **Proposal:** High upside, creates a new market, low capex via conversion. Beats status quo.
2. **Do Nothing:** Continue selling station wagons/vans; risk stagnation as fuel efficiency and family needs shift.
3. **New Large Van:** Too expensive, cash-poor company cannot afford, misses the "garage" niche.

Fit: Aligns with survival (cash constraint) and innovation (new category). Reuses existing engineering (compact platform) to reduce risk.

Pre-mortem: Failure occurs if buyers reject the "car-like" positioning and view it strictly as a commercial vehicle, or if the compact platform proves too cramped despite claims. This is a two-way door; if it fails, the platform can revert to cars. Cost of delay is high; rivals could identify the same gap once the concept is public.

Evidence: The proposal cites the government loan (solvency) and existing compact platform (technical feasibility). The "garage fit" is the differentiator against full-size vans.

Blockers: NONE. The risks cited (no existing category, cash limits, copycats) are actually arguments *for* the proposal: the lack of a category means first-mover advantage; cash limits necessitate this specific low-capex approach; copycats validate the opportunity but cannot move as fast on a converted line. The platform space limit is a design constraint, not a factual blocker, solvable by the "garage size" requirement.

Risks: Market education (defining the category), execution of the factory conversion. These become kill criteria: if pre-orders do not show family (not commercial) intent by Q2 1984, pivot.

The plan survives the attack because the constraints (cash, platform) are the very reasons this specific path is the only viable one.

DECISION: GO
