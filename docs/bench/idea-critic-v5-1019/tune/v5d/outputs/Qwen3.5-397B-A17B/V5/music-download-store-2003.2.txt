First call: NO-GO. The proposal lacks a stated edge; it attempts to sell a paid product in a market dominated by free alternatives without a unique distribution advantage or cost structure that guarantees adoption.

Steelman: Bundling a seamless, legal, per-song purchase experience directly with the best-selling portable hardware creates a closed-loop ecosystem that free file-sharing cannot match in convenience or reliability.

Options:
1. Do nothing: Continue selling hardware while piracy erodes label relationships and long-term music value.
2. Subscription model: Follow rivals offering unlimited access for a monthly fee, risking higher complexity and lower margin per unit.
3. Proposed store: High friction (licensing every label), low margin (99 cents), and limited reach (own computers only). The proposed path is more fragile than doing nothing if licenses fail.

Fit: Aligns with hardware dominance but assumes users will pay for what they get free, a dangerous assumption without a locked-in mechanism stronger than "light" protection.

Pre-mortem: The store fails because labels withdraw licenses due to low volume, or users reject the 99-cent price point when free options exist. The "own computers only" restriction caps growth, allowing rivals with open standards to capture the software layer. This is a one-way door with high sunk costs in licensing.

Evidence: All figures (99 cents, major labels, own computers) are assumptions in the prompt. No data proves users will pay. The margin calculation is unverified; if label payments exceed 70 cents, the model bleeds money on volume.

Blockers:
1. No stated edge: The proposal admits free sharing is "widespread" and rivals are choosing subscriptions. It offers no fact proving 99-cent singles beat free or subscriptions.
2. Distribution constraint: Limiting the store to "own computers" ignores the majority of potential users on other platforms, ceding the market.
3. Margin risk: Without verified royalty rates, the 99-cent price may be below cost.

Risks: Label withdrawal, user rejection of price, rise of superior subscription models.

The plan fails the basic operator test: it enters a price war against "free" with a constrained product and unproven economics. It must first prove the margin works and the hardware lock-in is sufficient to drive adoption against free alternatives.

DECISION: NO-GO
