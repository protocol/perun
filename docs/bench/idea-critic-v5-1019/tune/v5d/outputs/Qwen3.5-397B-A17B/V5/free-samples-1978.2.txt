First call: NO-GO. The initial four-city test demonstrated little demand, and renaming a product without changing its fundamental value proposition or addressing the lack of perceived need is unlikely to reverse this trend.

**Steelman:** The initial failure was due to poor branding and lack of user familiarity; a targeted free sample campaign in a receptive environment (offices) will validate true demand before a costly national rollout.

**Attack:**
1.  **Evidence Contradiction:** The proposal cites "disappointing sales" and "little demand" as facts. Renaming does not alter the adhesive's utility or the user's need. If the core problem is that office workers "may not see a need," free samples alone rarely create a market where none exists; they often just generate free usage.
2.  **Better Way:** Before spending on a city-wide sample distribution and renaming exercise, the company should conduct low-cost interviews or small-scale concept testing to determine *why* the initial test failed. Was it price, awareness, or genuine lack of utility? The current plan assumes the answer is awareness/branding without proof.
3.  **Kill Criteria:** The success metric is "most recipients say they would buy." Stated intent to purchase is a notoriously poor predictor of actual sales, especially for free items. This metric invites false positives. A real pilot requires actual transactions, not surveys.
4.  **Risk:** The cost of sampling an entire mid-sized city's offices is significant. If the "need" is truly absent, this burns capital confirming a negative with higher precision but no new strategic insight.

**Blockers:**
*   The plan relies on "stated intent" rather than actual purchase behavior to greenlight a national rollout. This is a fatal flaw in validation logic.
*   It ignores the primary evidence: the product already failed to find demand in a real market setting.

**Risks:** High cost of sampling with low conversion; renaming costs without guaranteed uplift; scaling a product with unproven utility.

The proposal fails to address the root cause of the initial failure (lack of need) and uses a weak metric (intent vs. sales) to justify massive future spend.

DECISION: NO-GO
