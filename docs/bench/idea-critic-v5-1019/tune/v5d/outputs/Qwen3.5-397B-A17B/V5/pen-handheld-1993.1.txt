First call: NO-GO. The proposal lacks a stated edge; high price, unproven category, and unreliable core technology (handwriting) offer no advantage over existing notebooks or nascent keyboards, relying solely on publicity rather than product superiority.

Steelman: A pen-based device at $700 creates a new market by replacing paper notebooks, leveraging CEO publicity to overcome early adoption barriers despite technical immaturity.

Alternatives:
1. Do nothing: Avoids capital loss on unproven tech; zero risk.
2. License tech to established players: Reduces hardware risk, tests market demand without brand damage.
3. Delay launch: Wait for recognition accuracy to improve and component costs to drop.

Fit: Assumes "leading a new category" is the north star. However, leading requires a functional product; a broken core mechanism (unreliable input) contradicts the vision of a "personal digital assistant."

Pre-mortem: The device launches, users fail to input data reliably due to recognition errors, returns surge, and the brand becomes synonymous with expensive failure. This is a one-way door for reputation; delay costs only timing, not market existence.

Evidence: The proposal itself admits handwriting recognition is "unreliable in testing." No evidence suggests publicity fixes functional defects. The $700 price is high with no software ecosystem to justify it.

Blockers:
1. Core functionality failure: The primary input method is explicitly stated as unreliable. A computer that cannot reliably accept input fails its basic definition.
2. No stated edge: High cost and large size are negatives; "publicity" is not a product advantage against functional competitors.

Risks: Unproven category, lack of software, pocketability issues. These are secondary to the functional blocker.

The plan commits to launching a product with a known, critical defect in its main value proposition. No kill criteria or pilot phase is proposed to validate recognition before full launch. The "edge" is assumed, not demonstrated.

DECISION: NO-GO
