First call: NO-GO. The proposal states a real need (integration) but offers no stated edge proving this specific plan beats the supplier's current performance, ecosystem, and proven laptop-scale thermal profile using only the "same design approach as in phones." Phone architectures often fail to meet laptop sustained-performance and legacy-software compatibility requirements without massive, uncosted re-engineering.

**Steelman:** Leveraging existing phone silicon expertise will eventually yield superior integration and margins for laptops, justifying the transition cost.

**Attack:**
1.  **Fit & Evidence:** The proposal admits laptop chips are "unproven" against the supplier. Phone success does not equal laptop success; thermal envelopes and instruction sets differ. No evidence is provided that the phone architecture supports existing laptop software without performance degradation or expensive translation layers.
2.  **Alternatives:** "Do nothing" maintains immediate software compatibility and zero transition risk. A "hybrid" approach (co-design with supplier) offers integration benefits without the binary risk of cutting off the supplier entirely. The proposal fails to show why these safer paths cannot achieve 80% of the upside.
3.  **Pre-mortem:** Assume failure: The new chips overheat under sustained laptop loads, or key professional software runs too slowly via emulation, causing a mass exodus of pro users to competitors. The "confusion" risk becomes a brand crisis.
4.  **Blocker:** The core claim relies on the assumption that "same design approach" works for laptops. This is a factual gap, not just a risk. The proposal explicitly lists "existing software must keep running" as a risk but offers no technical path to guarantee this given the architectural shift. Without a verified compatibility layer or performance benchmark, the plan fails its own primary constraint.

**Decision Method:**
*   **Blockers:** The plan lacks evidence that the phone-based design can execute existing laptop software at competitive performance levels. The stated risk of software incompatibility is a potential blocker that the proposal does not resolve, only acknowledges.
*   **Risks:** High engineering cost, supplier retaliation, market confusion, thermal failure.
*   **Verdict:** A plan that admits its core component is "unproven" in the target domain and risks breaking the primary customer value proposition (software compatibility) cannot proceed as proposed. It requires a prototype validation phase before approval.

DECISION: NO-GO
