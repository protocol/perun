First call: NO-GO. The proposal ignores the fundamental mismatch between supply and collapsing demand; producing more units without a stated edge in distribution, pricing, or financing only accelerates cash burn.

**Steelman:** Doubling production aims to achieve economies of scale to lower unit costs, betting that volume will eventually clear inventory once the market turns.

**Attack:**
1.  **Cash Flow Blocker:** With 4,500 unsold units (7,500 produced - 3,000 sold), capital is already trapped. Doubling output requires immediate cash for parts and labor while interest rates are rising, making financing expensive. The company has "limited cash"; this move likely triggers insolvency before any theoretical cost savings materialize.
2.  **Demand Mismatch:** A recession suppresses demand. Increasing supply when dealers cannot finance existing stock creates a logistical bottleneck and forces deeper discounting, eroding margins further.
3.  **No Stated Edge:** The proposal offers no mechanism to move metal (e.g., new export markets, government bailout, radical price cuts). It assumes production efficiency solves a sales crisis.

**Alternatives:**
*   *Do Nothing/Reduce:* Match production to sales rate to preserve cash.
*   *Pivot:* Focus on clearing the 4,500 unit backlog via incentives rather than adding to it.

**Pre-mortem:** By mid-1982, the company exhausts its credit line holding 10,000+ unsold cars, forcing bankruptcy as dealers refuse further deliveries.

**Blockers:**
*   **Fact:** 60% of produced inventory is unsold.
*   **Fact:** Rising interest rates increase the cost of holding this inventory and financing new production.
*   **Fact:** Limited cash reserves cannot sustain doubled output without immediate sales velocity, which is absent.

**Risks:** Market recovery taking longer than anticipated; dealer network collapse under inventory weight.

**Decision Logic:** The plan fails the basic liquidity test. Producing more of what you cannot sell, while borrowing at high rates with limited cash, is a direct path to failure. There is no "go-if" condition here; the math of cash outflow vs. inflow breaks immediately.

DECISION: NO-GO
