First call: NO-GO. The proposal identifies a real need but fails to state an edge; "large infrastructure" is a cost center, not a product advantage, and the team lacks the specific capability to run a developer platform.

Steelman: The company can leverage its massive, spiky internal infrastructure build-out to achieve economies of scale that incumbents cannot match, offering lower prices to developers despite lacking prior platform experience.

Options:
1. Do nothing: Avoids capital risk and distraction; preserves focus on core retail margins.
2. Partner with existing vendors: Reduces execution risk but captures less margin and offers no unique edge.
3. Proceed as proposed: High capital risk, unproven market, direct competition with established giants, and no stated differentiator beyond internal capacity.

Fit: This diverges sharply from the core retail vision and north star. Without a stated strategic pivot to become an infrastructure provider, this is a distraction.

Pre-mortem: The initiative fails because the organization treats developer reliability as an internal IT concern rather than a product SLA, leading to outages that drive away early adopters. The heavy capital spend triggers investor backlash when retail margins dip, forcing a premature shutdown. This is a one-way door with high delay costs if the market matures without them.

Evidence: The proposal explicitly admits no experience in developer platforms, an unproven market, and existing strong incumbents. The "spiky infrastructure" is a description of current inefficiency, not a validated product feature. No evidence suggests external developers prefer this retailer over established hardware vendors.

Blockers:
1. No stated edge: The proposal relies on existing infrastructure as an advantage, but without a product strategy or pricing model that beats incumbents, this is merely a cost, not a competitive moat.
2. Capability gap: The team has zero experience running a developer platform at scale, a critical failure point for a service business.
3. Capital conflict: Investors already question heavy spending; adding a speculative, capital-intensive line without a clear path to profitability contradicts current financial constraints.

Risks: Margin dilution, brand damage from reliability issues, and strategic distraction.

The plan fails the basic test of having a defensible advantage against incumbents given the team's inexperience and the unproven nature of the market.

DECISION: NO-GO
