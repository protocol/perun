First call: NO-GO. The proposal identifies a need but fails to state a competitive edge; "low-cost" is insufficient when users must source peripherals for a device lacking an integrated display or input, especially when "ordinary laptops and tablets are already cheap."

Steelman: A $35 bare-board computer creates a unique, hackable entry point for education that integrated consumer devices cannot match, relying on scavenged peripherals to maintain the price floor.

Attack: The value proposition collapses under the stated constraints.
1. **Total Cost of Ownership:** The $35 unit price is misleading. Adding a display, keyboard, mouse, power supply, and SD card likely doubles or triples the immediate cost for schools, erasing the price advantage over used laptops or tablets which are self-contained.
2. **Operational Inexperience:** The charity admits to having "no manufacturing or support experience." Supplying a component-level device to students requires robust support and supply chain management they do not possess. A small batch selling out indicates supply failure, not demand validation.
3. **Friction vs. Benefit:** Schools prioritize reliability and ease of deployment. A solution requiring users to "add their own" critical components introduces massive friction compared to turnkey tablets.

Alternatives:
- **Do Nothing/Status Quo:** Use existing cheap laptops/tablets. Lower friction, known support paths, immediate usability.
- **Bundled Kit:** The charity partners with a manufacturer to sell a complete kit (board + case + power + SD). This increases unit cost but solves the usability gap.
- **Software Focus:** Develop curriculum for existing hardware rather than creating new hardware.

Pre-mortem: The project launches, schools struggle to source compatible power supplies and displays, support tickets overwhelm the volunteer charity, and the units sit unused because the total setup cost exceeded the budget for a used laptop.

Blocker: The stated fact that "buyers must add their own display, keyboard and power supply" combined with "ordinary laptops and tablets are already cheap" creates a total-cost and usability deficit that the proposal does not solve. The lack of manufacturing experience makes mitigating this risk impossible as proposed.

Risks: Supply chain fragility, high total cost for end-users, lack of support infrastructure.

DECISION: NO-GO
