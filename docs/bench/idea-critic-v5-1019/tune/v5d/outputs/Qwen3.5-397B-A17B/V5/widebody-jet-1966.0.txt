First call: NO-GO. The proposal relies on a single customer for an unprecedented capital outlay while facing an existential technological shift (supersonics) that could render the product obsolete before ROI.

Steelman: Building a massive, convertible wide-body now secures the dominant player's future against capacity constraints and creates a dual-use asset before competitors react.

Options:
1. **Do nothing:** Maintain current production; risk losing the key customer to a competitor who dares to build big.
2. **Supersonic pivot:** Bet entirely on the expected shift to supersonic travel; ignore subsonic capacity growth.
3. **Proposed plan:** Massive bet on subsonic scale with freighter flexibility.
The proposed plan must beat "do nothing" on the stated facts. It does not: one order cannot mathematically support a factory and engine program of this magnitude without assuming immediate, widespread market adoption which contradicts the "only one airline has ordered" fact.

Fit: Assumes the north star is "maximizing seat capacity" rather than "technological leadership" (supersonics). If the industry shifts to supersonic as expected, this factory becomes a stranded asset.

Pre-mortem: The program bankrupts the company. The single customer cancels due to economic downturn, the supersonic transition happens faster than anticipated making large subsonic jets undesirable, or the new engine fails certification. The "freighter conversion" feature adds cost but saves the program only if the passenger market collapses entirely.

Evidence: The proposal admits the outlay is "far larger than any... made," the engine is "unproven," and demand is limited to "only one airline." These are not risks; they are stated facts that contradict the viability of the plan as proposed. The math of one buyer funding a revolution does not work without a committed pipeline not present in the text.

Blockers:
1. **Demand mismatch:** One airline order is insufficient to justify a new factory and engine development; the unit economics fail based on stated volume.
2. **Technological obsolescence:** The explicit expectation of supersonic replacement threatens the core value proposition of a larger subsonic jet.
3. **Capital exposure:** The outlay exceeds all prior experience with no diversified customer base to absorb the shock.

Risks: Engine failure, freighter conversion never utilized, supersonic timeline acceleration.

DECISION: NO-GO
