First call: NO-GO. The proposal lacks a stated edge; high cost and no apps contradict the target demographic's needs.

Steelman: Leveraging acquired messaging hardware to lock in teens via a carrier-exclusive, data-centric device on a fresh OS, betting that social integration outweighs the lack of third-party apps.

Attack: The plan fails on basic unit economics and product-market fit. Teenagers are price-sensitive; a data plan costing as much as a full smartphone plan removes the affordability advantage. Rival devices offer vast app catalogs; launching with zero apps on a new, unproven OS offers no compensatory value. The "sunk cost" of two years development is irrelevant to future viability and creates a dangerous commitment bias. The strategy ignores the ecosystem reality: users buy platforms, not just hardware. A single-carrier restriction further limits reach in a segment that values flexibility and peer network effects across devices.

Alternatives:
1. Do nothing: Preserve capital, avoid launching a likely flop.
2. Port existing platform: Use the company's proven mobile OS to gain immediate app compatibility, reducing risk.
3. Open hardware: Sell the acquired messaging phone unlocked or across multiple carriers without a mandatory expensive data plan.

Pre-mortem: The phone launches, teens reject the high monthly cost and barren app environment, sales flatline, and the brand suffers reputational damage among youth. The sunk costs become total losses.

Blockers:
1. Price mismatch: Data plan cost equals full smartphone plans, pricing out the target teen demographic.
2. Value gap: No app store at launch vs. rivals' large catalogs on established OSs.
3. Strategic drift: Using a new OS instead of the company's own platform increases execution risk without clear upside.

Risks: Carrier dependency, slow OS adoption, brand dilution. These are secondary to the fatal blockers.

The proposal commits to a high-cost, low-value product for a budget-conscious segment while ignoring existing assets (the company's own platform). The sunk cost argument is a fallacy that should not drive future investment.

DECISION: NO-GO
