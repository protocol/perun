First call: NO-GO. The proposal lacks a stated edge; high price and licensee margin requirements contradict the dominant "razor-and-blades" model where incumbents sell at a loss to capture software revenue.

Steelman: Licensing shifts capital risk to partners, enabling rapid market saturation if premium graphics justify the $700 tag to early adopters.

Attack:
1. **Economics:** Incumbents subsidize hardware to maximize game attach rates. This plan forces licensees to profit on hardware, inflating the price to $700. At this price, the addressable market shrinks drastically compared to subsidized competitors.
2. **Incentive Misalignment:** Licensees bear manufacturing costs and risk. If the console fails to sell due to price, they lose money. Incumbents control the ecosystem; here, fragmented licensees may lack the motivation to market aggressively if margins are tight or volume is low.
3. **Competition:** Strong incumbents can engage in price wars this start-up cannot match without deep pockets to subsidize the hardware itself. A $700 console is an easy target for undercutting.
4. **Adoption Barrier:** Early adopters pay premiums for exclusivity, not just graphics. Without a killer app library secured upfront, high-end graphics alone do not justify a 3x-4x price premium over existing systems.

Blockers:
- **Pricing Model:** The requirement for licensee hardware profit creates a structural price disadvantage against competitors selling at a loss. This contradicts the proven path to console market share.
- **Channel Conflict:** Multiple licensees building similar hardware may compete on price, eroding the very margins they need, while the start-up collects royalties regardless of their survival.

Risks:
- Market rejection due to price.
- Licensee bankruptcy reducing market presence.
- Lack of exclusive software driving hardware sales.

The plan fails as proposed because it ignores the fundamental economic engine of the console business: hardware subsidies to drive software monopoly rents. Without the start-up absorbing hardware losses or securing exclusive, system-selling software first, the $700 price point is unsustainable against established rivals.

DECISION: NO-GO
