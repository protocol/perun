**Immediate Safety Gate (Non-Negotiable)**
Because the current failure mode involves physical injury and property destruction, you must implement a **human-in-the-loop gate** immediately. No measurement generated by the algorithm should trigger a glass cut or installation attempt without explicit confirmation by a trained technician who has independently verified the dimensions using a manual tape measure. Full automation of this high-stakes action is the failure condition; the "seamless" user experience must be secondary to preventing harm.

**Harm Inventory & Simulation Plan**
Before relying on simulations, record the specific harms: **Unsafe automation** (shattering glass/injury) and **Over-reliance** (technicians trusting flawed AR data). Your simulation strategy must target these specific failures, not just general accuracy.

1.  **Define Probe Sets (The "What"):** Do not test random inputs. Construct specific adversarial scenarios based on known failure modes:
    *   **Lighting Extremes:** Simulate low-light, direct glare, and mixed artificial/natural light conditions that confuse AR depth perception.
    *   **Geometric Edge Cases:** Input non-rectangular shapes, obstructed views (curtains/plants), and reflective surfaces that mimic real-world homeowner environments.
    *   **Positive/Negative Controls:** Include a set of "known-good" standard windows (negative control for failure) and "known-bad" distorted inputs (positive control for failure) to verify the engine rejects unsafe measurements.

2.  **Establish Metrics & Stop Conditions (The "When"):**
    *   **Metric:** Measure the deviation rate between simulated AR output and ground-truth dimensions.
    *   **Threshold:** Set a zero-tolerance threshold for deviations exceeding the safety margin of the glass material.
    *   **Stop Condition:** If *any* simulation in the high-risk category (e.g., low light + complex shape) produces a measurement that would cause shattering, the launch is blocked. Do not invent an "acceptable" failure rate for injury risks.

3.  **Address Confidence & Disclosure:**
    *   **Uncertainty Tiers:** Do not display raw confidence percentages (e.g., "87% accurate") to technicians, as this invites over-trust. Instead, define coarse tiers: **Confirmed** (multiple data points align), **Corroborated** (single source, stable), or **Unverified** (low light/high glare). If the system outputs "Unverified," the software must hard-lock the installation workflow until manual entry occurs.
    *   **Transparency Artifact:** Create a simple "Model Card" for your team documenting these limitations, the specific lighting conditions where the model fails, and the required manual override procedures. This ensures every team member understands the system's boundaries.

**Execution Constraints**
Given your fixed budget and inability to upgrade sensors, your software rules must be conservative. Program the system to default to "Unverified" whenever lighting or angle data falls outside the narrow band of proven reliability. The cost of a slower installation process (manual verification) is the required trade-off for preventing injury.

**Residual Risk Statement**
Even with rigorous simulation, residual risk remains because simulations cannot perfectly replicate every homeowner's environment. Therefore, the human gate is not a temporary fix but a permanent requirement until hardware capabilities improve. Do not claim the system is "safe" or "validated" based on simulation alone; report the results as "Unverified for autonomous operation."
