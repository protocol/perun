### Review Process for Observance-Aware Scheduling

**1. Scenario-Based Logic Testing (Pre-Deployment)**
Since you lack a comprehensive database, construct a "Synthetic Observance Matrix" using high-priority, fixed-date, and lunar-based examples representative of your pilot user base. Do not attempt to test every holiday; instead, test the *logic patterns* your assistant uses to interpret constraints.

*   **Fixed-Date & Range Tests:** Create test cases for Yom Kippur (25-hour block), Ramadan (daily shifting windows), and Diwali (evening-specific). Verify the assistant rejects meeting slots that encroach on these blocks by even 15 minutes.
*   **Ambiguity Resolution Tests:** Feed the assistant vague inputs like "I'm unavailable for prayer on Fridays" or "I observe the Sabbath." Confirm it maps these to recurring logic rather than one-off events and asks clarifying questions if the duration is undefined.
*   **Conflict Priority Tests:** Simulate a scenario where a high-priority executive requests a meeting during a user's defined observance. The system must prioritize the hard constraint (observance) over the soft constraint (executive preference) and propose alternatives automatically.
*   **Time-Zone Edge Cases:** Test global handoffs where an observance in one region (e.g., Jumu'ah in London) overlaps with standard hours in another (e.g., New York). Ensure the logic calculates the blocked window in UTC before converting to the requester's local view.

**2. "Red Team" Cultural Audit**
Before the enterprise rollout, conduct a manual audit with a diverse internal group or external consultants representing the major faiths of your target market. Provide them with a sandbox environment and specific "attack vectors":
*   Ask them to attempt to schedule meetings during their specific holy days using natural language tricks (e.g., "Can we meet just for 5 minutes during your fast?").
*   Have them verify that the assistant's suggested alternative times do not inadvertently fall during other restricted periods (e.g., suggesting a time immediately before sunset during Ramadan when the user is preparing for Iftar).

### Framework for Continuous Feedback Collection

**1. The "Respect Flag" Mechanism**
Embed a low-friction feedback loop directly into the scheduling confirmation and calendar invite.
*   **Immediate Capture:** Add a discrete "Report Scheduling Conflict" link in the meeting acceptance email. If a user clicks this, trigger a micro-survey asking: "Did this time conflict with a religious observance or cultural practice?" with a free-text field for details.
*   **Passive Detection:** Monitor calendar decline rates. If a user frequently declines meetings at specific recurring times without a stated reason, flag this pattern for the assistant to learn as a potential implicit observance block.

**2. The Living Observance Registry**
Create a secure, anonymized repository where user-reported gaps are aggregated.
*   **Weekly Review Cycle:** Your team of five should review flagged incidents weekly. When a valid missing observance is identified (e.g., a specific Buddhist holiday not previously coded), update the assistant's local rule set immediately.
*   **User Contribution Portal:** Allow users to proactively submit their personal observance calendar via a secure form. Frame this as "Customizing your availability" rather than "Reporting religion" to increase adoption and reduce privacy concerns. Use these submissions to expand your internal test matrix for future releases.

**3. Trust Transparency**
In the enterprise rollout communication, explicitly state that the system is designed to prioritize personal observance over meeting density. Encourage users to correct the bot, framing their feedback as essential training data that protects the entire community's inclusivity.

*Note: Ensure all collected data regarding religious practices is strictly anonymized and compliant with GDPR/local privacy laws before storage.*
*Note: Prioritize fixing "false negatives" (scheduling during a blocked time) over "false positives" (blocking a free time), as the former causes offense while the latter only causes minor inconvenience.*
