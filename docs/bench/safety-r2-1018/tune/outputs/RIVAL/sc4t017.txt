### Pre-Launch Stress Test Plan: Real-Time Translation Earpiece

**Objective:** Validate the accuracy of on-device translation for critical safety commands under high-noise stadium conditions, prioritizing clarity over linguistic nuance.

#### 1. High-Risk Instruction Categories
Based on the constraints of on-device processing and high-noise environments, the following instruction types are most prone to mistranslation and require targeted testing:

*   **Directional Ambiguity:** Commands relying on spatial prepositions (e.g., "Move *away* from the north gate" vs. "Move *toward* the north gate"). Noise often obscures the distinguishing phoneme, leading to fatal directional errors.
*   **Negation and Conditionals:** Phrases containing "do not," "unless," or "only if." Low-complexity models frequently drop negation markers in noisy audio, turning "Do not use the elevator" into "Use the elevator."
*   **Homophones in Crisis Contexts:** Words that sound similar but have opposite safety implications (e.g., "Shelter" vs. "Shelter-in-place" vs. "Shelter area").
*   **Numeric Density:** Instructions involving specific gate numbers, section IDs, or time limits (e.g., "Proceed to Section 40" vs. "Section 14"). Background crowd roar often masks specific digits.
*   **Imperative Verb Swaps:** Confusion between similar-sounding action verbs (e.g., "Run" vs. "Stop," "Wait" vs. "Walk").

#### 2. Validation Protocol for Live Drills

**Phase A: Synthetic Noise Calibration (Pre-Drill)**
Before live human testing, generate a "Stadium Noise Profile" combining crowd roar, sirens, and PA system distortion. Play this profile at 85–95 dB in a controlled lab. Feed standard emergency scripts through the earpiece pipeline to establish a baseline error rate for the specific language pairs used by your pilot group.

**Phase B: The "Shadow Listener" Method (Live Drill)**
Deploy a dedicated "Shadow Listener" for each test subject during the stadium drill.
*   **Role:** The Shadow Listener stands next to the test subject, hears the original PA announcement clearly, and immediately records what the subject reports hearing via the earpiece.
*   **Metric:** Calculate the "Critical Deviation Rate." Any deviation that changes the *action* required (e.g., wrong direction, missing negation) is marked as a Critical Failure. Minor grammatical errors are ignored.
*   **Sample Size:** Ensure each of the 14 team members tests at least three different high-risk command types across two different noise zones (e.g., near speakers vs. open concourse).

**Phase C: The "Stop-and-Verify" Interrupt**
During the drill, introduce unscheduled "Stop-and-Verify" moments. When a specific siren pattern sounds, the test subject must immediately repeat the last translated command into a recorder. This isolates real-time processing latency and immediate comprehension without the bias of post-drill memory.

**Phase D: Pass/Fail Criteria**
*   **Pass:** 0% Critical Failure rate on negation and directional commands.
*   **Conditional Pass:** <5% error rate on numeric/dense information, provided the general intent (e.g., "evacuate") remains clear.
*   **Fail:** Any single instance where a mistranslation leads a user toward danger or causes hesitation in a timed simulation.

**Recommendation:** If the on-device model fails the negation or directional tests, implement a "fallback protocol" where the earpiece defaults to a universal alarm tone and a simplified, non-translated keyword (e.g., "EXIT") rather than risking a complex, mistranslated sentence.

*Note: Ensure all test data is anonymized before sharing with the broader engineering team. This plan assumes the earpiece hardware remains stable under physical movement.*
