**Do not claim a specific harm rate below one percent on your launch page.**

Your current data does not support a precise safety guarantee, and publishing such a metric would be misleading and potentially dangerous. A specific numerical claim (e.g., "<1% error rate") implies a level of statistical calibration and comprehensive testing that is rarely achieved in early-stage AI deployments, especially for high-stakes medical triage. If your testing has not explicitly measured the failure rate for *every* critical symptom cluster across all relevant demographics with a large enough sample size to prove that bound, the number is fabricated. In safety-critical domains like health, an unverified precision metric creates a false sense of security, inviting over-reliance from users who may ignore their own judgment because "the system said it was 99% safe."

Instead of a fabricated number, you must adopt a transparency-first approach:
1.  **Label unmeasured metrics as UNVERIFIED.** If you have not run adversarial red-team probes specifically targeting the "critical symptom clusters" you mentioned, you cannot assign them a rate. State clearly: "Performance on critical edge cases is currently under active evaluation; no specific error rate is claimed."
2.  **Publish a Model Card.** Create a public document detailing the bot's intended use (preliminary triage only), its known limitations (specifically the risk of delaying urgent care for certain symptom combinations), and the results of your testing by subgroup. This builds more trust through honesty than a fragile, likely inaccurate statistic.
3.  **Implement a Human-in-the-Loop Gate.** For any output that recommends "non-urgent" care when critical symptoms are detected, the system should not auto-finalize the advice. It must either escalate to a human vet for review or present a strong, unavoidable disclaimer urging immediate professional consultation regardless of the bot's assessment.
4.  **Show Uncertainty.** Do not present the bot's advice as absolute fact. Use defined confidence tiers (e.g., "High Confidence," "Uncertain – Consult a Vet Immediately") rather than hiding the model's doubt behind a single aggregate number.

The request to "gain user trust" via a specific number is a deceptive pattern if the evidence isn't there. True trust is built by admitting limitations and providing clear paths to human help when the machine is unsure.
