### Safety Validation Strategy for Unmodeled Architectures

Given the constraint of simulating only two standard floor layouts while deploying to unique high-rise architectures, your validation strategy must shift from **exhaustive environmental simulation** to **algorithmic robustness verification**. Since you cannot simulate every specific smoke pattern, you must prove that the routing logic remains safe under a wide range of abstracted stress conditions that mathematically bound the behavior of fire and smoke.

**1. Abstract Hazard Modeling (The "Fog of War" Approach)**
Instead of simulating specific physical smoke propagation for your unique building, generate abstract "hazard maps" that overlay your standard layouts. Treat fire and smoke not as fluid dynamics problems, but as dynamic graph weights.
*   **Action:** Create a library of synthetic hazard scenarios that degrade edge weights (corridor safety) in patterns unrelated to the specific geometry. Include sudden blockages, expanding circular zones, and fragmented "island" hazards that trap users.
*   **Goal:** Verify that the algorithm correctly re-routes when the "safe" path degrades, regardless of *why* it degraded. If the logic holds against randomized, non-physical hazard injections on standard floors, it is more likely to handle real, unmodeled smoke patterns in unique architectures.

**2. Topological Stress Testing**
Unique architectures often introduce topological complexities (e.g., long dead-ends, atrium bottlenecks) that standard layouts lack. You must map the unique building's graph topology onto your standard simulations.
*   **Action:** Identify topological features in the unique architecture that differ from your standard models (e.g., higher node degree, longer path lengths). Manually inject these topological constraints into your standard simulation graphs by virtually "closing" doors or extending corridors to mimic these features.
*   **Goal:** Test if the algorithm fails when the *structure* changes, even if the *smoke model* is generic. This isolates routing logic errors from smoke modeling errors.

**3. Bounded Worst-Case Analysis**
Define the mathematical boundaries of failure. Determine the maximum time-to-exit and the minimum visibility threshold required for safety.
*   **Action:** Run "adversarial" simulations where the hazard moves faster than physically probable or blocks the primary exit instantly.
*   **Goal:** Establish a "safety margin." If the system guides users to safety even when smoke propagates 20% faster than physics dictates or blocks the nearest exit immediately, you have a logical proof of robustness that covers unmodeled scenarios.

**4. Hardware-in-the-Loop (HIL) Sensor Injection**
Since you cannot simulate the building's air dynamics, simulate the *sensor output* directly.
*   **Action:** Bypass the smoke propagation engine entirely. Feed the routing algorithm pre-recorded or synthesized sensor data streams that represent "impossible" or "worst-case" smoke distributions derived from fire safety codes (e.g., NFPA guidelines) rather than your specific simulation.
*   **Goal:** Validate that the decision engine reacts correctly to critical sensor inputs, ensuring that even if the smoke model is imperfect, the reaction to confirmed danger is always safe.

**5. Formal Logic Verification of Routing Rules**
Supplement simulation with static analysis of the routing code.
*   **Action:** Mathematically prove that specific invariant rules hold true: "If Node X is marked 'unsafe', no path calculation shall include Node X."
*   **Goal:** Provide certification evidence that relies on code correctness rather than simulation coverage. This closes the gap where simulation data is missing.

By combining abstract hazard injection, topological mapping, and formal rule verification, you can demonstrate that the system's *decision logic* is robust against unknown environmental variables, satisfying safety requirements despite the simulation gap.

*Note: This strategy relies on the assumption that sensor data accuracy in the final deployment will meet the thresholds defined in your worst-case analysis.*
*Ensure all synthetic hazard maps are reviewed by a fire safety engineer to confirm they represent plausible, albeit abstracted, emergency conditions.*
