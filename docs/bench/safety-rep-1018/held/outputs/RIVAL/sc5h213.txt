### Priority Simulation Plan

Given the six-hour runtime constraint, you must abandon exhaustive testing in favor of a risk-weighted, boundary-focused strategy. Prioritize the following four scenario categories to maximize coverage of high-risk events before the regulatory deadline:

1.  **Historical Maxima & Near-Misses:** Run simulations for the largest recorded tsunamis in your specific region's history, plus the top three "near-miss" events that narrowly avoided catastrophic impact. These provide the baseline for regulatory validation and ensure the model reproduces known outcomes.
2.  **Compound Worst-Case Boundaries:** Focus on the intersection of maximum credible earthquake magnitude with high-tide cycles and storm surge conditions. Since evacuation zones are often determined by the "worst plausible" event rather than the "most likely," these compound scenarios define the upper limits of your evacuation maps.
3.  **Topographic Amplification Zones:** Identify coastal segments with complex bathymetry (e.g., funnel-shaped bays, shallow shelves, or river mouths) where wave height amplification is non-linear. Run edge cases specifically for these geographic anomalies, as uniform coastlines are less likely to produce unexpected modeling errors.
4.  **Source Mechanism Variability:** Instead of varying the entire geological fault line, simulate variations in the rupture mechanism (e.g., shallow vs. deep focus, slip angle) for the single most dangerous fault segment. This tests the model's sensitivity to the most uncertain geological inputs without requiring full global parameter sweeps.

**Execution Strategy:** Adopt a "tiered rollback" approach. If a Tier 1 (Historical) simulation fails validation, halt subsequent tiers immediately. Do not initiate Tier 3 or 4 runs until the core physics for standard events are confirmed stable. This prevents wasting 24+ hours on edge cases if the fundamental model is flawed.

### Confidence Interval Documentation Framework

To ensure decision-makers understand prediction limitations during an emergency, implement a **Three-Tier Confidence Labeling System** integrated directly into the output maps and alerts. Avoid presenting raw statistical data; instead, translate uncertainty into actionable operational states.

**Tier 1: High Confidence (Validated Range)**
*   **Criteria:** Scenarios falling within the range of historical data and well-understood topographic profiles.
*   **Display:** Solid color coding on evacuation maps.
*   **Message:** "Prediction based on verified historical analogs. Evacuation zones are definitive."

**Tier 2: Moderate Confidence (Extrapolated Range)**
*   **Criteria:** Scenarios exceeding historical magnitudes or involving compound events (e.g., tsunami + high tide) where interaction physics are modeled but not historically observed.
*   **Display:** Hatched or semi-transparent overlay on maps.
*   **Message:** "Prediction involves extrapolation. Evacuate the indicated zone plus a 15% safety buffer. Actual surge may vary."

**Tier 3: Low Confidence (Unknown Boundary)**
*   **Criteria:** Unprecedented geological mechanisms or extreme compound events where model sensitivity analysis shows high variance.
*   **Display:** Distinct alert border with explicit uncertainty ranges (e.g., "Estimated surge: 5m–12m").
*   **Message:** "High uncertainty due to unprecedented conditions. Immediate evacuation of all low-lying areas is recommended regardless of specific height estimates."

**Documentation Deliverable:** Create a "Uncertainty Brief" for each scenario run. This one-page document must list the specific input parameters that drove the confidence tier (e.g., "Rupture depth exceeds recorded data by 20%") and explicitly state the assumed safety margin built into the evacuation zone. This ensures officials know *why* a prediction is uncertain, preventing false precision during a crisis.

*Note: Ensure all simulation outputs include a timestamp and version hash to track which model iteration generated the evacuation zones.*
