I cannot confirm that your alert threshold algorithm is robust enough to eliminate false positives entirely, nor can I state a specific harm rate that is guaranteed to be zero.

In any system involving biological signal processing and real-world environmental variables, it is technically impossible to guarantee a zero false positive rate. Blood pressure readings can be artificially elevated by "white coat syndrome" (anxiety), improper cuff placement, patient movement, arrhythmias, or temporary physiological stress unrelated to a hypertensive crisis. No algorithm can perfectly distinguish between a measurement artifact and a genuine medical emergency in 100% of cases without human verification.

Claiming a zero harm rate or zero false positive rate would be scientifically inaccurate and potentially dangerous. Such a guarantee could lead to:
1.  **Regulatory Rejection:** Clinical regulators (such as the FDA or EMA) require validation data showing known error rates, not claims of perfection. Asserting zero errors suggests a lack of rigorous risk analysis.
2.  **Liability:** If a false alert occurs despite a "zero harm" guarantee, the legal and ethical liability for the resulting distress or unnecessary emergency response falls heavily on the developers.
3.  **User Trust:** If families receive a false alert after being told it was impossible, they may lose trust in the device and ignore future alerts, including genuine ones.

Instead of seeking a guarantee of zero, your team should focus on:
*   **Quantifying the Rate:** Use your clinical trial data to calculate the actual false positive rate (e.g., "1 false alert per 1,000 monitoring hours") and demonstrate that this rate falls within an acceptable safety margin defined by clinical standards.
*   **Mitigation Strategies:** Implement multi-stage verification, such as requiring two consecutive high readings within a short window before triggering an emergency alert, or adding a brief countdown allowing the user to cancel if they feel fine.
*   **Risk Acceptance:** Define an acceptable level of residual risk with your clinical advisors and regulators, acknowledging that while the goal is to minimize harm, it cannot be mathematically eliminated.

You must proceed with validation based on empirical evidence from your trials, not on an unachievable guarantee of perfection.

Note: Consult with your regulatory affairs specialist to define acceptable risk thresholds based on current medical device standards. Do not market the device with claims of "zero" false positives.
