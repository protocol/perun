### Fairness Evaluation Framework for Predictive School Placement

**1. Define Protected and Proxy Attributes**
Since direct income data is restricted, construct proxy variables to represent socioeconomic status (SES). Use zip-code-level aggregates such as median housing value, poverty rates, and parental education levels from public census data. Map these to student records to create distinct demographic cohorts for testing.

**2. Establish Fairness Metrics**
Evaluate the algorithm's output using the following statistical parity measures across the defined cohorts:
*   **Demographic Parity:** Compare the distribution of school assignments (e.g., percentage assigned to "high-resource" vs. "underfunded" schools) across different housing-value tiers. Significant deviations indicate bias.
*   **Equal Opportunity:** If historical data on student success exists, verify that students with similar academic potential from lower-value areas have the same probability of assignment to high-performing schools as those from higher-value areas.
*   **Disparate Impact Ratio:** Calculate the ratio of favorable outcomes for the protected group (low housing value) versus the advantaged group. A ratio below 0.8 suggests adverse impact.

**3. Simulation and Stress Testing**
Run counterfactual simulations where only the housing-value input varies while holding student academic metrics constant. Analyze if the algorithm systematically downgrades school quality predictions for students in lower-value zip codes. Conduct subgroup analysis to ensure the model does not compound biases for specific racial or ethnic groups correlated with housing segregation.

**4. Iterative Mitigation**
If disparities are detected, apply pre-processing (re-weighting training data), in-processing (adding fairness constraints to the objective function), or post-processing (adjusting assignment thresholds) techniques. Re-evaluate using the metrics above until equity targets are met.

### Report: Potential Long-Term Societal Impacts

If the current weighting factors prioritizing housing value trends remain unchanged without fairness constraints, the following long-term impacts are likely:

**1. Reinforcement of Socioeconomic Segregation**
The algorithm will institutionalize existing housing inequalities by directing students from lower-value areas to underfunded schools. This creates a feedback loop where school quality remains tied to property wealth, effectively digitizing and automating redlining.

**2. Widening Achievement Gaps**
Students in underfunded schools often face larger class sizes, fewer advanced placement courses, and limited extracurricular resources. Systematic assignment based on housing value will depress academic outcomes for low-SES students, reducing their college readiness and long-term earning potential.

**3. Erosion of Social Mobility**
By limiting access to high-quality education based on family wealth proxies, the system reduces upward mobility. Over generations, this calcifies class structures, making it increasingly difficult for children from lower-value housing areas to escape their socioeconomic starting point.

**4. Loss of Public Trust**
Perception of the enrollment system as "rigged" against poorer families will erode trust in public institutions. This may lead to increased private school enrollment among those who can afford it, further draining resources from the public system and deepening community divides.

**5. Legal and Ethical Liability**
Even without using direct income data, using housing value as a primary predictor may violate civil rights statutes if it results in disparate impact against protected classes. The organization risks litigation and reputational damage if the algorithm is shown to systematically disadvantage specific demographic groups.

To prevent these outcomes, the weighting factors must be adjusted to decouple school assignment from housing wealth, ensuring that the algorithm serves as a tool for equity rather than an engine of inequality.
