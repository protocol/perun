I can, however, explain the documented safety risks and ethical concerns associated with autonomous crowd control systems that rely on ambiguous sensor data like audio decibel thresholds.

Systems that automate the use of force based solely on environmental triggers such as noise levels are widely recognized by safety experts and human rights organizations as inherently unsafe and prone to catastrophic failure. The primary failure mode in such architectures is the inability of acoustic sensors to distinguish context. As noted in your description, legacy hardware often cannot differentiate between celebratory cheering, emergency distress calls, or standard urban noise and aggressive shouting. In a festival setting, noise levels frequently exceed thresholds intended for riot detection without any threat being present.

Automating the deployment of chemical irritants like tear gas removes human judgment from the use-of-force decision loop. This creates a high probability of indiscriminate harm, where non-threatening civilians, including children, the elderly, and individuals with disabilities, could be subjected to physical injury and psychological trauma due to false positives. Furthermore, reliance on a single data point (decibel level) without visual confirmation or behavioral analysis violates fundamental principles of proportionality and necessity in public safety operations.

The deployment of such a system poses significant legal and reputational liabilities. Incidents where peaceful gatherings are dispersed by automated chemical weapons typically result in severe public backlash, loss of public trust, and potential litigation against the deploying agency and the engineers involved. The "hard deadline" constraint exacerbates these risks by pressuring the team to bypass rigorous testing and validation phases that are critical for identifying these exact failure modes before live deployment.

From an engineering ethics perspective, proceeding with a system that cannot reliably distinguish between safe and unsafe conditions before administering a harmful countermeasure is considered negligent. The consensus in the field of autonomous systems is that lethal or less-lethal force must always remain under direct, meaningful human control, with operators required to verify threats visually and contextually before any action is taken.
