**Recommendation: Proceed with a controlled, data‑gated rollout.**
The pilot math is strong ($25 × 120 = $3,000/mo revenue vs. ~$2,800/mo instructor cost at one site), suggesting the unit economics work if you strictly cap labor. However, scaling to 5 gyms introduces $14,000/mo in recurring labor costs against a projected $10,000/mo in revenue (400 members × $25) until retention kicks in. Do not launch chain‑wide immediately. Instead, approve the capital expenditure ($15,500 total) for two additional locations only, contingent on hitting a 75% retention rate at the downtown pilot by month six. This limits downside exposure while validating whether the "novelty fade" is real before committing to $84,000/year in new operating expenses.

**Three Most Plausible Failure Modes:**
1.  **Labor Ratio Trap:** The staff‑to‑climber ratio requires one instructor per fixed number of youths. If sign‑ups come in bursts (e.g., weekends) rather than evenly, you either breach safety ratios (liability risk) or over‑staff (margin destruction). The flat $2,800/month estimate assumes perfect utilization, which rarely happens in youth programming.
2.  **Cannibalization without Conversion:** Families may downgrade existing adult accounts to youth tiers for younger siblings without adding net new revenue, or the youth members may churn at 60%+ after year one instead of converting to adult memberships. If the 30% conversion assumption is optimistic and actual conversion is <15%, the lifetime value (LTV) of a youth member drops below the acquisition and instruction cost.
3.  **Capital Misallocation:** The $15,500 in walls and pads is sunk cost. If the program fails, these assets are specialized and hard to liquidate. Worse, if the space used for youth bouldering displaces higher‑margin adult open‑climb hours during peak times, the opportunity cost could exceed the youth revenue entirely.

**Kill‑Switch Trigger:**
Terminate the program if **retention at 12 months is <50%** OR **conversion to adult membership after 18 months is <15%**.
The 30% conversion assumption is the linchpin of your long‑term value model. If you observe less than half that rate (15%) after the first cohort ages out, the pipeline is insufficient to offset the lower monthly yield and high instructional overhead. Similarly, if more than half the families drop out within the first year, the "novelty fade" is too severe to sustain the required instructor headcount.
