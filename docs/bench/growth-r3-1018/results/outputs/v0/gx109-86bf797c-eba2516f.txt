**Recommendation: Adopt "Weekly Active Patients with a Confirmed Clinical Intervention" as your North Star Metric.**

Your intuition is correct: daily device usage is a vanity metric in this context because high usage without clinical action does not improve health. Conversely, hospital readmission rates, while the ultimate outcome, are too lagging (often 30+ days) and too noisy (influenced by patient comorbidities, social determinants, and hospital discharge policies) to serve as a primary driver for weekly engineering and clinical alignment. You need a leading indicator that proves your system is not just collecting data, but actively changing care.

**Evaluation of Candidates:**

1.  **Daily Device Usage (Reject):**
    *   *Pros:* Easy to measure; indicates technical connectivity.
    *   *Cons:* As your pilot showed, this can rise while outcomes remain flat. It incentivizes engineering to maximize uptime rather than clinical utility. A patient can wear a device 24/7 and still suffer an adverse event if no one acts on the data. This is a classic "instrument-up" trap.

2.  **Hospital Readmission Rates (Keep as Guardrail, not North Star):**
    *   *Pros:* Directly ties to hospital financial incentives (value-based care) and patient health.
    *   *Cons:* Too slow for iterative product development. With only 500 patients over six months, your statistical power to detect significant changes against external noise is low. Using this as the primary metric will paralyze decision-making until you have years of data.

3.  **Alert Response Times (Keep as Operational SLO):**
    *   *Pros:* Measures clinical team efficiency.
    *   *Cons:* Optimizing for speed alone can lead to alert fatigue or false positives. Fast response to a non-critical alert adds no value. This is a system health metric, not a customer value metric.

4.  **Weekly Active Patients with a Confirmed Clinical Intervention (Selected):**
    *   *Definition:* The count of unique patients per week where device data triggered a specific, documented clinical action (e.g., medication adjustment, nurse call, provider notification) within a defined time window.
    *   *Why it works:* It bridges the gap between raw data (usage) and long-term outcomes (readmissions). It forces engineering to build reliable alerting and data clarity, and it forces clinical teams to engage with the data meaningfully. If this number rises, you are demonstrably altering the course of care, which is the necessary precursor to reducing readmissions.
    *   *Anti-gaming:* To prevent gaming, the "intervention" must be verified (e.g., logged in the EHR or care platform) and tied to a threshold breach, not just a manual check-in.

**Implementation Framework for Dr. Evans:**

Present this metric as the "Value Delivery Rate." It answers the question: "How many patients are we actually helping this week?"
*   **Input Metrics:** Device connectivity rate (ensure data flows), Alert precision % (reduce noise), Clinical team SLA adherence.
*   **Guardrail Metric:** 30-day Readmission Rate (monitor to ensure interventions aren't causing harm or missing big picture trends).
*   **Behavioral Alignment:** Engineering focuses on data reliability and actionable alert logic; Clinical teams focus on closing the loop on alerts. Both teams move the same needle.

This approach allows you to show hospital partners immediate, tangible activity that correlates with care quality, without waiting months for readmission data to validate your pilot.

*Note: Ensure the definition of "Confirmed Clinical Intervention" is standardized across partner hospitals to maintain data consistency. Route all patient identifier handling to your privacy compliance protocol to maintain pseudonymity in analytics.*
