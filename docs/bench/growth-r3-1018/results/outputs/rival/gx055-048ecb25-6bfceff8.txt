# FreshBox Pilot Success Framework: Viability Beyond Volatility

Given your small market size (12,000) and high sensitivity to local events, relying on weekly retention rates is statistically dangerous. A single local festival can skew a week's data by 20%+ purely due to sample size, not product failure. To secure your $2M expansion fund, you must shift from **point-in-time metrics** (weekly snapshots) to **cohort-based resilience metrics** that smooth out local noise.

Present the following four-pillar framework to your board:

### 1. Primary Metric: 4-Week Cohort Retention Curve
Stop reporting "weekly retention." Instead, track the percentage of subscribers who joined in a specific week (a cohort) and are still active 4, 8, and 12 weeks later.
*   **Why:** This isolates the product experience from external calendar events. If the "Jan 15 Cohort" retains 70% of users after month one, that signal holds true regardless of whether a festival happened in week 3 or week 4.
*   **Success Threshold:** Define success as a flattening curve where retention stabilizes above 65% by week 4. If the curve flattens, the business is viable; if it continues to drop, the model leaks.

### 2. Secondary Metric: Order Frequency per Active Subscriber
Measure the average number of orders placed per *active* subscriber over a rolling 30-day window, excluding the first 14 days (onboarding period).
*   **Why:** This separates "churn" (leaving forever) from "pause" (skipping a week due to a festival). In a grocery model, a user skipping a festival week but ordering the next two is a success, not a failure.
*   **Success Threshold:** Target >0.8 orders per week per active user over a 30-day rolling average. This proves that when life is normal, the habit sticks.

### 3. Growth Quality: Organic Referral Rate
Track the percentage of new sign-ups attributed to "Word of Mouth" or direct traffic (no ads) versus paid channels.
*   **Why:** In a town of 12,000, organic growth is the ultimate validator of product-market fit. High paid acquisition with low organic spread suggests the product works only when subsidized.
*   **Success Threshold:** >30% of new subscribers coming from organic/referral sources by Month 3. This demonstrates the "community virus" required for expansion.

### 4. Unit Economics: Contribution Margin per Delivery Route
Calculate revenue minus variable costs (food, packaging, delivery fuel/labor) per specific delivery route, not per individual order.
*   **Why:** In dense, small markets, profitability is driven by route density, not individual order margin. One festival might lower order volume, but if you optimize routes to maintain margin, the model holds.
*   **Success Threshold:** Positive contribution margin on 80% of routes, even during low-volume weeks.

### Presentation Strategy for the Board
Do not present a single line graph of weekly retention. Instead, present a **Cohort Heatmap**:
*   **Rows:** Sign-up weeks (Jan Week 3, Jan Week 4, etc.)
*   **Columns:** Weeks since sign-up (Week 1, Week 2, Week 3...)
*   **Cells:** Retention %

This visualizes that while *absolute* numbers dip during the festival week across all rows (the external factor), the *relative* decay rate of each cohort remains consistent. This proves the drop was environmental, not structural.

**Narrative for the Board:** "Our pilot proves that while local events cause temporary volume volatility, the underlying unit economics and cohort retention remain stable. The model is not fragile; it is simply sensitive to community rhythms, which we will account for in our expansion forecasting."
