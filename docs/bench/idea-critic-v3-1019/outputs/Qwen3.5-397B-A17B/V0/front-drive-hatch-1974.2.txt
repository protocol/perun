The claim is that replacing a iconic, rear-engined air-cooled car with a radical front-engine, water-cooled hatchback during an oil crisis and profit decline will save the company. The steelman is that the core product is commercially dying, and only a complete technological and stylistic pivot can secure long-term survival despite short-term financial pain.

However, the failure hypothesis is severe: executing a capital-intensive retooling while profits are shrinking risks immediate insolvency before the new model generates revenue. The company lacks experience with the proposed engineering layout, increasing the risk of costly delays or quality failures. Furthermore, alienating the loyal base of the existing car without a guaranteed mass-market replacement during a demand contraction could collapse sales entirely.

Alternatives include evolving the existing platform to meet emissions/efficiency standards at lower cost, or seeking a partner to share development burdens. Doing nothing preserves cash but accepts eventual obsolescence.

The pre-mortem reveals the most likely cause of failure is running out of cash during the retooling phase due to underestimated costs and overestimated speed to market. This is a one-way door; once the old lines are scrapped, there is no retreat. The cost of delay is the continued erosion of the core model's sales, but the cost of action is potentially total company failure.

Given the lack of verified evidence that the company has the liquidity to survive the transition, the inexperience with the new technology, and the macroeconomic headwinds reducing total demand, the risk profile is unacceptable without a confirmed funding source or a phased approach. The proposal assumes the market will wait for the new car and that the company can execute a complex engineering shift flawlessly while broke. These are fatal gaps.

DECISION: NO-GO
