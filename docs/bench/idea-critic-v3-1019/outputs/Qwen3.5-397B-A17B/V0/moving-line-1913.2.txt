The claim is that converting to a moving assembly line will lower prices through efficiency gains despite high upfront costs and worker dissatisfaction. The steelman is that standardization and flow reduce unit labor time enough to offset conversion costs and turnover, creating a dominant market position via price leadership.

However, critical flaws exist in the current proposal. First, the "high volume of a single model" requirement creates extreme fragility; if demand shifts or the single design fails, the entire capital investment becomes stranded with no flexibility to pivot. Second, the risk of "high worker turnover" is treated as a manageable side effect rather than a potential operational killer; if the labor pool refuses the repetitive conditions, the line stops, and the fixed-cost structure becomes a liability. Third, the proposal assumes rivals cannot match the price drop quickly enough to erode the first-mover advantage, ignoring that competitors might adopt the same method without the burden of retrofitting an active plant.

The "do nothing" alternative maintains flexibility and variety, protecting against design fatigue. A "better way" might be a phased pilot on a secondary line to prove turnover rates and actual throughput gains before risking the main plant's output. The current plan lacks a specific kill criterion: at what exact turnover rate or volume shortfall does the project halt? Without a capped, time-boxed trial, the company bets its entire liquidity on an unproven social and mechanical experiment.

The evidence provided admits the conversion disrupts output immediately. If the ramp-up takes longer than anticipated due to worker resistance or mechanical teething issues, the cash flow gap could bankrupt the operation before savings materialize. The proposal offers no buffer for this delay.

DECISION: NO-GO
