The claim is that a dual-tier streaming service with equity-based licensing will succeed in 2008 Europe despite piracy and low ad yields. The steelman: offering instant access via a legal, free ad-supported tier converts pirates better than litigation, while equity alignment secures catalog access and upside for labels.

However, the failure hypothesis is strong: the unit economics likely collapse before scale. In 2008, mobile data is expensive and Wi-Fi is spotty, limiting "instant" desktop usage to specific contexts. More critically, ad rates for audio streaming are unproven and likely insufficient to cover the "large minimum payments" demanded by majors. If most users stay on the free tier (high probability given easy piracy), the company burns cash on guarantees without sufficient revenue per listener. The equity offered may be worthless if the cash burn kills the venture before an exit.

Alternatives include a niche high-fidelity paid-only service (lower risk, lower ceiling) or waiting for smartphone ubiquity (better UX, higher engagement). The "do nothing" option avoids the massive upfront liability.

The pre-mortem reveals the company runs out of cash within 18 months because label guarantees exceed ad revenue and subscription conversion remains below 5%. This is a one-way door: once multi-year guarantees are signed, the capital structure is locked, and failure means total loss. The cost of delay is low; waiting for smartphone penetration (2009-2010) drastically improves the "instant" value proposition and ad inventory quality.

The proposal assumes ad revenue will scale linearly with users and that equity will satisfy label cash-flow needs. Evidence suggests labels prioritize guaranteed cash over speculative equity in downturns. Without a verified path to positive unit economics on the free tier or a mechanism to cap downside risk on guarantees, the financial model is fragile.

The strongest attack survived is that labels might accept equity; however, this does not solve the immediate cash-flow mismatch between guaranteed payouts and slow ad-revenue ramp. The kill criterion is clear: if projected ad revenue per user does not exceed licensing costs within 12 months, the model fails. Given the 2008 context, this threshold is unlikely to be met.

DECISION: NO-GO
