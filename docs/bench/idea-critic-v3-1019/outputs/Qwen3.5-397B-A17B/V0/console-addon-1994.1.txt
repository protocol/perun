The claim is that a $160 add-on can effectively bridge the 16-bit to 32-bit gap without cannibalizing the upcoming true 32-bit successor. Steelmanned: this extends the current lifecycle and keeps developers engaged during the transition year.

However, the attack reveals fatal flaws. First, **cannibalization**: releasing a near-32-bit experience for $160 one year before a full next-gen console creates a "wait" incentive. Why would consumers buy a partial upgrade now when the complete solution arrives soon? Second, **developer fragmentation**: forcing studios to split resources between rushed add-on titles, standard 16-bit games, and the new 32-bit architecture dilutes quality across all platforms. Third, **technical deception**: an add-on cannot truly replicate 32-bit architecture; marketing it as such risks brand credibility if performance fails to match promises.

The "do nothing" alternative preserves brand trust and focuses developer effort on the inevitable future. The "cheaper way" is accelerating first-party 16-bit titles to maximize the current install base's value without confusing the market.

The pre-mortem is clear: the add-on launches with a tiny library, confuses consumers who delay purchases anticipating the real next-gen, and fractures developer support, causing both the add-on and the subsequent console to underperform. The cost of delay (waiting for the true next-gen) is zero compared to the cost of a failed bridge product that tarnishes the brand.

There is no reversible path here; once announced, market confusion is immediate. The kill criterion is simple: if the add-on's price point is within 50% of the rumored next-gen console price, it will fail. At $160, it likely is.

This proposal attempts to solve a timing problem with a product that solves nothing for consumers (who will wait) and everything negative for developers (who must rush). It violates the core principle of clear generational transitions.

DECISION: NO-GO
