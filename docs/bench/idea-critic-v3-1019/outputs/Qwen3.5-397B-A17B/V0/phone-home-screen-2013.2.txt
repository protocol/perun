The claim assumes users want a social feed to replace their primary phone interface, ignoring that a home screen is a utility launcher, not a content stream. The steelman—that this maximizes engagement by removing friction—fails against the "better way" of integrating deeply into the existing OS via widgets or notifications, which offers high visibility without the friction of a full replacement.

The proposal suffers from fatal distribution and utility gaps. Limiting support to "a handful of phone models" in 2013 (primarily Android) excludes the growing iOS market and fragmenting Android landscape, capping reach immediately. Pushing other apps out of view creates active hostility; users need maps, cameras, and keyboards instantly, not just social feeds. This friction leads to abandonment, not retention.

Privacy concerns regarding "always-on" access are not merely reputational risks but adoption killers. Users are unlikely to grant a single app total control over their device's entry point, especially one known for data harvesting. The "partner phone" strategy is a costly hardware gamble that distracts from the core software mission and risks becoming shelfware if the software experience falters.

The pre-mortem is clear: The launcher installs, annoys users by hiding essential tools, drains battery via constant syncing, and is disabled within 48 hours. The "do nothing" alternative (improving the main app) retains users without risking the brand on a fragile, intrusive layer. The cost of delay is negligible compared to the cost of a public failure in a new category. There is no reversible "small step" here; a launcher is binary—it either becomes the daily driver or it is deleted. The leading metric would be "Day-7 active launchers," which historically collapses for third-party social launchers.

DECISION: NO-GO
