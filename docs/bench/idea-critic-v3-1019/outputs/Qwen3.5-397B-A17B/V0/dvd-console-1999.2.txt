The proposal bets on short-term hardware losses to secure long-term platform dominance via DVD adoption and ecosystem loyalty. The steelman is that absorbing a per-unit loss now creates an insurmountable barrier to entry for rivals by bundling a premium media player at a console price, locking in users and developers for a decade.

However, the skeptic identifies a fatal fragility: the plan relies on high chip yields to meet launch demand while pricing below cost. If yields remain low, supply will be constrained precisely when marketing spend is highest, ceding the holiday season to the rival launching a year earlier. Without volume, the loss-leader strategy collapses into pure loss without market share gain. Furthermore, if developers struggle with the architecture, the software library will not justify the hardware subsidy, leaving the company bleeding cash on unsold inventory.

The "better way" analysis suggests that if the goal is media dominance, licensing the DVD technology to existing players is cheaper than subsidizing a gaming console. If the goal is gaming dominance, a cheaper, non-DVD unit might ensure higher volume and developer ease, avoiding the yield bottleneck. The current plan attempts to win two expensive wars (media format and gaming architecture) simultaneously with thin margins and unproven manufacturing.

The pre-mortem reveals the most likely failure mode: a perfect storm of low yields and developer friction leads to a weak launch lineup and empty shelves. The rival captures the mindshare, and the company is stuck with expensive, unsold units that cost more to make than they sell for, with no path to profitability because the installed base never materializes. The kill criterion is clear: if chip yields cannot be guaranteed to meet 80% of forecasted demand three months prior to launch, the financial risk outweighs the strategic benefit. Since the proposal admits yields are a risk and offers no mitigation other than hope, and given the rival's head start, the probability of catastrophic financial loss is too high to approve as is. The plan requires a guaranteed supply chain or a revised pricing strategy before commitment.

DECISION: NO-GO
