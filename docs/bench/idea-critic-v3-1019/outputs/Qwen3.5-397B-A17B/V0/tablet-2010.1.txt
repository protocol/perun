The claim is that a 10-inch tablet, priced at $499 and lacking a camera or multitasking, will succeed where previous tablets failed by leveraging an existing phone ecosystem. The steelman is that the device fills a specific "third pocket" niche for media consumption and light browsing, offering a superior app experience compared to netbooks without the complexity or cost of a full laptop, relying on brand loyalty and the immediate availability of popular apps.

However, the skeptic identifies fatal flaws in the current proposal. First, the price point of $499 is dangerously close to entry-level laptops which offer full multitasking, keyboards, and cameras, making the tablet's limitations (no camera, no multitasking) hard to justify to rational consumers. Second, historical data explicitly states tablets have sold poorly for a decade; ignoring this without a distinct differentiator beyond screen size is reckless. Third, the "no camera" constraint is a significant regression in a consumer electronics market rapidly moving toward integrated communication features. The "better way" analysis suggests that either including a camera and multitasking to match laptop utility or dropping the price significantly below $300 to position it as an impulse buy are necessary to overcome the "big phone" criticism. As proposed, the device offers less functionality than a laptop and a phone for a premium price, creating a value gap that the brand alone may not bridge. The pre-mortem reveals the most likely failure mode is consumer confusion: it is too expensive to be a toy, yet too limited to be a primary computing device, resulting in inventory stagnation similar to previous decade's failures. The lack of a clear "killer app" exclusive to the tablet form factor, combined with removed features standard in 2010, makes the risk unacceptably high.

DECISION: NO-GO
