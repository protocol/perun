The claim is that acquiring a major US web portal for $12.5 billion in stock will secure a dominant global presence for a Spanish-language telecom provider. The steelman version posits that only a "big bang" acquisition can bypass organic growth barriers to instantly establish US scale, leveraging the portal's existing user base to cross-sell telecom services globally.

However, the attack reveals fatal flaws. First, the target ranks behind rivals in traffic; buying a declining asset at a premium multiple of revenue destroys capital rather than creating it. Second, the payment method is 100% volatile stock from a company already experiencing price instability, effectively allowing the US target's shareholders to gamble with the acquirer's currency during a bubble. Third, the operational complexity of integrating two distinct cultures across two continents without a proven track record introduces massive execution risk that could paralyze both entities.

The "do nothing" or "smaller partnership" alternatives are superior. A strategic alliance or minority stake would test US market fit without betting the company on a single, overpriced, hard-to-integrate asset. The proposal fails the "what would have to be true" test: it requires the target's traffic decline to reverse immediately post-acquisition and the integration to succeed perfectly despite cultural and geographic friction, all while the acquirer's stock remains inflated enough to justify the price. History suggests such cross-border, cross-industry mega-mergers often result in value destruction due to integration failure and overpayment.

The strongest attack survived is the urgency argument—that speed is required—but speed does not justify buying a losing asset at a peak price with volatile currency. The cost of delay is lower than the cost of a failed $12.5 billion integration. There are no kill criteria provided to limit losses if traffic continues to drop or integration stalls.

DECISION: NO-GO
