The claim is that acquiring a UK/Ireland teen social network for $850M cash in 2008 will successfully pivot a legacy ad portal to youth social networking. The steelman: this purchase instantly grants critical mass in a hard-to-reach demographic, leveraging existing IM infrastructure to lock in users before a larger rival dominates.

However, the attack reveals fatal flaws. First, the price-to-revenue ratio is extreme; paying $850M for "small revenue" implies betting entirely on future hyper-growth in a market known for rapid churn and fickle teen preferences. Second, the "larger rival" growing fast suggests the target is already the #2 option; in network effects, #2 often becomes worthless quickly if #1 pulls ahead. Third, the buyer's history of shifting strategies indicates poor integration discipline; layering a volatile social culture over a rigid portal bureaucracy usually drives the very talent and user base being purchased away.

The "do nothing" or "build/buy smaller" alternatives are ignored. A cheaper path would be integrating social features into the existing IM service organically or acquiring smaller, innovative tech teams rather than an expensive, potentially peaking asset. The cost of delay is low compared to the risk of immediate overpayment; waiting clarifies whether the target or the rival wins the market.

The pre-mortem is clear: 18 months post-acquisition, user growth stalls as teens migrate to the larger rival, the portal's ad model clashes with social UX, and the $850M is written down as goodwill impairment. The integration with IM feels forced, accelerating exodus. There is no defined kill criterion or metric for success here, only a massive cash outflow based on hope.

Given the buyer's unstable strategic history, the premium price for small revenue, and the existential threat of the faster-growing rival, this acquisition is a classic "buying the peak" error. The risk of total capital loss outweighs the potential upside.

DECISION: NO-GO
