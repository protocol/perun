The claim is that a cash-strapped, shrinking computer maker can successfully pivot to a 64-bit console market in 1993 by launching early in two cities at $249. The steelman: being first to market with superior 64-bit architecture creates a psychological lead and developer foothold before established rivals release their competing next-gen hardware.

However, the attack reveals fatal structural flaws. First, the "better way" analysis fails: doing nothing preserves remaining cash, while the proposed path burns scarce capital on hardware manufacturing and marketing without a software library. A console without games is useless; the proposal admits third-party commitment is low and the hardware is difficult to program. This creates a chicken-and-egg death spiral: gamers won't buy a console without games, and developers won't invest in a hard-to-program platform with no installed base.

Second, the financial premise is unsupported. The company has "little cash," yet launching hardware requires massive upfront investment in tooling, inventory, and marketing to compete against two large rivals entering within a year. A limited two-city launch does not solve the distribution problem; it merely delays the inevitable cash crunch while fragmenting marketing impact. The price point ($249) may be competitive, but without volume sales driven by hit software, the unit economics will likely fail to recoup R&D.

Third, the pre-mortem is clear: the most likely failure mode is running out of money six months post-launch due to poor software sales, leaving the company with unsold inventory and no path to profitability. The "first 64-bit" marketing angle is a weak differentiator against established brands with stronger game libraries. The cost of delay is negligible compared to the cost of a failed launch that bankrupts the firm.

There is no viable kill criterion that saves this specific plan; the fundamental mismatch between limited resources, high development friction, and the critical need for third-party software makes success statistically impossible. The proposal ignores the reality that platform wars are won by ecosystems, not just bit-width or launch timing.

DECISION: NO-GO
