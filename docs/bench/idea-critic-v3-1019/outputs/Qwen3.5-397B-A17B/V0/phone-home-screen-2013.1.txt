The claim assumes users want a social feed to replace their primary phone interface, ignoring that home screens are launchpads for diverse tasks, not just social consumption. The steelman is that deep integration increases engagement by reducing friction to view content. However, the "better way" analysis reveals a fatal flaw: this approach fights the OS vendor's control and user habits simultaneously. Unlike a standalone app, a home screen replacement is intrusive, difficult to uninstall, and easily perceived as hijacking the device.

The pre-mortem is clear: users install it out of curiosity, find their utility apps (maps, camera, banking) buried or harder to access, and revert to the stock launcher within days. The "partner phone" constraint limits scale to a niche, while the "open OS" dependency means a single platform update could break the launcher entirely, killing the product overnight. Privacy concerns regarding "always-on" access to the home screen will trigger regulatory scrutiny and user backlash far faster than a standard app.

The proposal fails the "do nothing" test; building a superior standalone app with widget support achieves 80% of the visibility goal with 10% of the friction and risk. The cost of delay is low because the core app already exists; forcing a UI layer adds development debt without guaranteeing retention. The strongest attack is behavioral: users curate their home screens for efficiency, not passive consumption. Replacing this with a walled garden of social posts creates immediate utility loss.

There is no reversible "small step" here; launching a launcher is a binary, high-visibility bet that alienates users if it fails. The kill criterion is missing, but the premise itself is flawed: you cannot force attention by occupying real estate users value for other reasons. This is a solution looking for a problem that the main app already solves more elegantly.

DECISION: NO-GO
