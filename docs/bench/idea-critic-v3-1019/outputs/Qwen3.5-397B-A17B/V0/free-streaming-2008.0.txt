The claim is that licensing major labels via upfront payments and equity in 2008 Europe will create a viable business against piracy. The steelman: only legitimate, instant access with full catalogs can convert pirates, and labels must be partners to survive.

However, the skepticism is severe. The model assumes ad revenue can subsidize free users, yet the proposal admits ad revenue per listener is low. With "most users never paying," the unit economics likely collapse: high fixed licensing costs (minimum guarantees) versus low variable revenue. The "equity" sweetener does not pay monthly server bills or recoup large upfront cash outlays if conversion lags.

The "better way" analysis suggests this specific capital structure is fragile. A cheaper path exists: negotiate revenue-share-only deals without massive minimums, or launch with independent labels first to prove conversion before approaching majors. The current plan bets the company on unproven conversion rates to satisfy incumbent gatekeepers who have little incentive to help a new competitor cannibalize their existing declining sales.

The pre-mortem is clear: the company runs out of cash within 18 months. Labels demand their minimum guarantees regardless of user growth. Ad markets dip (as they did in 2008-2009). User acquisition costs rise while conversion stays below the breakeven threshold. The "free piracy remains easy" risk means the value proposition is not strong enough to drive the necessary paid conversion to cover the heavy licensing overhead.

The kill criterion is missing: there is no defined conversion rate or revenue-per-user threshold that triggers a stop-loss before the upfront capital is exhausted. Without a verified path to positive unit economics on the first day, or a licensing model that aligns costs with actual revenue rather than fixed minimums, this is a bet on hope, not math. The risks (high fixed costs, low ad yield, low conversion, persistent piracy) overwhelmingly outweigh the unproven benefit.

DECISION: NO-GO
