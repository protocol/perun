Steelman: Launching a $199 online-enabled console a year early captures the hardcore market and establishes an ecosystem before the competitor arrives, leveraging first-mover advantage in connectivity to offset weak third-party support.

However, the attack on this claim reveals fatal flaws in the current context. First, the financial constraint is binding: selling hardware at a loss is explicitly ruled out by the company's trouble, yet a $199 price point for a modem-equipped machine in 1998 likely requires subsidization to compete, creating an immediate cash-flow crisis. Second, the "online play" differentiator relies on software; with major publishers refusing to commit, the unique selling proposition has no content to drive adoption, rendering the modem a costly feature for an empty lobby. Third, the piracy risk associated with the disc format threatens any narrow margin available, potentially turning every unit sold into a net loss if software revenue cannot be captured. Finally, the "wait" behavior from consumers is rational; if the market leader's machine is known to arrive in one year, early adopters may still delay purchasing a potentially inferior, unsupported platform rather than buy twice.

The proposal assumes that being first to market with online features overrides the lack of games and financial instability. This is unproven and dangerous. Without confirmed hit titles to justify the hardware purchase, and without the capital to sustain losses until scale is reached, the launch risks accelerating the company's collapse rather than reversing it. The piracy vector further undermines the economic model. A "go" decision here ignores the verified lack of publisher support and the rigid financial ceiling. The safer path is to secure software commitments and a sustainable cost structure before announcing a launch date, even if it means ceding the one-year window.

DECISION: NO-GO
