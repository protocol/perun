The claim assumes hub-and-spoke congestion will persist and that airports will invest heavily to accommodate a 550-seat double-decker. The steelman is that this aircraft uniquely solves capacity constraints at saturated major hubs where slot restrictions limit frequency increases.

However, the "better way" analysis reveals a critical vulnerability: the industry is shifting toward point-to-point travel using smaller, efficient twin-engine jets. This trend bypasses congested hubs entirely, eroding the core value proposition of a very large aircraft. If airlines prefer frequency over capacity, the market for 550-seat jets shrinks drastically. The rival's potential strategy to stretch an existing jumbo offers a lower-risk, lower-cost alternative for carriers still needing large capacity, potentially cannibalizing sales before development completes.

The pre-mortem indicates failure if airport infrastructure upgrades lag behind the aircraft's readiness or if fuel efficiency standards tighten, making four engines economically unviable compared to twins. The development budget of $10.7 billion is massive; any overrun threatens the manufacturer's solvency given the narrow addressable market. The risk that "the market for very large aircraft may be small" is not just a risk but a probable outcome given the point-to-point shift.

While 50 firm orders exist, they represent only six customers, creating dangerous concentration risk. If even two launch customers defect due to delays or changing network strategies, the program's economics collapse. The proposal lacks a clear kill criterion tied to order book diversity or infrastructure commitment timelines. Without evidence that hub congestion will worsen rather than dissolve via operational changes or point-to-point routing, the bet relies on a static view of aviation dynamics.

The strongest attack survived is the immediate cash flow from 50 orders, yet this does not mitigate the long-term structural mismatch between the product and the emerging point-to-point model. The cost of delay is high, but the cost of building the wrong plane is existential.

DECISION: NO-GO
