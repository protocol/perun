The claim is that replacing stationary assembly with a powered moving line will lower costs enough to justify conversion risks and labor turnover. The steelman: a moving line minimizes worker movement and standardizes task duration, creating unprecedented throughput that dwarfs the cost of conversion and allows price reductions to capture massive market share, offsetting turnover via higher wages or simplified hiring.

However, critical failures loom. First, the "high volume" requirement is a binary switch; if demand dips even slightly during the disruptive conversion period, the fixed cost of the line becomes a bankruptcy trigger rather than an efficiency engine. Second, the assumption that buyers will tolerate a single design ignores the competitive moat of variety; if rivals introduce minor customization, the standardized product becomes obsolete regardless of price. Third, the labor risk is understated: repetitive motion injuries and psychological burnout could cause strikes that halt the entire line, whereas stationary teams offer redundancy.

The "do nothing" option retains flexibility and variety, protecting against demand shocks. A "better way" might be hybridizing: moving lines only for sub-assemblies while keeping final assembly flexible to test market tolerance for variety before full commitment. The proposal lacks a kill criterion for demand elasticity or a phased rollout plan to mitigate the "all-in" risk. Without evidence that the market size guarantees the necessary volume to amortize the conversion cost immediately, the bet is too concentrated. The risk of rivals exploiting the lack of variety while the plant is disrupted is a fatal flaw in the current "big bang" approach.

DECISION: NO-GO
