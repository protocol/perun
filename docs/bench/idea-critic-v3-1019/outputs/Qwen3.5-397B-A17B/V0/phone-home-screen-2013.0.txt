The claim is that a replacement home screen will capture attention without an OS. The steelman: a lightweight launcher leveraging existing social graphs can dominate screen time faster and cheaper than building hardware or an OS.

However, the attack reveals critical fractures. First, the "open mobile operating system" in 2013 (Android) is rapidly standardizing its own launcher experience; fighting the platform holder on their own turf invites relegation or API strangulation. Second, the constraint to "handful of phone models" contradicts the goal of "more people's time." Fragmentation kills network effects; if the partner phone is niche and the launcher incompatible with flagship devices, adoption stalls. Third, the risk of "pushing other apps out of view" is not a bug to users; it is a feature they may reject. Users curate their home screens for utility, not passive consumption. Forcing a walled garden of social posts violates the mental model of a phone as a tool, triggering the "privacy critics" and user churn.

The "do nothing" alternative—optimizing the existing app within the standard OS—offers lower friction, full device compatibility, and no privacy backlash. The proposed path requires users to fundamentally alter their device behavior for a single network, a high-friction ask with limited reversibility once installed (uninstalling launchers is confusing for non-technical users).

The pre-mortem shows failure via low retention: users install out of curiosity, find their utility apps buried, and revert to the stock launcher within 48 hours. The cost of delay is low because the core app already captures attention; forcing a UI layer adds engineering debt without guaranteed incremental gain. The evidence suggests this is a solution in search of a problem, prioritizing "ownership" of the screen over actual user value. The mismatch between the goal (broad attention) and the method (niche hardware + intrusive software) is fatal.

DECISION: NO-GO
