Steelman: Acquiring the British volume maker instantly grants the German premium brand a small-car platform, off-road capability, and UK manufacturing footprint for £800m, bypassing years of internal R&D.

However, the attack on this claim reveals fatal structural flaws. First, the target is a chronic loss-maker with low productivity; injecting capital into inefficient plants without a guaranteed turnaround plan merely burns cash. Second, and most critically, the Japanese engineering partner objects and threatens withdrawal. Since this partner supplies "much" of the engineering, their exit creates an immediate technology vacuum that £800m cannot instantly fill, halting new model development. Third, brand overlap risks cannibalizing the acquirer's existing entry-level sales while diluting the premium image.

The "better way" analysis fails to find a superior alternative within this specific deal structure. Buying a distressed asset with a hostile key supplier and overlapping products violates basic M&A hygiene. The cost of delay (waiting for the Japanese partner to soften or finding a different target) is negligible compared to the cost of acquiring a broken supply chain and unprofitable factories.

Pre-mortem: One year post-acquisition, the Japanese partner withdraws, new models are delayed due to engineering gaps, and the British plants continue losing money despite investment. The German brand's reputation suffers from association with low-quality volume production. The £800m purchase price plus heavy investment commitments creates a massive financial drag with no clear path to profitability.

Kill criteria are missing: There is no defined metric for productivity improvement, no contingency for the Japanese partner's exit, and no cap on the "heavy investment" required. The proposal relies on the assumption that capital alone fixes cultural and engineering deficits, which history contradicts.

The strongest attack survived? None. The dependency on a hostile engineering partner is a single point of failure that invalidates the entire strategic rationale. Without a binding agreement securing the Japanese partner's continued involvement, the acquisition is an unfunded liability, not an asset.

DECISION: NO-GO
