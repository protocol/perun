The claim is that a national launch of fat-free chips using a novel, undigested fat substitute will succeed despite known deterrents. The steelman: first-mover advantage in the "guilt-free" snack category could secure dominant shelf space and brand recognition before competitors react, leveraging heavy advertising to override label concerns.

However, the attack reveals critical fractures. First, the "required label statement about possible effects on the digestive system" is not a minor compliance footnote; in 1998, consumer trust in processed food additives is fragile. Explicitly warning buyers of digestive distress directly contradicts the core value proposition of "feeling good" while dieting. Second, the higher cost structure removes the price-sensitive impulse buy, the primary volume driver for chips. Third, the shift in dieter attention away from pure fat reduction means the product solves a problem consumers are beginning to deprioritize, while introducing a new, visceral risk (gastrointestinal issues).

The "do nothing" or "wait" alternative is stronger. Delaying allows competitors to test the market regionally or lets the company observe if the ingredient gains acceptance in lower-stakes categories before committing to a national, heavy-ad spend launch. A regional pilot would verify if the label actually scares buyers without risking the entire brand equity and marketing budget on an unproven assumption.

The strongest attack survived is the fear of immediate market rejection due to the label. Even with heavy advertising, you cannot buy your way out of a warning label that promises physical discomfort. The cost premium further narrows the margin for error. Without a proven mechanism to neutralize the label's psychological impact or a lower-cost entry strategy, a national launch is a high-bet gamble on a shifting consumer trend with a built-in deterrent. The risk of a high-profile failure, reinforcing consumer group opposition, outweighs the potential first-mover benefit.

DECISION: NO-GO
