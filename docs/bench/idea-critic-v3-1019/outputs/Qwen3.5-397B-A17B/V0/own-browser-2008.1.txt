The claim is that a search company must build its own browser to protect web usage revenue. The steelman: only a proprietary, high-performance browser can break the duopoly of the preinstalled dominant vendor and the funded open-source alternative, securing the search engine's future.

However, the failure hypothesis is severe. The company lacks desktop software DNA; shipping a stable, secure browser at scale is an operational cliff, not just an engineering task. If the product ships with bugs or performance issues, it damages the core search brand rather than saving it. Furthermore, antagonizing the dominant OS vendor risks retaliatory exclusion from their platform, while simultaneously undermining the existing open-source partner, potentially fracturing the only current leverage against the monopoly.

The "do nothing" option retains the status quo revenue without incurring massive fixed costs or channel conflict. A "better way" exists: deepen the investment in the existing open-source partner to accelerate their engine, avoiding the need to build a separate desktop distribution machine. The proposal assumes users will switch for speed alone, ignoring the immense friction of changing default settings and the stickiness of preinstallation.

The cost of delay is real if the dominant vendor locks down the web, but the cost of a failed launch is existential. The proposal lacks a kill criterion for the development phase: if the engine does not beat benchmarks by a specific margin within 12 months, the project should terminate before public embarrassment. Without a proven distribution channel beyond "free download," the user acquisition cost may exceed the lifetime value of the incremental search queries.

The risks of brand damage, partner alienation, and operational overreach outweigh the theoretical benefit of a standalone browser given the company's current capabilities. The proposal should be revised to focus on accelerating the existing open-source partner or acquiring a smaller browser team with proven desktop shipping experience before attempting a ground-up build.

DECISION: NO-GO
