The claim is that a $299–$349 console with a built-in touchscreen, branded as a successor to a hit motion-control system, will reverse sales declines despite weak third-party support and imminent rival launches.

Steelman: A unique hybrid input method (screen + motion) creates a defensible niche for casual and core gamers, leveraging existing brand equity to secure early adoption before competitors react.

However, the attack reveals critical fractures. First, branding ambiguity is fatal: selling under the previous name with a "short suffix" directly invites the confusion that it is merely an accessory, not a new generation. This cannibalizes the installed base without guaranteeing new user acquisition. Second, the hardware strategy is misaligned; matching graphics from six years prior while charging a premium price point offers no value to core gamers, who are the only segment capable of sustaining a platform when casual users migrate to free/cheap mobile apps. Third, the "thin" launch lineup and "lukewarm" publisher sentiment indicate a death spiral: without software, hardware sells poorly; without hardware sales, publishers remain absent.

The "do nothing" alternative (supporting the current console longer) avoids channel confusion. A "cheaper way" would be releasing the screen as a verified accessory first to test demand without the risk of a full console launch. The cost of delay is low because the casual market has already shifted to mobile; rushing a underpowered device into a market about to be flooded by next-gen rivals (PS4/Xbox One era) guarantees obsolescence within 12 months.

The pre-mortem is clear: The console launches, retailers treat it as a peripheral, core gamers reject the outdated graphics, publishers skip it due to low install base, and the brand equity is diluted by a perceived "cash grab" rather than innovation. The kill criterion is the lack of a strong software pipeline; without it, the hardware is a commodity with no differentiator other than a gimmick that the market (mobile) has already solved more cheaply.

This proposal fails to address the core identity crisis, risks brand destruction, and enters a hardware price war with inferior specs.

DECISION: NO-GO
