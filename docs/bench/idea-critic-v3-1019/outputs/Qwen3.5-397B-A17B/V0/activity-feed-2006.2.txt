The proposal to retain the feed while adding privacy controls is the only viable path that balances growth with user trust. A "do nothing" approach ignores the immediate risk of mass exodus driven by negative press and organized protest; in a network effects business, losing critical mass is an existential threat. Conversely, removing the feed entirely surrenders the product's core innovation to rivals and validates the complaint that the feature itself is flawed rather than its default visibility.

The strongest attack on this plan is that offering controls is insufficient because the damage to trust is already done, and users may perceive the apology as insincere or the controls as too complex to manage. If users believe the platform is inherently predatory, granular settings will not stop them from leaving. However, this objection fails because the alternative (removal) confirms the predatory narrative by admitting the feature was a mistake, whereas controls admit a configuration error. The backlash is specifically about *intrusiveness*, which is solvable by shifting agency to the user. The risk of rivals winning members is mitigated only if this platform offers what rivals do not: a dynamic stream that respects boundaries.

The "do nothing" option guarantees continued churn. Removing the feature destroys the product's unique value proposition. The proposed middle path addresses the specific grievance (lack of choice) without abandoning the strategic asset (the feed). The apology acknowledges the misstep in default settings, not the concept itself. This approach turns a privacy violation into a privacy empowerment story, which is a stronger narrative for press coverage than a retreat.

The leading metric to watch is the daily opt-out rate versus the daily churn rate over the next 14 days. If churn remains high despite high opt-out rates, the feature itself is the problem, requiring a pivot. If churn stabilizes as users customize feeds, the strategy holds.
