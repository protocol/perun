**Steelman:** Capturing the premium hub-to-hub market with a 550-seat flagship creates an unassailable brand monopoly and leverages existing congestion to force airport upgrades.

**Attack:** The core premise—that congestion forces consolidation into larger planes—is fragile. If airlines prefer frequency over capacity to serve point-to-point routes, the 550-seat jet becomes a stranded asset. The $10.7 billion bet relies entirely on the "hub-and-spoke" model surviving, while the rival can stretch existing designs for a fraction of the cost. The requirement for gate modifications creates massive friction; airports may refuse upgrades for a single aircraft type, limiting operational flexibility.

**Better Way:** Develop a highly efficient, long-range twin-engine aircraft (250–300 seats). This addresses point-to-point growth, requires no airport changes, offers lower operating costs per seat, and reduces development risk by avoiding the "all-in" $10.7 billion exposure. It allows serving both congested hubs (via frequency) and emerging direct routes.

**Pre-mortem:** In 2030, the program is canceled after 200 units. Why? Fuel prices spiked, making four engines uneconomical. Airlines bypassed hubs entirely. The rival dominated with a flexible, cheaper twin-engine stretch. The specialized airports never materialized globally.

**Kill Criteria:** Proceed only if binding contracts include penalty clauses for airport infrastructure delays and if fuel-efficiency projections remain within 10% of twin-engine alternatives. Without these, the downside is catastrophic.

**Verdict:** The proposal ignores the structural shift toward point-to-point travel and over-relies on a single market segment. It locks the company into a high-cost, low-flexibility position while the rival retains optionality. The risk of creating a "white elephant" fleet is too high given the unverified assumption that hubs will remain the primary growth engine. The capital efficiency of a smaller, flexible aircraft dominates this strategy.

DECISION: NO-GO
