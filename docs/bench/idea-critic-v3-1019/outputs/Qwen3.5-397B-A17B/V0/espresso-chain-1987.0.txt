However, the "better way" analysis reveals fatal flaws in the current plan. First, the "do nothing" alternative is superior to expanding into distant cities immediately; the capital intensity of geographic expansion contradicts the lean footprint of "small bars." Second, the cheapest viable path is to dominate the local region first, proving unit economics before asking investors for expansion capital. Third, the proposal ignores Chesterton's Fence: the existing six stores likely survive on local loyalty, not a scalable national model.

The pre-mortem is clear: the venture fails because it burns investor cash on distant real estate before validating that the general public will pay a premium for espresso over cheap diner coffee. The "one-way door" here is signing long-term leases in new cities; the "two-way door" is testing brand renaming locally. The cost of delay is negligible compared to the cost of premature scaling.

Evidence is limited to the prompt: the buyer is a former director, not a current operator with excess cash. The assumption that investors will fund unproven geographic expansion in 1987 for a niche product is unsupported. The strongest attack survived is the supply-chain advantage of owning the roaster, but this benefit is negated if the retail outlets fail due to overextension.
