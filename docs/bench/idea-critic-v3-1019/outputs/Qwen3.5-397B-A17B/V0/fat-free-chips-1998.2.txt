The claim is that heavy advertising can overcome inherent product disadvantages to achieve a successful national launch. The steelman: a first-mover advantage in fat-free chips, backed by regulatory approval and significant marketing spend, will create a new category leader before competitors react.

However, three fatal flaws exist. First, the "required label statement about possible effects on the digestive system" is not a minor compliance detail; in the food industry, explicit warnings about digestive distress act as immediate purchase blockers, regardless of ad spend. Second, the product costs *more* than regular chips while offering a perceived negative utility (digestive risk) rather than a clear taste benefit, violating basic value propositions. Third, consumer sentiment is already shifting away from single-nutrient fixation (fat) toward holistic health, making a "fat-free" but chemically altered product misaligned with emerging trends.

The "do nothing" or "wait" alternative is superior. Waiting allows competitors to test the waters with smaller batches or lets the market educate itself on the ingredient's reputation without risking a full national rollout. A cheaper path exists: regional pilots to test if advertising can actually override the label warning before committing to national distribution.

The pre-mortem is clear: the launch proceeds, early adopters experience the warned digestive effects, social proof turns negative rapidly, and the brand becomes synonymous with "the chips that make you sick," destroying equity for the parent company. The cost of delay is low compared to the cost of a failed national introduction.

This proposal ignores the primary driver of food adoption: trust and taste. No amount of advertising can sustain a product that consumers are explicitly warned might cause physical discomfort, especially at a price premium. The risks (label fear, activist opposition, price disadvantage, shifting trends) collectively outweigh the unproven benefit of first-mover status in a potentially toxic category.

DECISION: NO-GO
