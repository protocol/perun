**Steelman:** Entering late with a superior product (wider cabin) leverages existing tooling to undercut rivals on comfort while minimizing retooling costs, potentially flipping airline preference despite the delay.

**Attack:** The primary failure mode is not technical but temporal and operational. Being "a year or more behind" in a lock-in prone market means rivals may secure production slots for a decade, rendering a better product unsellable. Furthermore, launching a new airframe while engineering is "stretched by other large programmes" invites catastrophic delays or quality failures, compounding the lateness. The assumption that airlines will wait for a wider cabin ignores the immediate revenue need filled by existing rival orders.

**Better Way:** The "do nothing" or "license/buy" option avoids stretching engineering. Alternatively, a derivative with minimal new tooling (not a new fuselage cross-section, even if reused) might be faster, though the proposal claims cross-section reuse already. However, the core constraint is engineering capacity. Launching now risks delaying the profitable large jets to chase a crowded small-jet market.

**Pre-mortem:** Assume launch slips two years due to staffing shortages. Rivals dominate the short-haul market entirely; the company is left with a superior but irrelevant plane and strained relations with large-jet customers due to delays.

**Verdict:** The proposal fails the "engineering capacity" and "market timing" check. Without a concrete plan to expand staff or delay other programs (not mentioned), and given the high risk of missing the window entirely, the project cannot be approved *as proposed*. It requires a resolved staffing plan and a stronger commitment from launch customers before proceeding. The risk of stretching current resources too thin outweighs the potential benefit of a wider cabin.

DECISION: NO-GO
