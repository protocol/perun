The claim is that a $160 add-on extending a 16-bit console's life with rushed 32-bit graphics will succeed despite a true 32-bit successor arriving in one year.

Steelman: This bridges the generational gap, retaining users and developers who cannot wait 12 months, generating immediate cash flow without delaying the next-gen launch.

However, the attack on this position is severe. First, the "cannibalization" risk is not just a risk; it is a near-certainty. If buyers anticipate a superior, native 32-bit system in only 12 months, they will not spend $160 on a transitional peripheral. They will wait. Second, rushing games for the holidays guarantees a weak launch library. A small library of low-quality titles fails to demonstrate the value of the upgrade, reinforcing the decision to wait. Third, developer fragmentation occurs immediately; studios must choose between optimizing for a crippled add-on architecture or saving resources for the native next-gen hardware. Most will choose the latter, leaving the add-on starved of software.

The "do nothing" alternative preserves brand integrity and focuses all engineering and marketing capital on the inevitable next-gen launch. The cost of delay (waiting 12 months) is lower than the cost of a failed product launch that confuses consumers and dilutes developer trust. The add-on creates a "two-door" problem where the one-way door of brand damage opens if the peripheral fails to gain traction, which is highly probable given the short runway.

The strongest objection is that the market will view the add-on as a "stopgap tax" rather than a value proposition. With only a year until the real solution, the perceived obsolescence of the add-on at the moment of purchase is too high to overcome, regardless of price. The evidence of consumer behavior in transitional tech periods suggests waiting for the native solution is the dominant strategy.

No kill criteria or leading metrics can save a product fundamentally misaligned with consumer incentives to wait. The proposal asks to spend capital to accelerate a transition that customers are willing to pause for, resulting in inventory write-downs and brand confusion.

DECISION: NO-GO
