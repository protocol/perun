The claim is that a 10-inch tablet, priced at $499 and lacking a camera or multitasking, will succeed where previous tablets failed by leveraging an existing phone ecosystem. The steelman argues that prior failures stemmed from poor software and clunky interfaces, not the form factor itself; this device solves the software gap by sharing a mature app store, creating a distinct "third category" between phone and laptop for media consumption and light browsing.

However, the skeptic identifies fatal flaws in the current proposal. First, the price point ($499) is dangerously high compared to "small cheap laptops" that offer full computing power, risking immediate rejection by value-conscious consumers. Second, the explicit lack of a camera and multitasking creates a severe feature deficit against competitors and even the company's own phones, making the "no-compromise" marketing message indefensible. Third, the "big phone" criticism is validated if the device does not offer capabilities distinct from the phone beyond screen size; without multitasking or camera integration, the value proposition collapses to merely a larger display, which history shows is insufficient for mass adoption.

The better-way analysis suggests that launching at $499 without key features invites failure. A cheaper entry point or the inclusion of at least one differentiating hardware feature (like a camera) is necessary to justify the premium over netbooks. The cost of delay is low; waiting to integrate a camera or refine the pricing strategy based on component costs would reduce the risk of a high-profile flop.

The pre-mortem reveals that the product launches to lukewarm reviews citing the missing camera and high price, sales stall after early adopters, and the project is canceled within 18 months, damaging the brand's innovation reputation. The strongest attack survived is the ecosystem argument, but only if the price and feature set align; as proposed, they do not. The proposal fails the fit check against market reality: it asks consumers to pay a premium for fewer features than available alternatives.

DECISION: NO-GO
