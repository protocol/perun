The claim is that a national launch of fat-free chips using an indigestible fat substitute will succeed despite known deterrents. The steelman: first-mover advantage in the diet sector outweighs labeling stigma and higher costs.

However, the attack reveals critical failures. First, the "indigestible" mechanism creates a direct physiological liability. A required warning label about digestive effects is not a minor compliance hurdle; it is a primary purchase barrier that signals immediate discomfort to the consumer. In the food industry, fear of gastrointestinal distress overrides abstract calorie counting. Second, the proposal ignores the "Chesterton's Fence" of consumer trust: active opposition from consumer groups suggests pending reputational damage that heavy advertising cannot easily drown out. Third, the economic model is fragile; charging a premium for a product that promises potential embarrassment, while competitors offer cheap, safe regular chips, creates a narrow path to profitability.

The pre-mortem is clear: six months post-launch, sales collapse as early adopters experience the advertised side effects, generating negative word-of-mouth that no ad budget can fix. The "do nothing" or "wait" alternative is superior: allow competitors to test the water or wait for the ingredient's reputation to stabilize. The cost of delay is low compared to the cost of a failed national rollout that damages the parent company's brand equity.

The proposal assumes dieters prioritize "fat-free" over "feeling well," a dangerous assumption given the explicit regulatory warning. There is no kill criterion defined for early signs of consumer backlash, only a blind push for scale. The risks (health stigma, group opposition, price premium) collectively outweigh the unproven benefit of being first.

DECISION: NO-GO
