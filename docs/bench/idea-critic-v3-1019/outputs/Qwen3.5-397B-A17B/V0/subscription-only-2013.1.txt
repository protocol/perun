The claim is that replacing perpetual licenses with a subscription-only model will secure the company's future despite immediate backlash. The steelman is that recurring revenue creates a predictable valuation multiple and funds continuous innovation, preventing the "feast-or-famine" cycle of major version releases while aligning vendor-customer incentives toward daily utility rather than upgrade marketing.

However, the attack reveals critical fragility. The proposal ignores the "rentership fatigue" of creative professionals who view tools as capital assets, not operating expenses. The risk of users fleeing to cheaper rivals or piracy is not hypothetical; it is a direct consequence of removing the ownership option entirely. The "revenue dip" during transition could be fatal if cash reserves are insufficient to bridge the gap before recurring revenue scales. Furthermore, a "subscription-only" mandate removes the safety valve for price-sensitive users, potentially shrinking the total addressable market rather than expanding it.

A better way exists: a hybrid model. Maintain the perpetual license for legacy users and those demanding ownership, while aggressively incentivizing the subscription tier with exclusive features or cloud services. This captures recurring revenue without forcing a binary choice that alienates the core base. The proposal's "all-or-nothing" approach fails the reversibility test; once trust is broken by removing ownership, it is nearly impossible to restore without significant concession.

The pre-mortem shows a scenario where 20% of the installed base cancels upgrades in protest, causing a 30% revenue drop in Year 1 that triggers layoffs and stalls development, validating the customers' fear of abandonment. The cost of delay is low; waiting to gauge competitor moves or testing a hybrid approach first avoids the irreversible brand damage of a forced migration.

Without a capped pilot, a hybrid alternative, or verified cash-flow modeling to survive the dip, the "subscription-only" mandate is a gamble with the company's survival. The evidence provided highlights loud objections and revenue risks that the proposal dismisses rather than mitigates.

DECISION: NO-GO
