The proposal attempts to retrofit privacy onto a system designed for radical transparency, a classic "bolt-on" failure mode. The core flaw is assuming users want *control* over the feed rather than *absence* of the feed. By keeping the feed as the default and only offering opt-out controls, the platform signals that intrusion is the norm and privacy is a burden the user must manually configure. This validates the protesters' claim that the company views their data as public by default.

The "apology" paired with continued enforcement of the feed appears disingenuous; it admits fault in execution but not in the underlying philosophy that caused the outrage. In 2006, social norms around digital privacy were shifting rapidly from "everything public" to "contextual integrity." Ignoring this shift to preserve engagement metrics risks a mass exodus where the cost of acquisition for new members exceeds the lifetime value of retained ones.

The risk of rivals leveraging this backlash is immediate and existential. A competitor offering "privacy by default" could siphon the most vocal, influential users—the very demographic driving the network's growth—leaving this platform with a stagnant, disengaged user base. The proposal fails to address the "do nothing" alternative (removing the feed entirely) or a "better way" (opt-in feed where users explicitly choose to share activity).

Furthermore, the proposal lacks specific kill criteria. There is no metric defined for when the backlash becomes unsustainable, nor a timeline for reviewing the efficacy of the new controls. Without a "stop" condition, the team may persist with a failing strategy until the brand is irreparably damaged. The assumption that "nothing new is shared" ignores the psychological impact of *visibility*; making past behavior suddenly visible feels like a violation, regardless of technical data reuse.

To survive, the platform must pivot from "control" to "consent." The current plan is a half-measure that satisfies neither the privacy advocates nor the growth team, likely resulting in the worst of both worlds: continued anger and reduced engagement.

DECISION: NO-GO
