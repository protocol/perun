The claim is that a European consortium should enter the 150-seat narrow-body market in 1984 with unproven digital fly-by-wire technology, despite lacking profitability and facing an entrenched American duopoly.

The strongest version of this argument posits that technological discontinuity (fly-by-wire) and commonality (shared cockpit) are the only viable vectors to displace incumbents, as competing on incremental improvements to a mature product line is impossible for a late entrant. Government aid is deemed essential to absorb the catastrophic risk of first-of-kind certification.

However, the failure hypothesis is severe: introducing unproven automation on civil airliners invites immediate regulatory rejection and pilot union hostility, potentially grounding the fleet before entry. The "digital" advantage is theoretical until certified; if regulators demand mechanical backups, the weight and cost benefits vanish. Furthermore, reliance on government aid exposes the project to political volatility, while the incumbent's ability to slash prices could render the new entrant commercially insolvent before reaching break-even volume. The consortium's lack of profitability means a single major delay or certification hurdle triggers insolvency.

Alternatives include licensing existing technology to reduce risk, focusing solely on regional jets where competition is weaker, or waiting for the technology to mature on military platforms first. The "do nothing" option preserves capital but cedes the entire single-aisle market forever.

The cost of delay is high, as the incumbent's model will further entrench its supply chain dominance. Yet, the risk of ruin is higher. The proposal relies on assumptions that regulators will accept unproven digital systems without lengthy, costly validation and that airlines will prioritize cockpit commonality over the reliability track record of the incumbent. Without a guaranteed path to certification that accounts for pilot resistance, the project is a bet on regulatory foresight rather than market demand. The "kill criterion" here is clear: if certification timelines extend beyond 36 months due to regulatory pushback on fly-by-wire, the consortium runs out of cash. Given the binary nature of certification risk and the lack of a proven fallback, the proposal as stated is too fragile.

DECISION: NO-GO
