The proposal attempts to solve financial distress by alienating the existing customer base and ignoring market readiness. The "steelman" argument is that radical simplification reduces costs and signals a future-proof direction, betting on rapid USB adoption. However, the evidence provided contradicts this: few USB peripherals exist, and users cannot connect current printers or drives.

Removing the diskette drive and legacy ports while the ecosystem relies on them creates an immediate usability cliff. A personal computer that cannot transfer files from the user's previous machine or print to their existing hardware is not a "low-priced alternative"; it is a non-starter for the core market. The risk of reviewer backlash is not merely reputational; it will result in immediate sales failure, accelerating the company's financial collapse rather than halting it.

The "do nothing" or "incremental improvement" options are safer. A cheaper version of the existing architecture retains compatibility while addressing the price sensitivity. The proposed "all-in-one" with removed standards is a one-way door; once launched, the incompatibility cannot be patched via software. The cost of delay (waiting for USB peripherals to mature) is lower than the cost of launching a product that fundamentally cannot perform standard tasks.

The kill criterion here is clear: if the device cannot support the user's current workflow on day one, it fails. Given the explicit lack of USB peripherals and the necessity of legacy ports for the target demographic, the proposal fails the basic viability test. The financial constraint mentioned ("little room for a miss") makes this high-risk gamble irresponsible. The company needs a product that sells immediately to stop the bleeding, not a visionary prototype that requires the market to change before it can succeed.

The strongest attack is the incompatibility with existing user assets (printers, drives, data transfer). This objection survives because no amount of "translucent color" or "internet reliance" compensates for the inability to perform local, essential tasks in 1998.

DECISION: NO-GO
