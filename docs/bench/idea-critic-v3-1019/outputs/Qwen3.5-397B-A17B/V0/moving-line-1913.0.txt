The claim is that a moving assembly line will lower unit costs enough to offset conversion expenses, labor turnover, and product rigidity. The steelman: continuous flow eliminates worker travel time, drastically increasing throughput and enabling price cuts that expand the market beyond what variety-focused rivals can reach.

However, critical failures loom. First, the "high volume" requirement is a binary switch; if demand dips even slightly during the disruptive conversion, the fixed cost of the line creates catastrophic losses compared to the flexible static stations. Second, the assumption that lower prices will permanently override the desire for variety is unproven; if buyers tire of the single design, the line becomes a liability that cannot easily pivot to new models. Third, the human factor: extreme repetition may cause turnover rates so high that training costs and quality defects erase the efficiency gains. The proposal lacks a specific kill criterion for turnover rates or a fallback plan if the single-model strategy fails to capture the projected volume.

While the "do nothing" option preserves flexibility and avoids conversion risk, it caps production speed and keeps prices high, limiting market share. A "better way" might be a hybrid approach or a pilot line before rebuilding the whole plant, but the proposal demands an all-or-nothing rebuild. The cost of delay is significant if competitors adopt similar methods first, yet the risk of building a monolithic system for a potentially fickle market is existential.

Without a verified guarantee of sustained high-volume demand for a single design and a concrete plan to mitigate the human cost of repetitive labor, the risk of total operational collapse during the transition is too high. The potential savings are theoretical until the line runs, but the costs are immediate and certain.

DECISION: NO-GO
