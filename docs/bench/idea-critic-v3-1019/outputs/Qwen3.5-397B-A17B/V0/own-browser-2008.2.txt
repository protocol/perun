The proposal argues that a search company must build its own browser to protect its core revenue stream against browser monopolies. The steelman is that owning the distribution layer (the browser) is the only long-term defense for a search business when competitors control the gateway to the web.

However, critical failure modes exist. First, the "separate process per tab" architecture introduces massive stability and memory overhead risks for a company with zero history of shipping complex desktop software; a buggy launch could destroy brand trust instantly. Second, open-sourcing the code while competing directly against the open-source project the company currently funds creates an immediate diplomatic crisis, potentially fracturing the very ecosystem the company relies on for its current market share. Third, the assumption that users will switch browsers ignores the immense friction of changing default settings on pre-installed dominant systems; without a pre-installation deal (which is unlikely given the antagonist relationship), adoption may remain too low to justify the massive engineering cost.

The "do nothing" alternative—doubling down on the existing open-source partnership and improving search quality—is cheaper and avoids alienating allies. A "better way" might be acquiring a smaller existing browser rather than building from scratch, mitigating the "never shipped desktop software" risk.

The pre-mortem reveals a likely scenario: the browser launches, consumes excessive RAM due to the new architecture, crashes frequently on older machines, and the open-source partner forks the code in anger, leaving the company with a niche product, a broken alliance, and no meaningful shift in search market share. The cost of delay is low; the search monopoly is not immediate, but the reputational damage of a failed first software venture is permanent.

There is no evidence provided that the company has the operational maturity to manage a separate release cycle, security patching cadence, and UI design team. The risks of execution failure and ecosystem alienation outweigh the theoretical strategic benefit at this stage.

DECISION: NO-GO
