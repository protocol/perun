The proposal to retain the feed while adding privacy controls is the only viable path to preserve network effects without conceding to demands for total removal. The core failure mode is not the visibility of data, but the lack of user agency. Removing the feed entirely validates the protest narrative that the feature was inherently malicious, potentially triggering a mass exodus of users who value connectivity over strict privacy. Conversely, doing nothing guarantees continued churn and negative press.

The "better way" of deleting the feature ignores that the complaints stem from surprise, not necessarily the data itself. Users often object to default settings rather than the existence of a function. By shifting from an opt-out to an opt-in model for sensitive categories (relationship status, photos) while keeping basic activity visible, the platform addresses the privacy violation claim while maintaining the "always-on" utility that drives engagement. This splits the difference: it appeases the vocal minority demanding control without destroying the product's core value proposition for the silent majority.

The pre-mortem reveals a risk that complex controls will confuse users, leading to false security or continued complaints. However, this is mitigated by simplifying the interface to broad toggles rather than granular per-item settings. The cost of delay is high; every day without a response fuels rival recruitment and solidifies the negative press cycle. A "go-if" condition is unnecessary here because the alternative (removal) poses an existential threat to the platform's growth model, whereas the proposed fix aligns with standard industry evolution toward user-controlled privacy. The strongest attack—that this is merely a appeasement that fails to stop leaks—is countered by the fact that the data was already accessible; the feed only aggregated it. Giving users the switch transfers moral responsibility back to them, defusing the protest's momentum.

DECISION: GO
