The proposal outlines a strategic entry into the 150-seat narrow-body market, currently dominated by established American competitors. The plan relies on introducing unproven digital fly-by-wire technology and side-stick controllers to civil aviation, alongside a common cockpit philosophy. While these innovations promise long-term operational efficiencies and fleet commonality, the immediate risks are prohibitive given the consortium's current financial instability.

The core issue is the introduction of radical, untested technology without prior civil certification history. Pilots and regulators have expressed resistance, creating a significant barrier to entry that could delay certification indefinitely. Furthermore, the development costs are described as large, yet the consortium is not yet profitable. Financing such an ambitious project through government aid does not mitigate the execution risk if the technology faces prolonged regulatory scrutiny or market rejection.

The commercial landscape presents additional dangers. With launch orders secured from only a few airlines, the project lacks a broad base of committed customers. This thin order book leaves the consortium vulnerable to aggressive price-cutting by the incumbent manufacturer, who holds a long-running, proven model. If the incumbent lowers prices, the new entrant may be unable to compete while simultaneously burdened by massive development debts.

Entering a mature market with an unproven product, high fixed costs, no profitability, and limited customer commitment creates an unacceptable probability of financial failure. The technological leap is too great to attempt without a stronger financial foundation or more substantial market validation. The combination of technical uncertainty, regulatory hurdles, and competitive vulnerability suggests that proceeding immediately would jeopardize the entire consortium. The risks outweigh the potential strategic benefits at this specific moment in time.

DECISION: NO-GO
