The proposal addresses a critical existential threat: revenue dependence on web usage controlled by entities with conflicting incentives. Relying on a dominant OS vendor's preinstalled browser and a partner funded by search deals creates a fragile ecosystem. If either partner restricts access or changes terms, the core business collapses. Building an independent browser secures the pipeline for web usage, ensuring long-term viability regardless of partner actions.

The technical specifications cited—a fast JavaScript engine and separate processes per tab—directly address the primary barriers to user adoption: speed and stability. Users rarely switch browsers due to inertia, but they will switch if the alternative offers a significantly superior experience. A faster, more stable browser provides the necessary catalyst to break habitual usage patterns.

While the risks are substantial, they are manageable compared to the risk of inaction. The dominance of the preinstalled browser is a hurdle, not a wall; superior performance can drive organic adoption and IT department interest. Straining the relationship with the current open-source partner is a acceptable trade-off for gaining independence; diversification reduces single-point dependency. The lack of prior experience in shipping desktop software at this scale is a valid concern, yet the open-source model mitigates this by leveraging community testing and development, accelerating iteration and bug fixes beyond internal capabilities.

Releasing the code as open source further lowers adoption friction, builds trust, and aligns with the company's existing brand perception in the developer community. Waiting for market conditions to improve or for partners to guarantee favorable terms is a strategy of surrender. The window to influence the browser landscape is narrowing as the dominant player consolidates power. Immediate action is required to establish a foothold before the market becomes impenetrable. The potential upside of securing the company's future revenue stream vastly outweighs the execution risks and partner friction.

DECISION: GO
