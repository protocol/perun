The company faces an existential threat as its sole successful product, a rear-engined, air-cooled vehicle, sees declining sales while recent alternatives fail to gain traction. Relying on a single aging model during a market contraction is unsustainable. The proposal to introduce a front-engined, front-wheel-drive, water-cooled hatchback directly addresses the shifting landscape created by the oil crisis, where fuel efficiency and modern packaging are becoming paramount. Although the risks are substantial, including high retooling costs amid shrinking profits and a lack of internal experience with this new architecture, the alternative is stagnation and eventual irrelevance.

Loyal customers may resist the change in character, but the broader market is moving away from the old engineering paradigm. Sticking with the legacy design guarantees a slow decline as regulations tighten and consumer preferences shift toward economical, practical vehicles. The involvement of an outside designer offers a fresh perspective necessary to break the company's recent streak of unsuccessful models. While the financial strain of new tooling is severe, it is a required investment to secure the company's future viability. Waiting for conditions to improve is not a strategy, as the oil crisis suggests long-term structural changes in the automotive industry rather than a temporary dip. The lack of internal expertise is a hurdle that must be overcome through this very project, not a reason to avoid it. Failure to innovate now, despite the costs and risks, ensures the brand will disappear when the current flagship finally becomes obsolete. The path forward requires bold action to replace the dying core product with one suited for the new economic reality.
