The proposal outlines a strategic pivot for a search company to secure its revenue base by building its own browser in 2008. The core logic is sound: since revenue relies on web usage, controlling the access point is vital, especially when reliance on a dominant vendor and a single open-source partner creates vulnerability. The technical specifications, including a fast JavaScript engine and process-isolated tabs, directly address performance and stability issues prevalent in existing solutions, offering a tangible user benefit.

However, the risks are substantial. The dominant competitor's preinstallation advantage creates a high barrier to entry. Users historically demonstrate inertia regarding browser switching, making adoption difficult without a compelling catalyst. Furthermore, the company lacks experience shipping desktop software at this scale, introducing significant execution risk. There is also the diplomatic hazard of alienating the current open-source partner funded by search deals, potentially fracturing existing market share before the new product gains traction.

Despite these hurdles, the status quo presents a greater long-term threat. Relying entirely on an operating-system vendor who controls the default experience leaves the search company at the mercy of another entity's strategic whims. If that vendor decides to favor its own search service, the company's primary revenue stream could evaporate overnight. Similarly, depending on a single open-source partner limits leverage. Building a proprietary browser diversifies this risk and establishes a direct channel to users. The technical innovations proposed are not merely incremental; they represent a necessary evolution to make switching worthwhile for users. While execution will be difficult and relationships may strain initially, the necessity of owning the distribution layer outweighs the comfort of the current arrangement. Waiting for market conditions to improve is not a viable strategy when the foundation of the business model is controlled by others. The potential to redefine web performance offers the only realistic path to breaking user inertia.

DECISION: GO
