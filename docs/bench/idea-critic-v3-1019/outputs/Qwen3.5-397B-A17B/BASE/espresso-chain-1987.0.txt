The proposal presents a high-risk venture in 1987, attempting to scale a niche luxury product in a market dominated by cheap drip coffee. The core strategy relies on convincing a mass market to pay several times the standard price for espresso, a behavior not yet established nationally. While the buyer possesses operational experience with small bars, scaling to distant cities introduces massive logistical and capital complexities that differ fundamentally from local management.

The financial structure is precarious. The acquisition requires raising external capital from investors to fund both the purchase of the six-store roaster and the aggressive expansion into new territories. This creates a double burden: servicing debt or equity expectations while simultaneously burning cash on unproven locations. If the niche appeal fails to translate in new cities, the heavy capital outlay for distant stores will likely lead to rapid insolvency before the brand can gain traction.

Furthermore, the plan assumes immediate brand synergy by renaming existing bars, yet it offers no evidence that the local roaster's brand holds sufficient equity to justify national expansion costs. The risk of consumer rejection is compounded by the economic climate of the late 1980s, where discretionary spending on premium beverages was untested at this scale. Without a proven model for multi-city operations or a guaranteed customer base for high-priced drinks, the probability of losing investor capital is unacceptably high. The gap between a few successful local bars and a national chain is too wide to bridge with the current resources and market conditions described. Proceeding now exposes investors to potential total loss without adequate mitigation strategies for market adoption or capital efficiency.

DECISION: NO-GO
