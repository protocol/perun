The proposal addresses a critical market gap where two competitors have already secured a one-year lead in orders for small twin-jets. While the company currently lacks an aircraft for short routes, entering now carries significant risks: engineering resources are already stretched by large programs, initial launch orders are scarce, and major airlines may be locked into rival contracts. However, the technical approach offers a distinct competitive advantage. By reusing the cabin cross-section from existing larger jets, the new aircraft will feature six-abreast seating. This provides a wider cabin than the five-abreast configuration offered by both competitors. In the short-haul segment, passenger comfort and operational commonality with existing fleets are powerful differentiators that can overcome a late entry. The wider fuselage directly addresses a limitation in the rival products, creating a unique value proposition that airlines may prefer despite the delay. Although engineering capacity is tight, leveraging existing designs reduces development time and cost compared to a clean-sheet approach. The risk of competitors locking in customers is real, but the superior product specification could disrupt those early agreements or capture the next wave of fleet renewals. Waiting longer would only increase the competitors' lead without improving the company's position. The strategic benefit of offering a uniquely spacious cabin outweighs the disadvantages of being second to market, provided the execution leverages the shared design elements to mitigate resource strain. The potential to redefine comfort standards in this segment justifies immediate commitment.

DECISION: GO
