The proposal seeks to dominate smartphone attention by replacing the native home and lock screens with a social-centric interface, supported by a partner device. While the strategic intent to bypass OS ownership is clear, the identified risks present immediate, unmitigated barriers to success.

First, user adoption is highly uncertain. The core assumption that users willingly surrender their primary navigation interface to a single social platform contradicts typical behavior regarding device personalization and control. Forcing friends' content to the forefront while obscuring other applications creates a restrictive experience that may drive users away rather than increase engagement.

Second, the technical limitation to only a handful of phone models severely caps potential reach. In a competitive mobile landscape, fragmenting the user base at launch limits network effects and reduces the value proposition for both users and the hardware partner. This narrow compatibility makes the initiative vulnerable to failure if initial sales do not meet aggressive targets.

Third, the privacy concerns regarding "always-on" social software are critical. In an environment where data sensitivity is paramount, launching a product that invites scrutiny from privacy advocates without a stated mitigation strategy exposes the company to reputational damage and regulatory risk. The proposal acknowledges these warnings but offers no specific plans to address them.

Finally, the push to hide other apps disrupts the established ecosystem users rely on. This friction could lead to high uninstall rates and negative sentiment, undermining the goal of capturing more time and attention. Without a clear path to overcoming these substantial hurdles regarding user acceptance, hardware scalability, and privacy trust, proceeding immediately is inadvisable. The risks outweigh the potential rewards in the current configuration.

DECISION: NO-GO
