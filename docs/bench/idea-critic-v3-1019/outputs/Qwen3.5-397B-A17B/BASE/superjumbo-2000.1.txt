The proposal targets a niche segment defined by hub congestion, yet the broader industry trend explicitly favors point-to-point travel using smaller, efficient twin-engine jets. Betting $10.7 billion on a 550-seat double-decker contradicts the observed shift in airline route structures. While 50 firm orders provide an initial foundation, they represent a tiny fraction of the volume needed to recoup such a massive development investment, especially if the total addressable market for very large aircraft remains limited as suspected.

The reliance on significant infrastructure upgrades at airports introduces a critical external dependency. If major hubs cannot or will not modify gates and taxiways quickly enough, the aircraft's operational utility becomes severely restricted, further shrinking its viable market. Additionally, the competitor's potential strategy to stretch their existing jumbo jet offers a lower-risk alternative for airlines needing more capacity, likely eroding demand for an entirely new, larger platform.

Development budget overruns are a noted risk, and given the unprecedented size and complexity of a double-decker design, the probability of exceeding the $10.7 billion estimate is high. With no network or files to validate cost controls or supply chain readiness, the financial exposure is unacceptable based solely on the facts presented. The combination of a shrinking market trend, high infrastructure barriers, aggressive competitor alternatives, and substantial financial risk creates a scenario where the likelihood of commercial failure outweighs the potential reward. The proposal attempts to solve a congestion problem with a solution that requires more space and investment precisely when the market is moving toward flexibility and efficiency.

DECISION: NO-GO
