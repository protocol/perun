**Steelman:** Acquiring the US portal instantly grants critical mass in the world's largest digital market, leveraging the telecom's capital to bypass organic growth barriers and create a dominant global Spanish-English gateway before competitors consolidate.

**Attack:** The proposal fails the "edge" test. The target ranks behind rivals in traffic, meaning the acquirer is purchasing a declining asset at a premium multiple during stock volatility. The stated "global portal" strategy relies on synergies that historically crumble under cross-continental integration complexity. There is no unique asset here that a cheaper, organic market entry or a partnership with a rising niche player could not achieve without the $12.5 billion balance sheet risk. The "fear of falling behind" is not an edge; it is a panic driver.

**Pre-mortem:** One year post-close, integration stalls due to cultural clashes between the telecom and web cultures. The portal's traffic continues to bleed to faster, more innovative rivals (like emerging search specialists). The stock price collapses under the weight of the debt/equity swap, forcing a fire sale of the US assets at a fraction of the purchase price. The core telecom business suffers from distracted management.

**Kill Criteria Check:**
*   **Blocker:** The target is a #2 or #3 player in a winner-take-most market, bought at a peak valuation multiple using volatile stock. This violates basic M&A discipline: never buy a declining leader at a premium with weak currency.
*   **Edge:** NONE. Size and distribution are not edges when the product is losing relevance.
*   **Risk:** Extreme integration risk, overpayment, and market timing error.

**Verdict:** The plan buys obsolescence at a premium. The lack of a defensible competitive advantage (the target is already losing the traffic war) combined with the massive financial exposure creates a fatal flaw. The "global portal" vision is a mirage that ignores the fragmentation of the early web. Do not proceed.

DECISION: NO-GO
