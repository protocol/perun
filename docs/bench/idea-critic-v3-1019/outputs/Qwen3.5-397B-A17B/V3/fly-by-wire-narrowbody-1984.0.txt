**Steelman:** A consortium can break a duopoly by forcing a generational leap in cockpit technology, using state aid to absorb the massive entry cost, and betting that commonality will drive long-term operator loyalty despite initial skepticism.

**Attack:** The core failure mode is market rejection of unproven digital fly-by-wire (FBW) on civil aircraft. If pilots refuse the side-stick or regulators delay certification due to lack of precedent, the value proposition collapses. Furthermore, relying on government aid creates political vulnerability; if the consortium is not yet profitable, any delay in launch orders triggers a liquidity crisis before the first plane is sold. The incumbent's ability to cut prices could render the new entrant's economics impossible before scale is reached.

**Alternatives:**
1.  **Do Nothing:** Avoid catastrophic financial risk but cede the high-volume narrow-body market forever, leaving the consortium dependent on wide-bodies.
2.  **Incremental Upgrade:** Retrofit existing wide-body tech to a smaller frame. This reduces technical risk but fails to offer the operational cost savings (commonality) needed to lure airlines away from the entrenched incumbent.

**Fit:** This moves the north star of breaking the duopoly but assumes the "edge" is technological superiority. However, being first with FBW is a risk, not an edge, unless the performance gain is undeniable. The "edge" here is actually the government's willingness to sustain losses longer than private competitors can survive.

**Pre-mortem:** The project fails because certification takes two years longer than planned due to novel FBW scrutiny. Launch customers defect to the incumbent's discounted legacy models. The consortium runs out of state patience and funding.

**Blockers:** NONE explicitly stated in the text that makes it impossible, but the risk profile is extreme.
**Risks:** High technical novelty, regulatory uncertainty, price war vulnerability, dependency on continuous state aid.
**Edge:** Potential for unmatched fleet commonality and lower long-term operating costs if FBW succeeds.

**Decision Logic:** The proposal addresses a strategic necessity (market entry) with a differentiated product. While risks are severe, the "do nothing" option guarantees strategic irrelevance in the largest market segment. The government backing mitigates the immediate financial blocker. The potential upside of defining the next standard outweighs the risk of being first, provided the kill criteria (certification timeline, launch order count) are strictly monitored.

DECISION: GO
