The claim is that a $160 graphics add-on for an aging 16-bit console can successfully bridge the gap to a next-generation 32-bit system launching in one year, despite rushed software and internal competition.

Steelmanned, this extends the current asset's life and captures holiday revenue without delaying the true successor. However, the proposal fails the blocker test.

**Blockers:**
1.  **Cannibalization with No Upside:** The add-on targets the exact same buyer pool as the upcoming 32-bit console. With the true successor only one year away, rational consumers will either buy the add-on and regret it in 12 months or wait entirely. This splits the developer community between three platforms (16-bit, Add-on, 32-bit), diluting the launch library for the critical next-gen system.
2.  **Price-to-Value Mismatch:** At $160, the add-on approaches the cost of a dedicated entry-level system. Historical data suggests peripheral pricing this high relative to the base console creates friction that kills adoption.
3.  **Rushed Software Risk:** "Rushed" games for a holiday launch directly threaten brand reputation. A small library of low-quality titles confirms consumer bias that the platform is dying, accelerating the exodus to competitors rather than holding the line.

**Edge:** NONE. Being first to market with a half-measure is not an edge when the full measure is imminent. The only advantage is short-term cash flow, which is outweighed by the strategic cost of confusing the market and fragmenting developer support right before the main launch.

**Risks:** Market confusion, developer fatigue, brand damage from poor launch titles, and inventory write-downs if the product flops (which is likely given the wait-for-next-gen logic).

**Kill Criteria:** If the add-on does not secure 5 strong launch titles verified by external studios three months prior to holiday, the project should be killed to focus resources on the 32-bit launch.

The proposal attempts to solve a timing problem with a product that exacerbates strategic fragmentation. The "cheaper path" is to aggressively price the existing 16-bit library and market the upcoming 32-bit system's capabilities now, rather than releasing a stopgap that competes with the future.

DECISION: NO-GO
