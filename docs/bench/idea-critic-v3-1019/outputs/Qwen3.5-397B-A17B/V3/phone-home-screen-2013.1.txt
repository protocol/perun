The claim is that a replacement home screen will capture attention without an OS. The steelman: leveraging existing social gravity to dominate the smartphone entry point is the cheapest path to increased engagement, bypassing the impossibility of building an OS.

However, the proposal fails the blocker test. The primary objection is fundamental user friction: a home screen replacement fights the host OS's native UX and updates, creating a fragile, high-maintenance surface that users often disable when it lags or breaks. The "handful of phone models" constraint is a fatal distribution blocker; without broad hardware coverage, the network effect cannot sustain the development cost, and pre-installing on a single partner phone historically yields negligible market share against entrenched competitors.

The "better way" analysis shows this approach is inferior to simply optimizing the main application for notifications and widgets, which achieves "front and center" visibility without the instability of a full shell replacement. The risk of privacy backlash is not theoretical; an always-on layer reading system state invites regulatory scrutiny that could damage the core brand.

The proposal lacks an edge. Being first to a clunky launcher is not an asset; it is a liability. The "strategic necessity" of owning the screen does not override the fact that users reject software that degrades their primary device experience. The evidence from similar historical attempts suggests high churn and low retention for third-party launchers unless they offer utility beyond content consumption, which this proposal does not.

There is no verified demand for a social-network-only OS layer. The cost of delay is low compared to the cost of shipping a product that users uninstall within weeks. The plan commits real resources to a distribution channel (niche hardware) that cannot scale.

Blockers: Limited hardware reach prevents critical mass; high friction of replacing native OS layers leads to churn.
Risks: Privacy backlash, OS update breakage, brand damage from poor UX.
Edge: NONE. (First-mover status on a bad idea is not an edge).

DECISION: NO-GO
