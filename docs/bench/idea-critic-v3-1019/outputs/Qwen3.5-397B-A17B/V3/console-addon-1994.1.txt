**Steelman:** A $160 add-on extends the 16-bit console's life by one year, capturing holiday revenue with 32-bit graphics while the true successor matures.

**Attack:**
1.  **Cannibalization & Confusion:** The add-on directly competes with the upcoming 32-bit console for the same finite budget of early adopters and developer attention. Releasing a "32-bit" experience now devalues the "true" 32-bit launch in 12 months, training consumers to wait for discounts rather than buy at launch.
2.  **Technical & Schedule Reality:** Rushing games for a holiday launch on a tightly scheduled hardware peripheral almost guarantees a weak library. A 32-bit add-on plugged into a 16-bit bus faces severe bandwidth bottlenecks, likely delivering inferior performance compared to a native 32-bit architecture, damaging the brand's reputation for quality.
3.  **Consumer Behavior:** With a known 12-month horizon to a superior, native system, rational buyers will delay purchase. The add-on becomes a "bridge to nowhere," unsupportable once the new console arrives, leaving early adopters with obsolete, expensive hardware.

**Better Way:** Maintain the 16-bit line with price cuts and strong software support until the native 32-bit console is ready. Do not fragment the developer base or confuse the market narrative six months before a major generational leap.

**Kill Criteria:** If the add-on's projected sales come primarily from users who would have bought the new console within 6 months, the net present value is negative due to brand erosion and support costs.

**Verdict:** The proposal sacrifices the clarity and impact of the next-generation launch for short-term, likely illusory, gains. The risk of market confusion, developer fragmentation, and consumer backlash over a "fake" 32-bit solution outweighs the benefit of a rushed holiday window. The "edge" is non-existent; being first with a compromised 32-bit experience is a disadvantage against a polished native launch.

DECISION: NO-GO
