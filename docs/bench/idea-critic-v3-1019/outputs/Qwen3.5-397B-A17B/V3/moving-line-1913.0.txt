The claim is that replacing stationary assembly with a powered moving line for a single standardized car will lower prices via efficiency gains, despite high conversion costs and labor risks.

**Steelman:** A moving line drastically reduces assembly time per unit, enabling volume high enough to amortize conversion costs and dominate the market through unbeatable pricing, assuming demand for the single model remains elastic.

**Attack & Alternatives:**
1.  **Do Nothing:** Maintain current high-margin, lower-volume production. Safe, but cedes price leadership.
2.  **Partial Automation:** Mechanize only heavy lifting, keeping stations fixed. Lower risk, but captures only a fraction of efficiency gains.
3.  **The Blocker Test:** The proposal hinges on "very high volume of a single model." If buyers tire of one design or if the conversion disruption creates a supply gap rivals exploit, the fixed costs of the new plant become a liability rather than an asset. The risk of "high worker turnover" due to repetitive tasks is not merely a labor issue; it threatens the line's continuity. If the line stops, the high fixed cost burns cash without output.

**Pre-mortem:** The conversion takes too long; competitors flood the market with varied models during the downtime. The company emerges with a faster factory but no customers, as the market rejected the "one design" constraint. The savings are passed to buyers, but the volume required to break even never materializes because the product lacks variety.

**Edge:** The only edge is a cost structure so low that competitors cannot match the price even with varied offerings. However, the proposal admits rivals already offer variety. Without proof that price sensitivity completely overrides desire for variety, the "single model" constraint is a fatal fragility. The plan assumes demand is infinite for this specific cheap car, which is an unsupported assumption. The risk of turnover and the "all eggs in one basket" model design create a scenario where a single shift in consumer preference or a labor strike collapses the entire operation.

There is no verified evidence that the market will accept zero variety in exchange for lower prices at the scale required to justify the massive conversion cost. The "edge" relies entirely on a hypothesis about consumer behavior that contradicts the stated risk that "buyers may tire of one design."

DECISION: NO-GO
