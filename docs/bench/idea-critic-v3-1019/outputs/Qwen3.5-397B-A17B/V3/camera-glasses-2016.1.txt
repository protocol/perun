**Steelman:** Leveraging an existing photo-messaging user base to capture first-person content via dedicated hardware could deepen engagement and create a new revenue stream, provided the novelty sustains and privacy hurdles are managed.

**Attack & Analysis:**
The proposal fails the "edge" test. The company's core competency is software; it has zero verified experience in hardware manufacturing, supply chain management, or retail distribution. Entering hardware requires massive upfront capital ($130 unit cost implies thin margins at scale) for a device with a single, non-essential function.

The "better way" analysis strongly favors **doing nothing** regarding hardware. Users already possess high-quality cameras in their pockets. Adding a $130 friction point (pairing, charging, wearing specific glasses) to solve a problem that doesn't exist (inability to take photos) is a classic value-destructive expansion. The reliance on "novelty-driven demand" is a recognized blocker, not a risk; novelty fades, leaving expensive inventory.

Privacy concerns are not merely reputational risks but potential regulatory blockers that could ban the product entirely in key markets, rendering the manufacturing investment a total loss. The pop-up vending machine strategy does not mitigate the fundamental flaw: there is no evidence users want *this* specific hardware from *this* specific software company. The proposal admits the company "has never made hardware," yet plans to immediately scale to "wide retail sale." This ignores the "valley of death" in hardware startups where first-generation units often fail quality or cost targets.

**Blockers:**
1.  **No Edge:** The device offers no advantage over the smartphone already required to view the content.
2.  **Capability Gap:** Zero internal experience in hardware manufacturing or retail logistics.
3.  **Demand Flaw:** Reliance on fading novelty rather than sustained utility.

**Risks:** Privacy backlash, inventory obsolescence, brand dilution.

**Conclusion:** The plan attempts to fix a non-existent problem with a capability the company lacks, using a business model (hardware) fundamentally opposed to its software DNA. The cost of delay is negligible compared to the cost of failure.

DECISION: NO-GO
