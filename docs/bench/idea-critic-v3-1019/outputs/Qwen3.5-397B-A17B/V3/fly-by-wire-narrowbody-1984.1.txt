The proposal attempts to disrupt a duopoly with unproven technology and fragile economics. The steelman is that digital fly-by-wire and commonality will drastically reduce long-term operating costs and training expenses, creating a superior product that justifies market entry despite high initial risks.

However, the skeptic hat identifies fatal blockers in the current facts. First, the core differentiator—civil fly-by-wire—is unproven. In aviation, "first-to-market" with a fundamental control change is often a path to catastrophe or regulatory paralysis, not dominance. Pilot and regulator resistance is not a mere risk; it is a probable adoption barrier that could ground the fleet or limit its operational utility, negating the efficiency advantage.

Second, the financial structure is unsustainable. The consortium is not yet profitable, yet the development costs for a clean-sheet design are enormous. Relying on government aid introduces political volatility; if administration changes or budgets tighten, the project collapses mid-development. The incumbent's ability to cut prices creates a margin war the consortium cannot win without deep, guaranteed pockets, which aid does not fully secure.

The "better-way" analysis suggests that entering a mature market with a "me-too" capacity but unproven controls is inferior to waiting for the technology to mature elsewhere or focusing on wide-body dominance where they already lead. The "do nothing" option preserves capital and avoids the high probability of a catastrophic failure that could bankrupt the entire consortium.

The pre-mortem is clear: The aircraft is delayed by certification hurdles regarding fly-by-wire, launch customers defect to the incumbent due to price cuts, and the consortium runs out of cash before delivering the first unit. There is no stated edge here other than "new technology," which is currently a liability, not an asset. Being first with unproven flight controls is not a competitive advantage; it is a gamble with existential stakes. The facts provided show no route to profitability that survives a price war or a certification delay.

DECISION: NO-GO
