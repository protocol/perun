The proposal to retain the feed while adding privacy controls is the only viable path to preserve network effects while addressing the immediate backlash. The core failure mode is not the existence of activity data, but the lack of user agency over its visibility. Removing the feed entirely ("do nothing" regarding the new feature) cedes the product's primary engagement driver to rivals and validates the complaint that the feature itself is inherently wrong, rather than its implementation.

The strongest attack against this plan is that privacy controls are too complex for the average student user or that the apology will be viewed as insincere, failing to stop member churn. However, the alternative—removing the feature—guarantees a loss of the "sticky" engagement that defines the platform's growth, effectively killing the product's unique value proposition. The risk of members leaving due to complexity is lower than the certainty of them leaving due to a barren home page. The "edge" here is speed: implementing controls faster than rivals can exploit the vacuum left by a full retraction.

The complaints indicate a demand for control, not necessarily the absence of sharing. By apologizing and pivoting to user-controlled visibility, the platform transforms a privacy violation into a privacy empowerment tool. This addresses the "intrusive" claim directly without sacrificing the utility of the stream. The negative press is mitigated by a tangible, user-centric fix rather than a retreat. Rivals cannot easily win members if the platform resolves the specific grievance (lack of choice) while maintaining the feature they lack.

The kill criterion is clear: if privacy controls do not reduce protest group activity by 50% within 30 days, the feature must be re-evaluated for removal. Until then, the cost of delay (continued churn and brand damage) outweighs the engineering cost of adding toggles. The plan beats "do nothing" (status quo outrage) and "full removal" (loss of engagement).
