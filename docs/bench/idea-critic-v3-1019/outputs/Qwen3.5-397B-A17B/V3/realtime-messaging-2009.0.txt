The claim is that launching a confused, slow, non-interoperable tool to 100,000 users will successfully replace email. The steelman: a massive preview creates enough density to solve the network effect before performance kills retention.

However, the facts provided constitute immediate blockers. First, the value proposition is undefined ("unsure what it is for") and the interface is confusing. Launching to 100,000 users in this state guarantees mass churn and reputational damage; users cannot adopt what they do not understand. Second, the utility is explicitly contingent on contacts also using it ("useful only if..."), yet invitations are already scarce. Flooding the market with confused users who cannot find anyone to talk to accelerates failure rather than solving the cold-start problem. Third, the product runs slowly in current browsers. In 2009, browser performance is the primary constraint for web apps; a slow real-time editor is unusable regardless of features. Finally, lacking email interoperability while attempting to replace email cuts off the only viable migration path, forcing an impossible "all-or-nothing" switch for users.

The "do nothing" alternative (fix core usability, speed, and clarity before scaling) offers a higher probability of success. A cheaper, safer alternative is a closed beta of 1,000 users to resolve the identity crisis and performance bottlenecks before inviting 100,000. The proposal fails the blocker test: the stated facts (confusion, slowness, isolation) directly contradict the core claim that expansion will work. There is no edge; being first with a broken, undefined product is not an advantage. The risks are not hypothetical; they are observed behaviors in the early user set. Scaling a broken product does not fix it; it amplifies the failure.

Blockers: Confused users, scarce network effects, poor performance, no interoperability.
Risks: Total brand damage, wasted infrastructure costs.
Edge: NONE.

DECISION: NO-GO
