The claim is that launching two social-networking phones on a new, app-less OS via a single carrier with a high-cost data plan will succeed with teenagers in 2010. The steelman argues that leveraging an acquired messaging brand and exclusive carrier distribution creates a focused, controlled ecosystem that bypasses the complexity of a full app store initially.

However, the attack reveals fatal blockers. First, the target demographic (teenagers) is highly price-sensitive, yet the proposal mandates a data plan costing as much as a full smartphone plan. This pricing structure directly contradicts the financial reality of the core user base. Second, the competitive landscape in 2010 is defined by rich application ecosystems; launching with "no application store" while rivals offer thousands of apps creates an immediate utility gap that social networking alone cannot bridge. Third, building a new in-house operating system ignores the company's existing mobile platform, incurring unnecessary development risk and fragmentation when speed to market is critical.

The "sunk cost" of two years of development is a fallacy; continuing to invest in a flawed product because money was already spent guarantees further loss. The cheaper, safer alternative is to port the messaging features to the company's existing, proven mobile platform and partner with multiple carriers to offer flexible, lower-cost data options.

The strongest attack survived testing is the assumption that teenagers will pay a premium for a limited device. Evidence from the proposal itself (high data cost relative to teen budgets) confirms this assumption is false. The plan fails its own logic: it targets a budget-conscious group with a premium-priced, feature-poor product in a market where features drive adoption.

Blockers:
1. Pricing mismatch: Data plan cost exceeds typical teen budget for mobile services.
2. Feature deficit: No app store at launch vs. rival catalogs creates non-starter utility gap.
3. Strategic drift: New OS ignores existing assets, increasing risk and time-to-market.

Risks: Carrier exclusivity limits reach; brand dilution if the new OS fails.

DECISION: NO-GO
