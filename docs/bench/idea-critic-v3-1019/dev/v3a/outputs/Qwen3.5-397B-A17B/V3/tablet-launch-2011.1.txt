The claim is that a computer manufacturer can successfully launch a tablet in 2011 using an acquired mobile operating system, priced at parity with the market leader, despite having a tiny app catalog and negligible OS market share.

Steelman: The company leverages its hardware brand loyalty and aggressive marketing to overcome the software deficit, betting that developer outreach will rapidly close the app gap before competitors react.

However, critical blockers exist. First, pricing a device with a "small fraction" of the leading ecosystem's apps at the same price point ignores the primary value driver for tablet buyers: software availability. Consumers will not pay a premium for unproven hardware with limited utility. Second, the operating system has "almost no market share," creating a chicken-and-egg problem where developers won't build for a niche platform, and users won't buy a device without apps. Heavy advertising cannot fix a fundamental value mismatch; it only accelerates the realization of the product's limitations. Third, leadership transition introduces execution risk precisely when steady strategic focus is required to nurture a fragile ecosystem.

The "do nothing" or "wait" alternative is superior. Entering a dominated market with a disadvantaged product during internal instability is likely to result in significant financial loss and brand damage. A cheaper, safer path would be to license a proven OS or price the device significantly lower to penetrate the market, but the proposal explicitly rejects these by demanding price parity and using the acquired OS.

The pre-mortem is clear: the device launches, sales flop due to lack of apps, developers abandon the platform, and the company writes off the acquisition. The cost of delay is minimal compared to the cost of a failed launch. The evidence provided (tiny catalog, no market share, leadership flux) directly contradicts the requirement for success in a network-effect-driven market.

Blockers: 1) Price parity with inferior software value proposition. 2) Negligible OS market share preventing developer adoption. 3) Leadership instability during critical launch phase.

DECISION: NO-GO
