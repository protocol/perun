The claim is that replacing a coupon-driven model with everyday low pricing (EDLP) and branded shops will reverse falling sales for a mid-market department store chain. The steelman: eliminating discount complexity restores margin and modernizes the brand, leveraging the CEO's prior success in electronics.

However, the skeptic hat identifies a fatal misalignment. The proposal admits most sales historically come from discounted items and the customer base is "older" and "coupon-driven." These customers are trained to wait for promotions; removing coupons does not change their behavior to value EDLP, it simply removes the trigger for their purchase. In electronics, brands drive traffic; in mid-market apparel/home goods, price sensitivity often dominates. The CEO's past success in a different category (branded electronics) creates a dangerous false analogy here.

The "better-way" analysis shows the "do nothing" or "gradual transition" options are superior. A full chain-wide rollout without a pilot ignores the massive risk of alienating the core demographic immediately. If the hypothesis is wrong, the chain loses its entire revenue base overnight with no off-ramp. A cheaper, safer alternative is a limited pilot in 5–10 stores to validate if the specific demographic will abandon coupon-waiting behavior. The cost of delay (waiting a quarter for pilot data) is negligible compared to the cost of a failed national rollout (potential bankruptcy).

The pre-mortem is clear: six months post-launch, sales collapse because loyal customers refuse to pay full price, and new customers do not arrive because the brand lacks the cachet of the electronics chain. This is a one-way door; once trust in "sales" is broken, it is hard to restore.

This fails the blocker test. The stated fact that "most sales have historically come from items sold at a discount" directly contradicts the core mechanism of the plan (that customers will buy at full price). There is no evidence the customer base wants this, and the lack of a pilot makes the risk unmanaged. The plan assumes a customer behavior shift without proof.

Blockers: The core revenue source (discount shoppers) is explicitly targeted for removal without evidence they will convert to full-price buyers.
Risks: Total revenue collapse, brand irrelevance, inability to revert.

DECISION: NO-GO
