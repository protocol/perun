The proposal commits $1 billion to build highly automated warehouses and a truck fleet across a dozen cities before validating unit economics in the single launch market. This ignores the fundamental constraint: the business has no operating track record, and groceries are notoriously low-margin with extreme sensitivity to customer density and logistics efficiency.

The steelman argument is that speed creates an unassailable moat before competitors react. However, the pre-mortem reveals a near-certain failure mode: if the first city's unit economics prove negative due to low density or high fulfillment costs (highly probable given the 30-minute slot promise), the company will be locked into billions of dollars of fixed, specialized infrastructure in eleven other cities. Unlike software, these warehouses cannot be pivoted; they are sunk costs that will bleed cash daily.

The "better way" is clear: operate the first city using manual picking or third-party logistics to prove the model, margins, and optimal warehouse design before committing to automation. This preserves capital and allows the business to learn the real constraints of grocery delivery. The current plan bets the entire company on an unproven hypothesis being correct on the first try, across multiple markets simultaneously.

The risks cited (capital intensity, thin margins, density needs) are not just risks; they are active blockers when combined with the scale of this commitment. A $1 billion outlay without a single quarter of proven positive unit economics violates basic capital allocation hygiene. The contradiction is stark: the proposal admits "no operating track record" yet acts with the certainty of a proven winner. There is no kill criterion here because the failure condition is total insolvency before learning begins.

This is not a calculated risk; it is a refusal to learn. The cost of delay (waiting to validate the first city) is negligible compared to the cost of building the wrong infrastructure at scale.

DECISION: NO-GO
