The claim is that a $700 pen-based handheld with unreliable handwriting recognition can successfully launch a new category via CEO publicity. Steelman: Early market entry with bold marketing can establish a category standard before technical perfection, leveraging first-mover advantage to attract developers despite initial flaws.

However, the attack reveals fatal blockers. First, the core value proposition (handwriting recognition) is admitted as "unreliable in testing." For a primary input method, this is not a risk but a functional failure; users cannot adopt a device that fails its main task. Second, the price ($700 in 1993) is prohibitively high for an unproven category with "little software," creating a negative feedback loop: high cost limits adoption, low adoption discourages developers, and lack of software justifies neither the cost nor the bulk. Third, the device is "too big for a pocket," violating the fundamental utility of a "handheld" or "personal" digital assistant. Publicity cannot sustain a product that physically fails its form factor and functionally fails its primary interface.

The "do nothing" alternative avoids sinking capital into a device likely to be rejected for poor usability and high cost. A "better way" would be to delay launch until recognition reliability meets a minimum viable threshold, reduce size to fit pockets, or lower the price point to reduce adoption friction. The current proposal relies on hype to override product deficiencies, a strategy that historically leads to high-profile failures when the product experience does not match the marketing promise. The risks are not merely "maybes"; they are confirmed defects in the current prototype state.

Blockers: Unreliable primary input method; non-portable form factor; prohibitive price for an ecosystem with no software.
Risks: Category rejection; developer abandonment; brand damage from over-promising.

DECISION: NO-GO
