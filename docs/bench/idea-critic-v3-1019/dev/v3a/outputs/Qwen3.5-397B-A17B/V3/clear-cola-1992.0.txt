The claim is that a clear cola, successful in test markets, should launch nationwide immediately with a major campaign. The steelman: clarity signals purity to a specific segment, creating incremental revenue that outweighs cannibalization and marketing costs before competitors react.

However, critical blockers exist within the provided facts. First, the core product hypothesis conflicts with established consumer psychology noted in the risks: shoppers explicitly link cola flavor with dark color. If the sensory expectation fails upon tasting, repeat purchase—the only metric that sustains a national brand—will collapse regardless of initial novelty. Second, the proposal ignores the "test markets can overstate demand" risk. Test markets often attract novelty seekers who do not represent the broader, repeat-buying population required for a nationwide rollout. Launching with a "large marketing campaign" amplifies this error, locking in high fixed costs for a product that may have zero retention.

The "better way" is a staged rollout. Instead of a nationwide launch, the company should expand to limited regions to verify repeat purchase rates and measure actual cannibalization against the flagship. This approach treats the "novelty vs. repeat" risk as a unknown variable rather than assuming success. A nationwide launch is a one-way door with high sunk costs; a regional expansion is a two-way door allowing for pivot or kill based on real sales data.

The pre-mortem is clear: the product launches nationally, initial sales spike due to curiosity, then plummet as consumers reject the taste-profile mismatch. The massive marketing spend yields no long-term equity, and the flagship brand suffers unnecessary erosion. The current proposal lacks a mechanism to validate the "repeat" assumption before committing to the full campaign.
