**Steelman:** Embedding general-purpose compute capabilities into mass-market GPUs now creates a foundational platform for future scientific and AI workloads, securing a first-mover advantage before rivals recognize the shift from fixed-function graphics to parallel computing.

**Attack & Alternatives:**
The primary blocker is economic: raising unit costs on every consumer chip without a committed paying market violates basic capital allocation principles during a fierce competitive race. The "niche" academic demand, currently met by hacking existing APIs, does not justify the massive CAPEX of a new compiler stack and dedicated silicon area. The superior alternative is "do nothing" regarding dedicated silicon; instead, wait for software demand to prove willingness to pay, then add features incrementally. Betting the core gaming margin on a hypothetical future market distracts engineering from the immediate existential threat: losing the graphics race to the main rival.

**Pre-mortem:**
Assume failure in 2007: The company burns hundreds of millions on toolchains and silicon area. Gamers see no benefit, only potential price hikes or lower performance-per-dollar in core graphics. The rival, focusing strictly on graphics performance, captures the gaming market share. The scientific community remains small, unwilling to subsidize the hardware, and the company runs out of cash to compete in its primary revenue stream.

**Verdict:**
This is a classic "one-way door" with high irreversibility (silicon tape-out) and zero current revenue validation. The proposal asks to subsidize a non-existent customer base using profits from a different, highly contested market. While the long-term vision of GPU computing is historically sound, the 2004 timing lacks the necessary pull signals (e.g., deep learning breakthroughs requiring this specific architecture) to justify the risk. The cost on every unit is a definitive blocker given the lack of committed customers. The plan fails the "beat do nothing" test because the cheaper, safer path is to let academics continue hacking APIs until a clear commercial use case forces a targeted response.

Blockers: Raising unit costs on all units with no committed customers; engineering distraction from core survival race.
Risks: Market never materializes; rival wins graphics dominance; cash burn cripples operations.

DECISION: NO-GO
