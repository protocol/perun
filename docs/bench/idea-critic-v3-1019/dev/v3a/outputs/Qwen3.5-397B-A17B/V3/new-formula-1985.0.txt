The claim is that replacing the flagship formula with a sweeter alternative, validated by blind taste tests, will reverse market share loss. The steelman: sensory superiority drives purchase, and removing the old formula forces migration to the superior product, maximizing efficiency.

However, the attack reveals a fatal blocker. The evidence cited (blind taste tests, surveys, focus groups) explicitly excluded the critical variable: the permanent removal of the original option. Taste is not the sole driver; emotional attachment, brand heritage, and the psychological reactance of losing a cultural icon are untested. The proposal assumes a "two-way door" (reversible) or simple substitution, but withdrawing the original creates a "one-way door" with immediate, high-severity backlash potential. The risks listed (emotional attachment, no fallback) are not merely possibilities; they are direct contradictions to the testing methodology. The data proves the new liquid tastes better in isolation, but it provides zero evidence that consumers will accept the *loss* of the old one. In fact, the lack of a fallback product violates basic portfolio risk management for a market leader.

A better way exists: launch the sweeter formula as a new variant alongside the original (a "New Coke" plus "Classic" strategy), allowing market forces to validate the taste preference without triggering the loss-aversion trigger. This captures the upside of the new taste while mitigating the existential risk of alienating the core base. The current plan bets the entire brand equity on a metric (blind taste) that ignores the actual purchase context (brand loyalty and availability).

The pre-mortem is clear: loyalists revolt, media narrative shifts from "taste improvement" to "corporate arrogance," and sales plummet not because the drink tastes bad, but because the choice was stolen. The cost of delay is negligible compared to the cost of a botched launch with no rollback path. Since the testing did not cover the core mechanism of the change (withdrawal), the evidence does not support the conclusion.

Blockers: The testing methodology fundamentally fails to account for the reaction to removing the original product, which is the central action of the proposal.
Risks: Total brand alienation, bottler rebellion, no recovery path.

DECISION: NO-GO
