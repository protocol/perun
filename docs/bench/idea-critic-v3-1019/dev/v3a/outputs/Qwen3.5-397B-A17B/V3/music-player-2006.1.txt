**Steelman:** A well-funded entrant can disrupt a complacent monopoly by offering superior hardware (screen, wireless sharing) and a unified store/subscription model, leveraging brand strength to overcome late entry.

**Attack:** The proposal contains a fatal internal contradiction. It explicitly states the new device will "drop the copy-protection standard that the company had promoted to partner stores and device makers." In 2006, major record labels mandated strict DRM (Digital Rights Management) as a precondition for licensing content. By abandoning the very standard the company previously championed to secure industry alliances, the proposal guarantees that labels will refuse to license music for the new store. Without licensed content, the store fails; without a store, the hardware is merely an expensive MP3 player with no ecosystem. The "large marketing budget" cannot sell a device with no music.

**Alternatives:**
1.  **Do Nothing:** Avoids alienating existing partners and wasting capital on a non-compliant product.
2.  **Compliant Entry:** Launch a player/store adhering to the established DRM standard to secure content licenses first, competing on hardware quality alone.
3.  **Software Only:** Launch a desktop client for existing hardware, bypassing the risk of manufacturing a new device before securing content rights.

**Fit & Pre-mortem:** The strategy assumes labels will relax restrictions for a new entrant despite the removal of their required security layer. History suggests the opposite: they will tighten control. The "wireless sharing" feature likely triggers immediate legal threats from rights holders if DRM is absent. The cost of delay is low because the current market leader is vulnerable only if they lose content exclusivity or hardware superiority, not if they remain the only legal option.

**Blockers:**
*   **Content Licensing:** The proposal admits to dropping the industry-standard copy protection. This is a verified fact within the text that directly prevents the core value proposition (a music store) from functioning. No amount of marketing solves a zero-inventory problem caused by legal non-compliance.

**Risks:** Late market entry, high customer acquisition costs, potential hardware margin compression.

**Verdict:** The plan relies on an assumption (labels will license without DRM) that contradicts the stated action (dropping DRM) and the known constraints of the 2006 music industry. The strongest attack—that the product cannot legally contain music—survives because the proposal offers no strategy to secure licenses without the promised DRM.

DECISION: NO-GO
