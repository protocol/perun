The claim is that replacing the flagship formula with a sweeter alternative, validated by blind taste tests, will reverse market share loss. The steelman: sensory superiority drives purchase frequency, and removing the old formula forces migration to the superior product, maximizing efficiency.

However, the attack reveals a fatal flaw in the evidence. The blind taste tests isolated flavor from brand identity, measuring only immediate sensory reaction (a "sip" test) rather than long-term consumption satisfaction or brand loyalty. By withholding the fact that the original would be removed, the research failed to measure the psychological cost of loss. The proposal ignores the "endowment effect," where consumers value what they already possess more than an objectively "better" alternative.

The "do nothing" option (keeping the original while perhaps introducing the new one as a variant) carries lower risk and preserves the heritage asset. The proposed path is a one-way door with no fallback; once the original formula is destroyed and production lines retooled, reversal is costly and humiliating. The risks identified—emotional attachment and heritage—are not merely "risks" but blockers because the testing methodology explicitly excluded them from the decision criteria. A product that wins in a blind sip test can lose in the real world where brand narrative dictates value.

The pre-mortem is clear: launching this change triggers a backlash framed as corporate arrogance erasing history, not improving taste. The lack of a fallback product means any negative reaction results in immediate sales collapse with no bridge back to the status quo. The testing data contradicts the core claim that "preference" equals "willingness to accept replacement." Since the evidence provided (blind tests) actively filters out the primary driver of this specific brand's value (heritage/emotion), the plan fails the blocker test. It substitutes a partial metric for total market reality.

Blockers: The research methodology excluded the variable of brand loss, rendering the "preference" data invalid for a replacement strategy.
Risks: Total alienation of the core base, irreversible damage to brand equity, no operational fallback.

DECISION: NO-GO
