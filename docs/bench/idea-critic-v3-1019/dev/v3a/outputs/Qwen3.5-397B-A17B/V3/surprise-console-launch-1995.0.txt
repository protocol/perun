The proposal to launch four months early in May 1995 at $399 fails the blocker test. The primary failure mode is a self-inflicted ecosystem collapse. By shipping with "few games ready" and excluding major retailers, the console launches with no software library and no distribution network. In the video game industry, hardware is a loss-leader for software; without titles, the hardware is unsellable regardless of price.

The $100 price premium over the competitor is unjustifiable given the inferior launch state. A higher price requires a superior value proposition (more games, better availability), yet this plan delivers the opposite. The "hard to program for 3D" constraint means developers cannot magically produce a library in four months; forcing an early date alienates the very partners needed to make the console viable.

Retailer retaliation is not merely a "risk" but a near-certainty that becomes a blocker. Excluding key channels at launch cedes shelf space and mindshare to the competitor permanently. Once the competitor secures exclusive deals or prime positioning in response to this slight, re-entering the market becomes exponentially more expensive or impossible.

The steelman argument—that early entry captures mindshare—collapses because mindshare in gaming is driven by playable hits, not announcement dates. Launching a barren console generates negative word-of-mouth that poisons the brand before the full library arrives. The "first mover" advantage here is actually a "first failure" guarantee.

There is no kill criterion that saves this specific May plan because the core constraints (no games, angry retailers, high price) are baked into the proposal's definition. The only viable path is to wait for the software lineup to mature and secure full retail distribution, even if it means launching alongside the competitor. Launching early with a broken value proposition guarantees market exit.

DECISION: NO-GO
