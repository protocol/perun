The claim is that acquiring the studio for $7.4B is the only viable path to secure its creative output and leadership. The steelman: immediate ownership prevents a rival partnership, installs a proven hit-making engine to replace a struggling internal unit, and aligns incentives via stock.

However, the "better-way" analysis reveals a critical flaw in the "buy outright" mandate. The primary risk is dependence on a few creative leaders and a single founder who would become a major shareholder. If the value resides entirely in these individuals, an all-stock acquisition at a massive premium ($7.4B for ~1 film/year) creates extreme concentration risk without guaranteed retention. A cheaper, safer alternative exists: a long-term, exclusive distribution and co-financing agreement with profit participation. This secures the pipeline and economic upside without the cultural integration hazard, the balance sheet shock, or the dilution of paying a 20x+ multiple for human capital that can walk away.

The pre-mortem shows failure occurring when the creative leaders chafe under conglomerate oversight, the founder uses their new shareholder leverage to block strategic shifts, and the stock price collapses as the "one film a year" cadence fails to justify the acquisition premium. The cost of delay is low; the partnership expires soon, but a bridge deal can be struck while negotiating a lighter-touch commercial alliance rather than a full merger.

The blocker here is the valuation relative to output velocity and the unmitigated risk of key-person dependency. The proposal assumes ownership equals control of creativity, which history suggests is false in animation. The plan fails the "cheaper alternative" test because a commercial partnership achieves the core goal (access to hits) without the catastrophic downside of a failed integration. The $7.4B price tag is a fact-based blocker given the current production rate and the availability of non-equity structures.

DECISION: NO-GO
