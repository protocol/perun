The claim is that launching the world's cheapest car at $2,500 from a new factory will successfully transition scooter families to cars. The steelman: extreme affordability unlocks a massive, untapped market segment, achieving volume through dedicated high-efficiency manufacturing.

However, critical blockers exist in the proposal's own facts. First, the "cheapest" positioning creates a fatal perception trap: buyers may equate low price with unsafe or inferior quality, directly contradicting the need to attract families concerned with safety. Second, the factory site faces active farmer protests; building a high-volume dedicated plant on contested land introduces severe timeline and operational uncertainty that threatens the entire cost structure. Third, the alternatives—scooters and used cars—are not just cheaper but proven; a new, unproven brand known only for being "the cheapest" offers no compelling status or reliability advantage to justify switching.

The "better way" analysis suggests a staged approach: validate demand and quality perception with a pilot run in an existing facility before committing to a controversial new factory. The cost of delay is low compared to the risk of building a stranded asset on protested land. The pre-mortem reveals failure via reputational damage from safety incidents or production halts due to land disputes.

The strongest attack is the brand positioning: marketing explicitly as "the cheapest" invites scrutiny that a basic, cost-cut vehicle cannot withstand, likely depressing demand among the very families it targets who prioritize safety over marginal cost savings. This contradicts the core revenue assumption. Without resolving the land conflict and repositioning the product away from "cheapest" toward "safe and accessible," the plan fails.

Blockers: 1) Land protests threaten factory viability. 2) "Cheapest" branding undermines safety perception required for family buyers.
Risks: Quality doubts, stronger used car competition.

DECISION: NO-GO
