The claim is that an untested, surprise logo overhaul will modernize a mature brand without triggering destructive backlash. The steelman: a clean break signals contemporary intent faster than evolution, and silence prevents pre-launch negativity.

However, the proposal fails the blocker test. It explicitly admits the core asset (the blue square) drives recognition, yet plans to diminish it while removing the familiar typography loyalists value. Simultaneously, it acknowledges the risk of instant social media backlash but chooses a "no advance announcement" strategy that guarantees the reveal will be chaotic rather than managed. Most critically, the design has **not been tested with customers**. For a mature retailer, alienating the existing base to chase a vague "contemporary" feel is a one-way door with no safety net.

The "do nothing" or "staged pilot" alternatives are superior. A staged rollout (e.g., online first or specific regions) allows for backlash measurement before committing to a full channel sweep. Testing the new design against the old with a customer panel is a low-cost step that directly addresses the "untested" flaw. Proceeding without data assumes the executive team's taste overrides market reality, a dangerous assumption in fashion.

The pre-mortem is clear: Launch day brings immediate social media ridicule and confusion; loyal customers feel alienated by the loss of the iconic square; sales dip as brand recognition fractures; the company is forced to revert, having paid for a full rebrand that damaged equity. The cost of delay (waiting to test) is negligible compared to the cost of a botched global launch.

This is not a matter of opinion but of verified risk management. You cannot fix a recognition problem by removing the recognized element without proof the new one works. The lack of testing and the "big bang" approach constitute a factual blocker: the plan relies on an unproven assumption that customers will accept the change instantly and positively.

Blockers: No customer testing data; high probability of alienating core base; "surprise" strategy amplifies backlash risk.
Risks: Permanent brand damage; sales decline; reversion costs.

DECISION: NO-GO
