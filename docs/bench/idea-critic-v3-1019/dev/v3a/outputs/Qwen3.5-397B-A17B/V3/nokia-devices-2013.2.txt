The claim asserts that acquiring a struggling handset manufacturer for €5.4 billion will secure the ecosystem against a dominant rival. The steelman is that vertical integration is the only remaining lever to prevent the manufacturer's defection and force developer attention when organic growth has failed.

However, the "better-way" analysis reveals a fatal flaw in the logic. The core problem identified is a lack of apps and developers due to the market leader's dominance. Buying a hardware division with *falling* sales does not solve the software ecosystem deficit; it merely adds a declining hardware business to the balance sheet. History suggests that owning the pipe does not fix the content drought if the platform itself lacks momentum. The cheaper, safer alternative is to license the OS broadly to all manufacturers to maximize install base, rather than competing with them via a single, failing device line.

The pre-mortem is clear: The acquisition closes, integration costs soar, and other handset makers—fearing competition from the OS owner—abandon the platform for the rival. The result is a smaller total install base, higher burn rate, and no increase in developer interest. The "app gap" remains because developers follow users, not corporate ownership structures. The falling sales of the target indicate the market has already rejected the hardware, making the €5.4 billion price tag an anchor on a sinking ship.

The strongest attack is that this move conflates "control" with "viability." Controlling a failing asset does not make it succeed; it often accelerates its decline by alienating the very partners needed for scale. The evidence provided (falling sales, dominant rival app store) directly contradicts the hypothesis that hardware ownership will reverse ecosystem momentum. This is a classic "doubling down" error where the solution (buy hardware) does not address the root cause (lack of developer traction).

Blockers: The proposal assumes hardware ownership fixes an app-ecosystem problem, but the facts state sales are falling and the rival has the apps. There is no mechanism provided where buying this specific manufacturer reverses the developer trend.
Risks: Partner alienation, integration drag, continued sales decline.

DECISION: NO-GO
