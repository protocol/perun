The claim rests on the assumption that acquiring the dominant platform neutralizes the threat of a free competitor and secures the buyer's pivot to open source. However, the target is loss-making, leaderless, and culturally hostile to the buyer. The primary blocker is the high probability of developer defection: if the core asset (the community) flees to the free alternative due to distrust, the $7.5 billion purchase price buys a hollow shell with no revenue path to justify the cost.

Alternatives include building a competing host internally (slower, but avoids cultural toxicity) or waiting for the target's valuation to collapse further given its lack of leadership and profitability. The "do nothing" option avoids immediate capital destruction while the market tests the free competitor's viability.

The pre-mortem reveals a clear failure mode: the buyer imposes subtle integration pressures, triggering a mass exodus of key repositories to the free rival, leaving the buyer with a stranded asset and regulatory scrutiny for no gain. The cost of delay is low; the free competitor must prove it can scale enterprise features before this deal becomes urgent.

The figures do not support a "go" decision today. Paying a premium for a loss-making entity in a contested market, where the primary value driver (trust) is actively eroding, fails the basic test of capital allocation. The proposal lacks a concrete, verified mechanism to prevent community flight other than a promise of independence, which history suggests is fragile post-acquisition. Without a binding, structural guarantee of autonomy that satisfies the community *before* the deal closes, the risk of total value loss is a blocker, not merely a risk.

Blockers: The purchase price assumes retention of a community that has stated distrust and a viable exit path; no verified mechanism exists to prevent this defection.
Risks: Regulatory blocking, continued financial losses, integration culture clash.

DECISION: NO-GO
