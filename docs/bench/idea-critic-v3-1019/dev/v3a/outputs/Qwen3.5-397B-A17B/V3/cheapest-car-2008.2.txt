The claim rests on launching the world's cheapest car at $2,500 to convert scooter families, assuming volume compensates for razor-thin margins. The steelman is that unprecedented affordability unlocks a massive, untapped demographic, creating a new market segment rather than fighting for existing share.

However, the "cheapest" label is a double-edged sword. In automotive markets, low price often signals unsafe or unreliable products, deterring the very families prioritizing safety. If buyers perceive the vehicle as a "death trap," volume targets will fail regardless of price. Furthermore, the proposed dedicated factory faces active farmer protests; building on contested land introduces severe timeline and reputational risks that could halt production before it starts.

Alternatives exist: a phased launch using existing contract manufacturing avoids the land conflict and reduces upfront capital exposure. Additionally, positioning the car as "smart and efficient" rather than "the cheapest" mitigates the stigma risk while targeting the same price-sensitive demographic. Used cars and scooters remain cheaper; if the new car's quality is compromised to hit the $2,500 point, it loses its only advantage over used vehicles (reliability) and its advantage over scooters (safety).

The pre-mortem reveals a likely failure mode: the factory is delayed by protests, forcing cost-cutting measures that lead to a high-profile safety recall, cementing the "cheap equals dangerous" narrative and killing demand. The cost of delay is high due to fixed factory costs, but the cost of a flawed launch is existential.

Blocker: The proposal ties the entire strategy to a new factory facing active land protests. This is a verified, immediate threat to the supply chain that cannot be resolved by marketing. Without a guaranteed production site, the volume targets are impossible, and the unit economics collapse. The risk of being known as "the cheapest" is manageable, but the risk of having no factory is fatal.

DECISION: NO-GO
