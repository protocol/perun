The proposal fails the blocker test on three verified facts provided in the text. First, the price of $10,000 is explicitly stated to be "several times that of leading business PCs." In a rational market, a product costing multiples of the competition while offering inferior performance ("runs slowly") and fewer capabilities ("little third-party software") cannot succeed. The value proposition is negative; customers would pay a premium for a worse experience.

Second, the existence of a "cheaper machine with a similar interface... being developed inside the same company" creates an insurmountable internal conflict. Launching the expensive, slow unit now would cannibalize or be immediately obsoleted by the company's own upcoming cheaper alternative. This violates basic portfolio strategy: do not launch a high-cost, low-performance product when a superior, lower-cost substitute is already in the pipeline.

Third, the "little third-party software" constraint is a critical blocker for business adoption. Office workers rely on specific applications; without an ecosystem, the hardware is useless regardless of its graphical interface. The proposal offers no plan to solve this chicken-and-egg problem before launch.

The steelman of this claim—that being first to market with a GUI justifies a premium—collapses because the product is not just first, it is demonstrably slower and more expensive than existing text-based competitors, and faces immediate internal competition. The risks are not merely "maybes"; they are stated facts that guarantee commercial failure as proposed. A cheaper, faster, or software-rich alternative (including the internal project) reaches the same upside with far less risk.

Waiting for the internal cheaper machine or reducing the price to match competitors while improving speed are the only viable paths. Launching now guarantees inventory write-offs and brand damage.

DECISION: NO-GO
