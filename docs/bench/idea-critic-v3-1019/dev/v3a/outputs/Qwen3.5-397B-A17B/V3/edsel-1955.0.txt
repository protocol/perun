The claim is that launching a new mid-priced division in 1957 will capture growth and fill a portfolio gap. Steelman: A dedicated brand with unique styling and a new dealer network is the only way to securely occupy the mid-market before competitors or economic shifts close the window.

However, the proposal contains a fatal internal contradiction. It acknowledges a "recession risk" and that the segment "could shrink," yet commits to a "massive advertising" spend and the capital-intensive construction of a "new dealer network." In a contracting economy, mid-priced vehicles are historically the first casualty as consumers trade down to low-priced reliability or hold existing cars. Building fixed infrastructure (dealerships) and burning cash on teasers right before a potential downturn creates maximum exposure with minimum flexibility.

The "better way" is to utilize the existing dealer network of the low-priced or premium lines to sell a new mid-priced model under a sub-brand or distinct badging. This avoids the sunk cost of new real estate and allows for rapid contraction if the recession materializes. The current plan forces a "one-way door" investment (new dealers) based on a "two-way door" market hypothesis (segment growth) that is explicitly flagged as uncertain.

The pre-mortem is clear: The 1957 debut coincides with an economic contraction. Sales miss targets because the brand is unproven and priced too high for scared consumers. The company is left with hundreds of underutilized, contractually obligated new dealerships and massive inventory, draining cash from the profitable low and high ends. The "extensive market research" likely reflects consumer desire in a growth environment, not behavior in a recession.

The strongest attack is the timing mismatch: committing to high fixed costs (dealers, ad campaigns) immediately before a predicted volatile period. The proposal admits the risk but proceeds with the most expensive execution path anyway. This fails the blocker test: the stated risk of recession directly contradicts the strategy of expanding fixed infrastructure now. The plan does not demonstrate how it survives the downturn it anticipates.

DECISION: NO-GO
