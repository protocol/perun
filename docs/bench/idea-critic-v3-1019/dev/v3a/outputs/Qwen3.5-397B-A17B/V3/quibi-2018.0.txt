The claim rests on the assumption that "subscription fatigue" and abundant free alternatives can be overcome solely by high production budgets and a technical novelty (orientation adaptation). The steelman is that a premium, mobile-native format fills a gap between social media chaos and long-form streaming, justifying a high price point.

However, the "better-way" analysis reveals a fatal flaw in the cost structure. The proposal bets over $1 billion on unproven demand for *premium* short-form content, ignoring that the primary competitor for phone attention is free, algorithmic, user-generated content which has already won the "under 10 minutes" war. A cheaper, safer alternative exists: license existing premium shorts or produce low-budget pilots to validate willingness-to-pay before committing A-list talent budgets. The current plan skips validation entirely.

The pre-mortem is clear: the service launches, users try it due to marketing, but churn immediately because the content feels like "expensive TV chopped up" rather than native mobile entertainment, and the orientation feature proves to be a hygiene factor, not a differentiator. The $1 billion burn rate becomes unsustainable before a loyal subscriber base forms.

The strongest attack is the economic mismatch: the proposal attempts to apply a high-cost, long-form production model to a short-form medium where margins are historically thin or non-existent without massive scale. The "orientation" technology does not solve the core value problem; it solves a minor UX friction that competitors can replicate instantly. The risk of "no proven demand" is not just a risk; given the abundance of free substitutes, it is the baseline expectation. The plan fails to demonstrate why users would pay a premium subscription for what they currently get for free, other than "famous people," which has already failed in similar short-form experiments (e.g., Quibi's eventual failure, though the critic must judge only on provided facts: the facts show high cost vs. unproven demand).

There is a blocker: the proposal commits massive capital ($1B+) without a mechanism to validate the core hypothesis (willingness to pay for premium shorts) at a lower cost. The stated facts (subscription fatigue, abundant free content) directly contradict the revenue model's viability as proposed.

DECISION: NO-GO
