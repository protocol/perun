Steelman: Acquiring this franchise secures a multi-generational IP engine capable of saturating theme parks, merchandise, and film pipelines, justifying the $4B premium through long-term ecosystem synergy rather than immediate box-office ROI.

However, the proposal fails the blocker test. The core thesis relies on the franchise feeding all business units, yet the facts state "much of the merchandise value is already licensed out." This creates an immediate structural contradiction: the primary revenue driver for a family entertainment conglomerate (consumer products) is inaccessible until existing contracts expire, severely delaying or negating the projected synergies. Furthermore, the "creative lead" is retiring precisely when the franchise needs revitalization to overcome "mixed reviews" and prevent "fan rejection." Buying a declining asset at a peak price ($4B) without the original visionary and with locked-up merchandise rights is not a strategic acquisition; it is a capital trap.

The "do nothing" alternative preserves $4B in cash for franchises with available merchandising rights and active creative leadership. A "license-only" approach could test fan appetite for new content without the irreversible burden of a $4B buyout and the baggage of retired leadership. The cost of delay is minimal compared to the risk of overpaying for an asset that cannot immediately fulfill its stated purpose of feeding the entire business ecosystem.

The contradiction between the goal (feed all businesses) and the fact (merchandise rights unavailable) is a hard blocker. The absence of the founder combined with mixed recent performance creates a high probability of failure for the proposed trilogy, which is the catalyst for the entire plan. Without a clear path to unlock merchandise revenue immediately or a successor to the founder confirmed as part of the deal, the financial logic collapses.

DECISION: NO-GO
