The proposal advocates a capital-intensive expansion into low-margin retail during a recession, directly contradicting the company's recent losses and limited market share (3–5%). The core assumption—that third-party retailers cannot effectively demonstrate the product or digital-music strategy—is unproven; specialized kiosks or shop-in-shop arrangements could achieve demonstration goals without the burden of prime mall leases.

The "better way" analysis reveals a superior alternative: investing in high-impact marketing and limited experiential zones within existing partner stores. This approach mitigates fixed lease risks, avoids direct channel conflict with current resellers, and preserves cash flow. The proposed model introduces massive operational complexity (inventory management, staffing, real estate) in an area where the industry has a documented poor track record.

The pre-mortem indicates a high probability of failure: a recession reduces discretionary foot traffic, while high fixed lease costs rapidly deplete remaining cash reserves. If the digital-music strategy fails to gain immediate traction, the stores become sunk-cost anchors dragging the entire company into insolvency. The risk of channel conflict could trigger retaliatory de-prioritization by existing third-party retailers, who currently provide the bulk of sales volume, effectively cutting off the company's primary revenue stream before the new stores become profitable.

The claim that company-run stores are *needed* for demonstration is the weakest link; it assumes a binary choice between ineffective third-party sales and expensive owned retail, ignoring hybrid models. Given the company's financial fragility, the cost of delay is negligible compared to the cost of immediate failure. The proposal fails the blocker test: the fixed costs and channel conflict risks outweigh the unproven upside of direct demonstration, especially when cheaper alternatives exist to test the music strategy.

DECISION: NO-GO
