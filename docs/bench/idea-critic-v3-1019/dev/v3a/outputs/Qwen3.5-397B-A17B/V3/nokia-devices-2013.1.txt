**Steelman:** Vertical integration is the only remaining lever to prevent the sole hardware partner from defecting to the dominant rival, preserving the operating system's existence despite €5.4B cost and falling sales.

**Attack:** The core failure mode is not hardware supply but ecosystem vitality. The market leader dominates due to app availability, a problem hardware ownership does not solve. Acquiring a division with falling sales and a large, hard-to-integrate workforce consumes capital needed for developer incentives without guaranteeing user growth. If the acquired unit continues to lose market share post-acquisition, the company owns a shrinking liability rather than a strategic shield. Furthermore, alienating other handset manufacturers could accelerate their exit, leaving the OS with only one internal, underperforming device.

**Alternatives:**
1.  **Do Nothing:** High risk of the manufacturer defecting, likely killing the OS.
2.  **Subsidy/Contract:** Pay the manufacturer to stay exclusive. Cheaper, reversible, avoids integration drag, but lacks total control.
3.  **Acquisition (Proposed):** Maximum control, maximum cost, maximum integration risk.

**Fit & Pre-mortem:** Assuming the goal is OS survival, this fits only if the manufacturer is imminently switching. However, if the acquisition fails to reverse sales trends within 18 months due to integration friction, the company faces massive write-downs while still lacking apps. The "one-way door" nature of buying a 20,000+ person workforce makes reversal costly.

**Blockers:** None explicitly fatal based *only* on provided facts; the falling sales are a risk, not a proof of impossibility. The threat of defection appears imminent enough to justify extreme measures if no cheaper contract exists (which cannot be verified without external tools).

**Risks:** Integration paralysis, continued sales decline, competitor retaliation, capital misallocation away from software ecosystem.

**Kill Criteria:** If smartphone unit sales do not stabilize within 4 quarters post-close, or if developer sign-ups do not increase by 20% YoY despite hardware control, divest the division.

**Verdict:** The desperation of the position (low single-digit share, sole partner at risk) makes the high-cost, high-risk acquisition a rational "bet the company" move to survive, provided the alternative is immediate platform irrelevance.

DECISION: GO
