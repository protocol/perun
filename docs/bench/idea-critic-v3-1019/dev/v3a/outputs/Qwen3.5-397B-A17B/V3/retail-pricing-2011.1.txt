The claim is that replacing a deep-rooted coupon culture with everyday low pricing (EDLP) and branded shops will reverse sales declines in a mid-market department store. The steelman: eliminating discount complexity restores margin and modernizes the brand, leveraging the CEO's prior electronics success to create a curated, trustworthy shopping environment.

However, the skeptic hat identifies a fatal mismatch in customer incentives. The existing base is explicitly "trained to wait for coupons"; their purchasing behavior is elastic and driven by perceived savings, not brand loyalty or convenience. In electronics, rapid obsolescence and fixed specs support EDLP; in apparel/home goods for this demographic, value is psychological and tied to the "thrill of the deal." Removing the discount mechanism removes the primary purchase trigger for the core revenue source without evidence of a replacement audience.

The better-way analysis fails to find a cheaper alternative because the proposal ignores the cheapest viable path: a pilot. The plan commits the entire chain to a "one-way door" change (rebuilding stores, retraining staff, rebranding) based on unverified assumptions about a different retail category. A staged rollout in 5-10 stores would validate whether the older demographic accepts EDLP before burning capital on a full rebuild. The "do nothing" option (optimizing the current coupon model) retains cash flow while testing small pricing adjustments, offering higher reversibility.

The pre-mortem reveals a clear failure mode: sales collapse as loyal customers stop buying, waiting for non-existent sales, while new customers do not arrive fast enough to offset the loss. The cost of delay is negligible compared to the cost of a chain-wide failure. The evidence provided—that most sales historically come from discounted items—directly contradicts the core assumption that customers will pay full price immediately. This is a blocker: the plan's own data shows the revenue engine relies on the very mechanism being deleted.

No kill criteria or metrics are proposed to monitor the transition, only a total commitment. The contradiction is stark: the proposal targets a coupon-driven base with a no-coupon strategy, without a bridge or test.

Blockers: The core customer base's purchasing behavior (waiting for coupons) is incompatible with the proposed EDLP model, and historical sales data confirms reliance on discounts.
Risks: Total sales collapse during transition; inability to attract new demographic before old one leaves; massive capital expenditure on unproven store format.

DECISION: NO-GO
