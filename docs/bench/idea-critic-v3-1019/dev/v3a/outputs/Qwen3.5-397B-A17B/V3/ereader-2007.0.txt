The claim is that a non-hardware bookseller should launch a subsidized, cellular-enabled e-reader to unlock digital sales despite piracy fears and margin pressure.

Steelman: Only a frictionless, purpose-built device with ubiquitous connectivity can create a mass market for e-books, aligning publisher security with consumer convenience to grow total readership beyond print limits.

**Skeptic:** The company lacks hardware DNA; failure here could bankrupt the core business. Publishers, fearing piracy, may withhold titles, rendering the device useless. If the device cannibalizes high-margin print sales without generating sufficient digital volume, net revenue collapses.

**Better-way:** License existing hardware or partner with a manufacturer to reduce capex risk. Wait for the market to mature. Focus on a software app for existing devices (PDAs, laptops) rather than building a "walled garden" device. However, prior dedicated readers failed due to poor UX and lack of content; a half-measure likely repeats this. "Do nothing" cedes the digital future to competitors.

**Kill-criteria:** If publishers refuse content due to pricing/DRM terms, the value proposition vanishes. If hardware costs exceed subsidy capacity before reaching critical mass, the model fails.

**Pre-mortem:** The device launches, but the cellular network is spotty, battery life disappoints, or the screen is too slow. Publishers sue or withhold bestsellers. The company is stuck with unsold inventory and angry shareholders.

**Decision Analysis:**
This is a one-way door with massive downside but potentially category-defining upside. The risks are existential: hardware incompetence, publisher revolt, and cannibalization. The proposal admits the company has *never* designed consumer hardware. Entering a capital-intensive, low-margin hardware business to sell lower-margin digital goods, while antagonizing supply partners (publishers), violates basic strategic fit. The "cheaper" alternative is partnering or software-only. The specific combination of building unknown hardware + subsidizing connectivity + lowering prices creates a triple-threat failure mode. No evidence suggests the company can execute hardware better than previous failed attempts.

Blockers: Lack of hardware capability is a factual deficit, not just a risk. Publisher opposition is a stated fact that blocks content availability.

DECISION: NO-GO
