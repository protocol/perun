The claim is that signing an unproven rookie to a record-breaking deal with non-compliant products will reverse market share loss in basketball. The steelman: only a radical, high-visibility disruption can dislodge entrenched incumbents; safe, incremental signing of proven veterans yields diminishing returns and fails to capture the cultural moment required to lead in basketball.

Alternatives considered: signing a proven veteran (high cost, low differentiation, "do nothing" regarding brand perception); waiting for the player to prove himself (missed first-mover advantage, higher cost later); or spreading the budget across multiple minor endorsements (diluted impact). The proposed path beats "do nothing" because the status quo is active loss of ground.

However, the proposal contains a fatal internal contradiction regarding the "uniform rules." The plan relies on the league fining the player for wearing the shoe to generate controversy and marketing value. Yet, if the league enforces its rules strictly by banning the shoe entirely rather than just fining the player, the product cannot be sold or worn in games, wiping out the entire investment immediately. The proposal treats a potential ban as a mere "risk" rather than a binary blocker that invalidates the revenue model. Furthermore, allocating a "large share of the marketing budget" to a single unproven asset violates basic portfolio theory without a defined kill criterion for early termination if the player underperforms or gets injured in preseason. The "signature shoe" mechanism has rarely succeeded beyond a season; betting the turnaround on a five-year commitment assumes a trajectory that historically collapses after year one.

The pre-mortem reveals that the most likely failure mode is not just poor sales, but regulatory elimination of the product before launch. Without a verified contingency for a total ban or a shorter initial contract term to mitigate the injury/unproven risk, the plan is structurally unsound. The evidence provided admits the player has "not yet played a professional game," making the five-year term an unsupported assumption of future elite performance.

DECISION: NO-GO
