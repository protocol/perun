The claim is that investing heavily in general-purpose programmability for mass-market graphics chips (2004–2006) without an existing paying market will create a valuable new revenue stream.

Steelman: Embedding general-purpose compute capabilities now creates a massive, distributed supercomputer as adoption grows, locking in developers and dominating a future scientific computing market before rivals react.

However, the proposal fails the blocker test. The plan explicitly raises unit costs on every consumer chip sold today to subsidize a feature with **no committed customers** and **no large paying market**. The company's revenue comes almost entirely from gaming, a sector engaged in a fierce race with a main rival where engineering distraction could be fatal. Adding cost to the core product to chase a niche, unproven academic use case violates basic unit economics: you cannot sustainably raise prices (or margins) on a volume-driven consumer product to fund a speculative enterprise platform that generates zero near-term ROI.

The "better way" is to wait for clear market signals or develop specialized enterprise SKUs rather than bloating every consumer unit. The risk of engineering distraction in a cutthroat gaming market is a verified blocker; losing the core gaming war to focus on a ghost market ensures total failure, not just missed opportunity. The pre-mortem is clear: the company burns cash, loses gaming market share due to higher costs or slower feature iteration, and the scientific market fails to materialize fast enough to offset the losses.

There is no evidence that the marginal gain in academic experimentation justifies the immediate penalty on the core business. The contradiction is stark: the proposal admits "no large paying market exists" yet demands capital expenditure and unit cost increases across the entire product line. This is speculation, not strategy.

Blockers:
1. Immediate unit cost increase on core revenue driver with no offsetting demand.
2. High risk of losing the core gaming market share to rivals due to engineering distraction.
3. Zero committed customers for the new capability.

DECISION: NO-GO
