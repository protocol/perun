Steelman: Transform an invisible component into a consumer demand driver by subsidizing partner ads that feature a new brand logo, forcing buyers to request the chip by name and compelling manufacturers to comply.

**Skeptic**: The core assumption is that end-users care about or can distinguish a component they cannot see. In 1991, no precedent exists for B2B component branding reaching B2C influence. If buyers ignore the logo, the entire marketing budget is wasted with zero sales lift. The claim that "buyers will ask" is unsupported by any market data provided; it is a pure gamble on changing consumer behavior overnight.

**Better-way**: The "do nothing" option preserves capital while relying on superior engineering to win OEM contracts, the established sales channel. A cheaper alternative is a trade-only program incentivizing sales teams and OEM product managers directly, bypassing the expensive and unproven consumer media buy. Spending a "large share" of the budget on an unproven consumer campaign violates the principle of staged investment when the upside is hypothetical.

**Kill-criteria**: This is a one-way door; once the budget is spent on consumer airtime, it cannot be recovered. The cost of delay is low; competitors are not currently winning via consumer branding because the category does not exist. The pre-mortem is clear: the campaign launches, consumers remain indifferent, OEMs resent the intrusion, and the company runs out of funds to compete on price or R&D.

**Blockers**: 
1. **Unsupported Core Claim**: The proposal asserts buyers will "ask for its chip by name" without evidence that consumers make purchasing decisions based on internal components. This contradicts the stated fact that "buyers do not know which chip is in their computer." You cannot create demand for an unknown entity without a prior education phase, which this proposal skips in favor of immediate paid placement.
2. **Resource Misallocation**: Allocating a "large share" of the budget to an unprecedented strategy with high resentment risk from the actual customers (OEMs) creates a single point of failure. If the consumer angle fails, the core B2B relationships are damaged, and the budget is gone.

The plan relies on a behavioral shift that is not demonstrated and risks alienating the only people who actually sign the purchase orders (the computer makers).

DECISION: NO-GO
