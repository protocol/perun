The proposal attempts to create a new market category with an unproven, high-cost device reliant on immature core technology. The steelman argument is that early dominance in a new form factor justifies the risk of launching with flawed handwriting recognition and a premium price point. However, the failure hypothesis is clear: if the primary input method (handwriting) fails in real-world conditions, the device loses its main differentiator against cheaper, proven alternatives like paper or laptops.

The "better way" analysis reveals significant weaknesses. Doing nothing preserves capital while competitors validate the market. A cheaper, staged alternative would be to license the technology to an established player or launch a smaller, lower-cost pilot device to test demand before a full global rollout. The current plan ignores the "Chesterton's fence" of existing successful tools (notebooks, early laptops) without offering a reliably superior replacement.

The pre-mortem indicates a high probability of commercial failure. The device is too large for pockets, limiting utility as a true "handheld." At $700 (a significant sum in 1993), the target audience is narrow, yet the software ecosystem is non-existent, creating a chicken-and-egg problem. The reliance on heavy CEO publicity cannot compensate for a product that does not function reliably in its primary mode. The cost of delay is low; waiting for recognition accuracy to improve or for the form factor to shrink reduces the risk of a brand-damaging flop.

Evidence suggests the core value proposition is broken. If handwriting recognition is unreliable in testing, it will be unacceptable to consumers paying a premium. The risks are not merely "maybes"; they are fundamental blockers to adoption. A product that fails at its main task, costs too much, and fits nowhere is a recipe for inventory write-downs. There is no kill criterion defined here to stop the launch if beta testing confirms the recognition flaws, which is a critical oversight.

Blockers: Core technology (handwriting) is unreliable; price point is prohibitive for an unproven category; form factor contradicts portability claims; no software ecosystem.
Risks: Market rejection, brand damage, financial loss from unsold inventory.

DECISION: NO-GO
