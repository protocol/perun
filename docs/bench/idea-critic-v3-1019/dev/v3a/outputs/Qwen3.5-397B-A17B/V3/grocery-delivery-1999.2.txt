**Verdict: HOLD (Internal); Recommendation to Executives: NO-GO**

The proposal fails the fundamental test of venture logic: it attempts to scale a low-margin, operationally complex business before validating unit economics in a single market.

**The Fatal Flaw**
The plan commits $1 billion to fixed assets (highly automated warehouses) based on zero operating data. In grocery retail, margins are thin and errors compound. Without a proven model in the first city, there is no basis to assume the chosen automation technology works, that customer density will materialize, or that the 30-minute promise is economically viable. Building a dozen warehouses amplifies unknown defects rather than solving them.

**Better Way**
The "do nothing" alternative regarding expansion is superior. The correct path is to operate in the single current city until positive unit economics are demonstrated over multiple quarters. Only then should capital be deployed to replicate the model, starting with one additional city. This preserves cash to pivot if the initial assumptions fail.

**Pre-Mortem**
Assume it is December 1999 and the company is insolvent. The cause: $900M spent on specialized warehouses in cities where demand never reached the density required to cover the fixed costs of automation. The rigid infrastructure prevents pivoting to a lower-cost fulfillment model (e.g., in-store picking) when the "high-tech" approach proves too expensive per order.

**Blocker**
The proposal explicitly admits to having "no operating track record" and "no learning of unit economics." Committing irreversible capital ($1B) before learning creates a scenario where the cost of failure is total loss of equity, while the upside is merely faster execution of an unproven, potentially broken model. The lack of data makes the $1B figure a guess, not an investment.

**Decision**
This is a one-way door with no map. You cannot optimize scale before validating the core mechanism.

DECISION: NO-GO
