The claim is that splitting the brand, accounts, and billing for a declining DVD service from a growing streaming service will enable better strategic focus, despite a recent 60% price hike for the combined offering.

**Steelman:** Separating the brands isolates the streaming business from the legacy DVD model's declining economics and operational constraints, allowing each to optimize for its specific market trajectory without internal conflict or brand dilution.

**Attack:** The proposal ignores the immediate context: a 60% price increase was just implemented in July. Splitting the billing and accounts in September, only two months later, will likely be perceived by customers not as a strategic optimization, but as a deceptive tactic to hide the true cost of the bundle they were forced into. The "loss of cross-service recommendations" is a secondary feature loss; the primary failure mode is reputational. By forcing separate logins and bills immediately after a massive hike, the company signals that the price increase was a prelude to fragmentation, validating customer fears of nickel-and-diming. The rationale that the DVD business is "declining" does not justify alienating the remaining loyal base who likely value the convenience of a single plan. The cost of delay (waiting for the price hike backlash to subside) is lower than the certainty of compounding customer anger with administrative friction.

**Better Way:** Maintain a unified account and billing interface while internally managing the businesses separately. Delay the visual and logistical split until the price sensitivity has stabilized or the DVD subscriber base has naturally attrited further.

**Verdict:** The timing creates a compounding negative event. The operational separation is sound in a vacuum, but executing it immediately post-hike turns a financial decision into a brand crisis. The risk of mass churn due to perceived deception outweighs the strategic benefit of immediate separation.

DECISION: NO-GO
