The proposal demands simultaneous execution of three unproven capabilities: cross-border logistics, new inventory systems, and rapid real estate integration. The "steelman" argument is that speed creates a defensive moat against the entrenched competitor. However, the failure mode is catastrophic capital burn before product-market fit is verified in the new region.

Opening 130–150 stores in two years removes the option to learn from early mistakes. If the inventory system fails or supply chains bottleneck, the entire network suffers immediately, with no pilot stores to serve as a control group. The "do nothing" or "slow rollout" alternative offers the same strategic upside (market entry) with significantly lower risk: proving the supply chain and price perception on 10 sites before committing to 140.

The premise that a "large US discount retailer" can simply transpose its model ignores the specific friction of bilingual labeling and local regulation, which often reveal hidden costs that destroy margin assumptions. The proposal admits the systems are "not yet proven"; scaling unproven infrastructure to support nearly 150 locations is an operational gamble, not a strategy. The entrenched competitor's dominance suggests that price perception is the primary barrier; if the new entrant cannot undercut prices immediately due to logistical inefficiencies, the expansion fails.

The strongest attack is the lack of a reversible "off-ramp." A two-year, 150-store blitz is a one-way door; once leases are signed and systems deployed, retreat is costly. A phased approach (e.g., 20 stores) allows for system validation and price testing without exposing the entire balance sheet to a single point of failure. The current plan bets the company on the assumption that execution will be flawless across all fronts simultaneously, a premise contradicted by the admitted novelty of the required systems.

Blockers: The plan requires scaling unproven systems to full capacity immediately, creating a single point of failure for the entire international division.
Risks: Regulatory delays, supply chain bottlenecks, and misaligned price perception could render the entire fleet unprofitable before corrections are possible.

DECISION: NO-GO
