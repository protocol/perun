The claim is that merging with a smaller carrier possessing a popular push-to-talk service justifies a $36 billion expenditure despite fundamental technological incompatibility. The steelman posits that acquiring the high-value business customer base immediately outweighs the massive integration burden and legacy debt, securing a dominant B2B position before competitors can react.

However, the "better-way" analysis reveals a fatal flaw in the cost-benefit structure. The proposal admits the networks use different, incompatible technologies and the target is limited for data. In 2005, the industry trajectory is unequivocally toward data-centric 3G/4G standards, not legacy voice-only systems. Acquiring a "limited for data" network forces the buyer to either sustain costly parallel infrastructure indefinitely or undertake a high-risk, high-churn migration of the very customers they are paying to acquire. The $36 billion valuation likely prices in the customer base while underpricing the technical obsolescence.

The pre-mortem is clear: integration drags on for years, draining capital from next-generation network builds. The "popular" push-to-talk feature, while valuable, is a software/application layer capability that could potentially be replicated or licensed without acquiring an entire incompatible physical network and its legacy baggage. The "do nothing" or "build/buy software only" alternative offers the same upside (entering the B2B market) without the $36 billion sunk cost and the anchor of incompatible hardware.

The strongest attack is the technological mismatch itself. You are buying a customer base attached to a dying technology. If the integration fails or takes too long, those business customers will defect to more reliable, modern data-enabled competitors. The goodwill asset is fragile; it evaporates if service degrades during the complex merger of incompatible systems. The proposal treats the network incompatibility as a manageable risk rather than a core structural blocker that negates the deal's economic logic. The cost of delay in building a native solution is far lower than the cost of integrating a conflicting one.

DECISION: NO-GO
