The proposal fails the blocker test on three verified facts within the text. First, the target segment is "business email," yet the device ships "without built-in email" unless paired with a phone. This directly contradicts the core value proposition for the stated user base; a business user cannot adopt a primary device that lacks its primary function out of the box. Second, the price "matches the rival's larger tablet." Offering a smaller screen, an untested operating system, and a fragmented app ecosystem at the same price point as the market leader's superior hardware creates no economic incentive for adoption. Third, the "untested" operating system combined with "developers may not commit" creates a hollow ecosystem risk that is exacerbated, not solved, by withholding core apps.

The steelman argument—that this is a trojan horse to sell more phones or test a new OS—collapses because the proposal explicitly targets the business sector where reliability and native functionality are non-negotiable. The "cheaper path" of waiting to integrate email or lowering the price to reflect the reduced capability is ignored. The current plan asks business users to pay a premium for a device that undercuts their productivity and matches a competitor's price while offering less value.

This is not a risk to be managed with kill criteria; it is a fundamental flaw in the product-market fit as described. The contradiction between the "business email" leadership claim and the "no built-in email" reality is a hard blocker. The pricing strategy relative to the rival's superior offering is a second blocker. Until the device includes native productivity tools or is priced significantly lower to account for its limitations and OS risk, it cannot succeed in the stated market.

DECISION: NO-GO
