The claim is that launching a $5,000 self-balancing transporter with 40,000-unit capacity in 2001 will succeed despite regulatory ambiguity and unproven consumer demand. The steelman posits that high-margin early adoption by enthusiasts and institutions will validate the category before mass market expansion.

However, the proposal fails the blocker test on three verified facts provided in the text. First, the legal status on sidewalks and roads is explicitly "unclear." Launching a hardware product requiring public infrastructure access without defined legality is a fatal operational blocker; if authorities ban usage post-launch, inventory becomes worthless. Second, the price is "high" while uses beyond institutional buyers are "unproven." Relying on a mass consumer market for a $5,000 novelty item without proven utility contradicts basic economic principles of the era, especially when the primary alternative (walking or cars) is free or already owned. Third, the product makes riders feel "conspicuous" and carries little, directly undermining its value proposition for general transport.

The "better way" analysis shows that "do nothing" or a limited institutional pilot is superior. A factory sized for 40,000 units assumes demand that the proposal itself admits is unproven. Building full-scale capacity before resolving legal status and proving consumer utility creates massive sunk costs. A cheaper, safer alternative is to produce a limited run for verified institutional contracts where legal status can be negotiated privately, delaying the $5,000 consumer push until laws clarify.

The pre-mortem reveals a likely failure mode: regulatory bans in major cities combined with poor consumer adoption due to price and social stigma, leaving the company with unsold inventory and no legal avenue for use. The cost of delay is minimal compared to the cost of a failed mass launch. The proposal's own evidence (unclear laws, unproven use, high price) contradicts its core claim of viability for a mass launch.

Blockers: Unclear legal status prevents guaranteed usage; high price lacks proven consumer demand; full factory capacity assumes unproven scale.
Risks: Social stigma limiting adoption; limited utility reducing repeat value.

DECISION: NO-GO
