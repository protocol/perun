The proposal attempts to pivot a brand defined by child-centric value toward adult premiumization through force of spend rather than product-market fit evolution. The steelman argues that massive advertising can overwrite decades of brand conditioning, instantly creating demand for a high-margin product among a demographic that currently associates the chain with toys and low prices.

However, the "better-way" analysis reveals a fatal flaw: the proposal ignores the "do nothing" or "staged pilot" alternatives. Launching the "largest advertising budget the industry has seen" before validating demand violates basic unit economics. The risks cited are not merely potential; they are structural contradictions. If customers visit for value and kids, a premium price point actively repels the core base without a guaranteed influx of new adults. Brand perception (fast/value vs. premium) cannot be bought away instantly; it requires product proof, not just noise.

The pre-mortem is clear: the campaign launches, awareness spikes, but conversion fails because the price-value equation does not match the brand's established contract with consumers. The company burns capital on acquisition for a product the audience does not want at that price point, damaging same-store sales by alienating regulars who feel priced out or confused.

The "kill criteria" test fails immediately. There is no evidence provided that adults desire premium food from this specific chain, nor that advertising alone can bridge the trust gap. The proposal relies on the assumption that spend equals success, ignoring the possibility that the brand is fundamentally incompatible with premium positioning. A cheaper, safer alternative exists: test the burger in select markets with modest spend to validate price elasticity and adult appeal before committing industry-record budgets.

Because the proposal demands maximum spend before proving demand, and because the identified risks (brand mismatch, price sensitivity) are blockers that the current plan does not address, the claim fails the blocker test. The plan as proposed guarantees significant financial loss with a low probability of strategic success.

DECISION: NO-GO
