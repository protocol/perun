**Steelman:** An invitation-only launch at $1,500 validates demand for a head-worn computer while mitigating initial privacy backlash through exclusivity, aiming to transition to a mass-market everyday device.

**Attack:** The proposal fails the "everyday consumer product" positioning test against its own constraints. A $1,500 price point excludes the mass market by definition, creating a brand perception of luxury niche rather than daily utility. The identified risks are not merely hurdles; they are fundamental blockers to the core claim of "everyday use." Privacy objections regarding a always-on camera in public spaces create a social friction that exclusivity cannot solve; it may exacerbate the "creepy" factor by limiting access to an elite few. Without a verified "killer app" that justifies the cost and social stigma, the device remains a solution searching for a problem. The battery life constraint further undermines "everyday" reliability. The plan assumes the hardware itself drives adoption, ignoring that the value proposition (what it *does*) is undefined for the average user. A cheaper, staged alternative focusing on enterprise or specific vertical use cases (where privacy and cost are less sensitive) would de-risk the technology before attempting a consumer pivot.

**Blockers:**
1. **Contradiction:** Positioning as an "everyday consumer product" while pricing at $1,500 (luxury tier) and restricting to invite-only contradicts the mass-adoption requirement for "everyday" status.
2. **Unresolved Core Risk:** No evidence provided that the "clear everyday use" exists; the proposal admits this is unknown yet proceeds to a consumer launch.
3. **Social/Privacy Barrier:** The camera feature creates a hostile social environment for "everyday" wear that an invite-only program does not resolve, only delays.

**Risks:** High return rates due to lack of utility; brand damage from privacy scandals; failure to transition from early adopters to general public due to price anchoring.

**Kill Criteria:** If pilot users cannot articulate one daily non-novelty use case within 30 days, or if public sentiment analysis shows >40% negative association with "privacy," the consumer rollout must halt.

**Verdict:** The plan attempts to skip the "finding the use case" phase by forcing a consumer product definition that the current facts explicitly state is missing.

DECISION: NO-GO
