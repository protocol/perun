The proposal claims a market gap for affordable, lightweight roadsters despite industry consensus that the niche is dead. The steelman is that mass-market abandonment created an underserved segment of enthusiasts willing to buy a focused, no-frills driver's car if priced accessibly.

However, the "affordable" and "lightweight" goals directly conflict with the stated risk of rising safety compliance costs. Meeting modern safety rules typically adds significant weight and expense, undermining the core value proposition. The proposal admits the company lacks sports-car heritage, increasing execution risk in tuning and marketing. Furthermore, relying on a finite pool of "early enthusiasts" without a clear path to broader appeal invites the very sales fade the proposal fears. The "do nothing" alternative avoids sinking capital into a shrinking segment with high regulatory headwinds.

The strongest attack is the economic contradiction: you cannot simultaneously deliver a compliant, safe convertible and keep it "affordable" and "lightweight" without sacrificing margin or performance, especially for a mid-size maker lacking existing sports-car infrastructure. The proposal offers no evidence that the remaining market size supports the development costs against these constraints. The risk of safety rules making convertibles costly is not just a possibility; it is a structural barrier that invalidates the "affordable" premise unless specific, unproven engineering shortcuts are assumed.

Without a verified plan to overcome the weight/cost penalty of safety regulations or evidence of a market large enough to sustain low-volume production profitably, the claim fails the blocker test. The contradiction between "affordable" and "compliant/safe" in a low-volume segment is a fatal flaw as presented.

DECISION: NO-GO
