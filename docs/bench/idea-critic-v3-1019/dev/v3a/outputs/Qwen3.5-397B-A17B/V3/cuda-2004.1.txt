The claim is that a graphics-chip maker should divert resources to build a general-purpose computing platform on consumer chips between 2004 and 2006, despite zero current revenue and high unit costs. The steelman: this creates a future monopoly in parallel computing by embedding hardware capability before demand materializes, locking in developers early.

However, the skeptic hat identifies a fatal blocker. The proposal admits "no large paying market exists yet" and relies on academic experimentation with "hacked APIs." The core business depends entirely on winning the fierce gaming rivalry; diverting engineering focus and adding unit cost to every chip risks losing the primary revenue stream that funds any future venture. The "better way" is to wait for clear market signals or partner with existing high-performance computing vendors rather than subsidizing unproven hardware on millions of consumer units.

The pre-mortem reveals the most likely failure: the company bleeds margin on consumer cards, loses ground to its rival in gaming performance, and the scientific community fails to adopt the proprietary toolchain because the ecosystem remains too niche. The cost of delay is low because the market does not yet exist; the cost of premature execution is existential.

The strongest attack is the lack of a customer willing to pay. Building a compiler and toolchain without committed enterprise buyers is speculation, not strategy. The proposal offers no kill criteria or pilot phase; it demands a multi-year, all-in commitment based on hope. A "go-if" structure is missing; there is no trigger based on actual adoption metrics.

This is a classic "build it and they will come" fallacy applied to hardware with thin margins. The contradiction is stark: the plan risks the known, vital gaming revenue for an unknown, unpaid scientific market. Without a single committed customer or a protected budget separate from core gaming R&D, the plan fails the blocker test.

DECISION: NO-GO
