The claim is that acquiring a struggling handset manufacturer for €5.4 billion will secure the ecosystem against a dominant rival by aligning hardware and software incentives. The steelman posits that without vertical integration, the manufacturer will defect to the market leader, accelerating the platform's irrelevance due to the app gap.

However, the skeptic hat identifies a fatal contradiction: the proposal admits smartphone sales are falling and the rival holds the developer mindshare. Buying a shrinking asset does not solve the core "app-ecosystem" problem; it merely adds a bleeding hardware liability to a software company lacking manufacturing scale. The "better way" analysis suggests that €5.4 billion could fund aggressive developer subsidies or acquire key app creators directly, addressing the root cause (lack of apps) rather than the symptom (hardware uncertainty). Furthermore, integrating a large, declining workforce introduces massive execution risk while competitors may react by further consolidating against this weakened, distracted entity.

The pre-mortem reveals a high probability of failure: the acquired division continues to lose money, draining resources needed for platform innovation, while the market leader's network effect remains unchallenged. The cost of delay is low compared to the cost of a failed integration; waiting allows for a clearer view of whether the platform can survive via licensing alone. The evidence provided—falling sales and a dominant rival ecosystem—directly contradicts the premise that ownership will reverse momentum. This is a classic "catching a falling knife" scenario where the acquisition locks capital into a losing strategy rather than pivoting to a viable niche or service model.

The strongest attack survived is the fear of the manufacturer switching platforms, but this risk is mitigated if the platform itself becomes irrelevant due to resource drain from the acquisition. The lack of a concrete kill criterion for when to divest the hardware unit makes this a one-way door with no exit strategy.

DECISION: NO-GO
