Steelman: Acquiring a modern, object-oriented OS core from a small vendor is the only viable path to halt market share erosion after two failed internal replacements, leveraging external innovation to bypass internal stagnation.

**Skeptic:** The target's low sales volume suggests the technology may be technically sound but commercially unproven or niche. However, the buyer needs the *technology*, not the revenue. The founder's "difficult history" is a personnel risk, mitigated by limiting their role to "advisor" rather than executive leadership. The high price relative to revenue is typical for strategic acquisitions where the asset is intellectual property and talent, not current cash flow.

**Better-way:** "Do nothing" guarantees continued decline given two prior internal failures. Licensing another vendor's system creates long-term dependency and margin erosion, failing to solve the core differentiation problem. Building from scratch again has already failed twice; the opportunity cost of a third attempt likely exceeds the acquisition price.

**Kill-criteria:** The deal fails if the technology cannot run existing critical applications or if the integration timeline exceeds the runway before insolvency. The proposal addresses migration risk by acknowledging it, implying a transition plan is part of the "core" strategy. The "few hundred million" price tag, while high, is likely a fraction of the company's remaining market cap or liquidation value if the decline continues.

**Pre-mortem:** Failure occurs if the founder sabotages integration or the technical architecture proves incompatible with legacy hardware. Yet, the advisor role limits operational sabotage, and object-oriented systems generally offer better abstraction layers for compatibility than monolithic predecessors.

**Decision:** The status quo is fatal. Internal development is proven broken. Licensing surrenders control. Acquisition buys a working engine and the architect who built it, isolating the human risk. The cost of delay is total market irrelevance. The blockers cited (revenue, history) are manageable risks, not fatal flaws, whereas the alternative is certain failure.

DECISION: GO
