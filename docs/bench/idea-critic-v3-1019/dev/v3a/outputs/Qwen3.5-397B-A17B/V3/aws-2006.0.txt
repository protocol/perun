The claim is that a 2006 online retailer should launch a pay-per-use cloud computing business despite having no developer platform experience, facing entrenched incumbents, and operating with thin retail margins while investors already question capital intensity.

Steelman: The retailer's massive, spiky internal infrastructure build-out creates excess capacity that, if productized, could transform a cost center into a high-margin revenue stream, leveraging existing hardware investments to enter an unproven but potentially vast market before competitors lock it down.

However, the proposal fails the blocker test. First, the core competency mismatch is fatal: running a reliable, multi-tenant developer platform requires distinct operational DNA, tooling, and support structures compared to a single-tenant retail backend; the proposal admits zero experience here. Second, the financial premise contradicts the stated context: investors are already hostile to heavy capital spending, and this venture requires *more* upfront specialization and marketing spend without guaranteed returns in an "unproven" market. Third, the risk of distraction is not merely theoretical; for a low-margin retailer, splitting focus between a brutal retail war and a complex platform play against established hardware vendors threatens the core business's survival.

The "excess capacity" argument is a fallacy in this era; retail traffic patterns do not map cleanly to developer workloads without significant architectural refactoring, effectively building two data centers. The "cheaper alternative" is to optimize internal costs or spin off the infrastructure as a separate entity with its own funding, rather than burdening the retail balance sheet. The market uncertainty ("unproven") combined with the lack of internal capability and investor skepticism creates a scenario where the cost of failure outweighs the speculative upside. The proposal relies on the assumption that infrastructure alone constitutes a product, ignoring the platform ecosystem required to sell it.

Blockers:
1. Zero organizational capability in developer platforms vs. high reliability requirements.
2. Investor hostility to capex contradicts the need for specialized platform investment.
3. High probability of core retail distraction leading to margin dilution in both sectors.

Risks:
1. Incumbent vendors crushing a nascent, unproven offering.
2. Cultural clash between retail operations and platform engineering.

DECISION: NO-GO
