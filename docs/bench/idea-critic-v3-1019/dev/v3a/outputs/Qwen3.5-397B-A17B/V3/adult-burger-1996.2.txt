The proposal fails the blocker test. The core claim—that a massive ad spend can overwrite a deeply entrenched "child-centered" and "value" brand identity to drive premium sales—is contradicted by the stated facts. The risks explicitly note that current customers visit for kids' meals and perceive the brand as fast, not premium. In 1996, brand perception was sticky; a price increase coupled with a shift away from the core value proposition creates a disconnect that advertising alone cannot bridge without first proving demand.

The "do nothing" or "staged pilot" alternative is superior. Launching the "largest advertising budget the industry has seen" before validating demand violates basic unit economics and risk management. If adults reject the premium positioning (a high probability given the brand's image), the company incurs catastrophic losses with no recovery path. A cheaper, safer alternative exists: test the premium burger in a limited market with modest promotion to measure actual adult uptake and price elasticity. If the product gains traction organically or with low-cost marketing, *then* scale.

The proposal lacks a kill criterion or a pilot phase. It assumes the brand can pivot instantly via spending power, ignoring the "Chesterton's fence" of why the menu is currently structured for value and families. The stated risks are not merely "maybes"; they are structural barriers inherent to the brand's existing equity. Proceeding as proposed bets the entire growth strategy on an unproven hypothesis with maximum exposure.

Blockers: The plan commits to maximum spend before validating the core assumption that adults will accept a premium price from a value/child-focused brand. The stated risks confirm this mismatch is likely.
Risks: Total loss of promotional budget; brand confusion alienating the core family demographic; failure to attract the target adult segment.

DECISION: NO-GO
