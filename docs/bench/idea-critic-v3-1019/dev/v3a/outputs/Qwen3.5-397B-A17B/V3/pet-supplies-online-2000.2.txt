The proposal describes a "blitzscale" strategy reliant on continuous access to cheap capital to fund negative unit economics. The steelman is that speed creates an unassailable monopoly before profitability is required. However, the plan fails the blocker test on three fronts.

First, the core mechanism—selling below cost to acquire customers—is not a sustainable business model but a cash-burn rate. With negative gross margins exacerbated by heavy shipping costs, every additional sale increases the loss. This requires perpetual capital inflow.

Second, the timing is fatal. The proposal targets an IPO in early 2000. While the market is currently hot, the reliance on "proceeds" for operations assumes the window remains open indefinitely. The stated risk that "capital markets could close" is not a mere possibility; it is a near-certainty given the historical context of the dot-com bubble peaking in March 2000. If the IPO window slams shut even weeks after the planned date, the company cannot fund the TV campaign, the acquisition, or the warehouses, leaving it with massive fixed costs and no runway.

Third, the strategy lacks a path to unit economics positivity. Buying a competitor and building warehouses increases fixed costs while the pricing strategy ensures variable losses. There is no "kill criterion" or pivot point defined where customer acquisition costs must drop or prices must rise to achieve breakeven. Without this, the company is betting entirely on market sentiment rather than business fundamentals.

The strongest attack is the dependency on an eternal bull market to subsidize a structurally loss-making operation. The proposal admits competitors are also spending heavily, suggesting a price war where the winner is simply the last one with cash, not the one with the best model. Given the imminent market correction and the lack of a profitability horizon, approving this plan as proposed guarantees insolvency upon the first market shock.

DECISION: NO-GO
