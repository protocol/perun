The claim rests on the assumption that "subscription fatigue" coexists with untapped demand for high-cost, short-form premium content. However, the proposal fails the blocker test on **proven demand**. The text explicitly states "no proven demand for premium short-form" while simultaneously citing "subscription fatigue" as a rising concern. These are not merely risks; they are contradictory market signals that invalidate the core revenue hypothesis. The proposal asks to raise over $1 billion based on an unproven category during a market contraction for similar models.

The "better way" analysis reveals a fatal flaw in the cost structure. High production budgets and A-list talent require massive scale to break even, yet the format (under 10 minutes) competes directly with abundant, free alternatives. A cheaper, safer alternative exists: validate demand with low-cost pilots before committing $1 billion. The current plan skips validation entirely.

The pre-mortem is clear: the service launches, users balk at paying for short content when free options exist, churn spikes due to fatigue, and the high fixed costs of talent and production drain the $1 billion capital raise with no path to profitability. The technology feature (orientation adaptation) is a commodity, not a moat sufficient to justify a premium subscription in a saturated market.

The contradiction check confirms the blocker: the proposal's own evidence ("no proven demand," "subscription fatigue") removes the route to its upside (mass subscription adoption). There is no factual basis in the text to believe users will pay a premium for this specific format. Without a pivot to an ad-supported model or a significantly reduced initial capital commitment to test the hypothesis, the plan is mathematically unsound based on the provided facts.
