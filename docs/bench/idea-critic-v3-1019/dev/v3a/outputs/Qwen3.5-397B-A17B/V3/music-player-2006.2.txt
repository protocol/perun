**Steelman:** A well-funded entrant can disrupt a complacent monopoly by offering superior hardware (screen, wireless sharing) and a seamless ecosystem, betting that user experience outweighs first-mover advantage and that content owners will follow the money.

**Skeptic & Fit:** The proposal admits being "late to market" against a dominant player with a "loyal ecosystem." In 2006, network effects in digital music are peaking. Dropping the industry-standard copy-protection (DRM) that the company previously championed creates an immediate political blocker: major labels, who control the inventory, are unlikely to license content to a store that undermines their existing security agreements with the incumbent. Without label support, the store is empty; without the store, the hardware is a toy.

**Better-Way:** The "do nothing" or "partner" option is stronger. Licensing the software to existing device makers or focusing solely on the desktop client avoids the massive capital expenditure of hardware manufacturing and supply chain management. Entering the hardware race without a unique, defensible content moat is a capital trap.

**Pre-mortem:** The project fails because labels refuse to license songs due to the removed DRM, leaving the store barren. Alternatively, the marketing budget burns out trying to convince users to switch ecosystems for marginal hardware gains, while the incumbent matches the features using their established retail leverage.

**Blocker Test:**
1.  **Content Access:** The proposal explicitly states the company is dropping the copy-protection standard partners rely on. There is no evidence labels will waive this requirement for a late entrant. This contradicts the core claim that a store can launch.
2.  **Differentiation:** "Larger screen" and "wireless sharing" are incremental improvements, not paradigm shifts sufficient to break a locked-in ecosystem late in the cycle.
3.  **Economics:** No price advantage exists, and the cost of customer acquisition against a loyal incumbent is prohibitive.

The reliance on a "large marketing budget" to solve a structural lack of content licensing and late-market entry is a fatal flaw. The contradiction between dropping DRM and securing label partnerships is unresolved.

DECISION: NO-GO
