The claim is that acquiring the studio for $7.4B is the optimal path to secure future hits and revitalize the buyer's struggling hand-drawn unit. The steelman: this is a unique, non-repeatable asset whose creative leadership is the only viable engine to reverse the buyer's animation decline, and letting it go to a rival is an existential threat.

However, critical blockers exist. First, the valuation relies on a pipeline of merely "one film a year." At $7.4B, the deal implies a multiplier that demands a massive increase in output or per-film revenue not evidenced in the proposal. Second, the "all-stock" structure makes the buyer's largest shareholder a major stakeholder in the combined entity, creating a governance conflict where one individual could effectively veto strategic directions or extract disproportionate value. Third, the risk of "dependence on a few creative leaders" is not mitigated; it is cemented by putting them in charge of the entire division without a succession plan or retention guarantees beyond the immediate transaction.

The alternative of "do nothing" or a limited distribution-only renewal was rejected due to the breakdown in talks, but the proposal fails to prove that *ownership* is required to solve the economics versus a restructured profit-sharing model. The price assumes perpetual hit-making from a team that has never operated at the scale of a major conglomerate. The culture clash risk is high given the buyer's recent underperformance; imposing the studio's leaders on the existing unit could cause mass exodus rather than synergy.

The strongest attack is the financial mismatch: paying a premium for a single-film annual cadence creates a value trap unless the acquisition immediately unlocks a 3-4x production increase, which the "culture clash" and "dependence" risks make unlikely. The proposal offers no mechanism to ensure this scale-up. Without a guaranteed ramp in production volume or a cap on the founder's new influence, the deal destroys shareholder value.

DECISION: NO-GO
