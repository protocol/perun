The claim asserts that a mandatory, paid online service will succeed despite 2002 market conditions where broadband penetration is low, competitors offer free access, and the company lacks operational experience in hosting.

**Steelman:** A unified, high-quality paid service creates a premium ecosystem that locks in users and funds superior infrastructure, differentiating the console from fragmented, free competitor offerings.

**Attack:**
1.  **Market Fit:** In 2002, "few homes have broadband" is a hard blocker. Making broadband *mandatory* for a paid service excludes the vast majority of the installed base immediately. Competitors allowing publisher-managed or free play capture the existing dial-up and casual market; this proposal cedes it entirely.
2.  **Pricing Friction:** Asking users to pay for the console, then an annual subscription, then potentially per-game fees (if publishers still charge) creates excessive friction against the "free" expectation.
3.  **Operational Risk:** The company has "never run" hosting/support operations. Launching a console-wide critical dependency without prior experience invites catastrophic reliability failures at launch, damaging the brand permanently.
4.  **Alternatives:** A voluntary opt-in service or a free tier supported by game licensing fees would mitigate adoption risk while building the required operational muscle gradually.

**Blockers:**
*   **Infrastructure Mismatch:** Mandatory broadband requirement contradicts the verified fact that few homes possess it in 2002.
*   **Capability Gap:** Lack of existing hosting/support operations presents an unmitigated execution risk for a core platform feature.
*   **Economic Contradiction:** The proposal relies on mass adoption to justify costs, yet the pricing and connectivity requirements actively prevent mass adoption.

**Risks:**
*   Subscriber base too small to cover infrastructure costs.
*   Publisher revolt if the service cannibalizes their own online models.
*   Brand damage from service outages due to inexperience.

The plan fails the "fit" test against current infrastructure reality and the "capability" test against organizational history. The mandatory nature of the subscription and broadband requirement transforms valid risks into immediate blockers. A "go-if" structure is impossible here because the infrastructure deficit (broadband availability) is external and cannot be fixed by a precondition. The cheaper, safer alternative is a voluntary, perhaps free, initial layer to build capacity and user habit before monetizing.

DECISION: NO-GO
