**Steelman:** Launching in May 1995 secures a four-month technical monopoly, establishing mindshare and shelf presence before the competitor arrives, betting that early adopters will pay a premium for exclusivity despite limited software.

**Attack:** The proposal fails on three verified fronts derived from the text itself. First, the **price disparity**: at $399, the unit is 33% more expensive than the rival's $299 equivalent. In a price-sensitive mass market, this creates an immediate barrier that "few games" cannot overcome. Second, the **software vacuum**: launching with "few games ready" while developers "did not plan for the early date" guarantees a barren library. Hardware without software is a demo unit, not a product; early buyers will churn immediately upon realizing the content drought. Third, the **channel fracture**: shipping "only to a few retailers" explicitly invites retaliation from excluded partners. In 1995, retail relationships were the primary distribution bottleneck; alienating major chains ensures poor placement and active sabotage of the brand, negating the head-start advantage.

**Alternatives:** The "do nothing" option (September launch) allows time to align developer schedules, secure broader retail buy-in, and potentially match the competitor's price point. A "staged launch" (May announcement, September ship) captures the marketing buzz without the operational suicide of shipping an incomplete ecosystem.

**Pre-mortem:** Assume it is December 1995 and the launch failed. Why? The high price scared off casuals, the lack of games bored the early adopters, and angry retailers refused to stock the holiday inventory, handing the entire season to the competitor. The "first mover" advantage evaporated because the product was not ready to sustain interest.

**Blockers:** 
1. **Price:** $100 premium over rival with no content justification.
2. **Supply:** Developer misalignment ensures a software drought at launch.
3. **Distribution:** Strategic exclusion of retailers threatens long-term market access.

These are not risks; they are facts stated in the proposal that guarantee commercial failure. The plan sacrifices channel relationships and product completeness for a calendar date that offers no defensible moat given the hardware limitations described.

DECISION: NO-GO
