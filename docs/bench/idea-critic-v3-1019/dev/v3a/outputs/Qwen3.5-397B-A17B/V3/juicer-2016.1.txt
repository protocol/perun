The claim is that a $699 Wi-Fi juicer with proprietary subscription bags will succeed in early 2016. The steelman: high upfront cost buys convenience and freshness verification, creating recurring revenue via a locked ecosystem.

However, the math fails the "beats do nothing" test. A consumer paying $699 for hardware plus a premium per-bag subscription faces a unit economics cliff. Buying fresh produce and using a $100 blender is drastically cheaper. The "freshness check" via app is a solution in search of a problem; spoilage is obvious via smell/sight, making the connectivity requirement a friction point, not a value add.

The market size concern is validated by the value proposition gap. This is not a "better way"; it is a "more expensive, less flexible way." The proprietary bag model (razor-and-blade) only works if the blade is significantly superior or the convenience overwhelming. Here, the convenience is negative: users must wait for delivery rather than buy fresh fruit on demand. The high device price creates a massive adoption barrier before the subscription even begins.

Pre-mortem: The device sits on counters as an expensive paperweight because users refuse to pay the subscription premium after the trial, and third-party bags are impossible to use. The "smart" features become liabilities when servers lag or the app updates break compatibility. The cost of delay is low; waiting allows competitors to fail with this model first.

Blocker: The core economic premise (subscription cost + hardware amortization < perceived value of convenience/freshness) is unsupported by the facts given. The alternative (standard juicer + grocery store produce) offers the same outcome (juice) at a fraction of the cost with zero lock-in. The proposal relies on users ignoring basic unit economics.

Risks: High churn, negative word-of-mouth due to price, and obsolescence if the company fails to support the cloud infrastructure.

DECISION: NO-GO
