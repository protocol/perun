**Steelman:** Acquiring a modern, object-oriented OS core from a small vendor is the only viable path to rapidly replace a failing, dated system when internal attempts have twice failed, despite high cost and integration risks.

**Attack & Analysis:**
The proposal faces immediate skepticism: the target has negligible revenue, the price is disproportionate, and the founder's history suggests cultural friction. Migrating developers to a new core is historically perilous. However, the "do nothing" alternative guarantees continued market share erosion and eventual irrelevance given two prior internal failures. Licensing a competitor's system surrenders strategic control and differentiation.

The "better way" of building internally is disproven by the company's track record; they cannot execute this alone. The "better way" of licensing fails the strategic fit for a hardware maker needing deep OS integration. The high price is not for current revenue but for the *time saved* and the *capability acquired* (object-oriented architecture) which is the missing ingredient in previous failures.

The primary blocker—technical integration risk—is mitigated by the specific nature of the target: a modern, object-oriented system is inherently more adaptable than the dated legacy code. The founder's return as an *advisor* rather than immediate executive leadership manages the cultural risk while securing essential institutional knowledge. The risk of the target's low sales is irrelevant; the asset is the technology and engineering talent, not the commercial track record.

**Edge:** The company possesses the distribution channel and brand to scale a technology the small vendor could never monetize alone. The urgent need for a modern core aligns perfectly with the target's specific architectural strengths.

**Decision Logic:**
*   **Blockers:** None. The risks (migration, culture, price) are severe but manageable compared to the certainty of failure via the status quo or licensing.
*   **Risks:** Integration complexity, founder friction, overpayment. These are execution risks, not fatal flaws.
*   **Edge:** Unique combination of desperate need, failed internal alternatives, and a target with the exact missing technological architecture.

The cost of delay (continued market collapse) outweighs the execution risk. The proposal represents a high-stakes, two-way door (if it fails, the company was likely doomed anyway; if it succeeds, it saves the business).

DECISION: GO
