The claim is that replacing the flagship formula with a sweeter variant, validated by blind taste tests, will reverse market share loss. The steelman: sensory superiority drives purchase, and removing the old formula forces migration to the superior product, maximizing efficiency.

However, the attack reveals a fatal blocker. The evidence cited (blind taste tests) explicitly isolates taste from brand context, yet the proposal ignores the verified risk of "emotional attachment" and "brand heritage." A blind test measures liquid preference, not brand loyalty. By withdrawing the original entirely, the plan converts a product choice into an identity crisis. The testing methodology failed to simulate the actual market condition: consumers choosing between brands, not just liquids. The fact that participants were not told the original would be removed invalidates the data for this specific strategic move; had they known, the "sweeter" preference likely would have collapsed under the weight of loss aversion.

The "better way" is a line extension: launch the sweeter formula as a new variant while retaining the original. This captures the taste-preference segment without alienating the heritage base. The proposed "do nothing" alternative (keeping the status quo) is losing share, but the proposed "all-in" swap risks total brand rupture.

The pre-mortem is clear: loyalists revolt, perceiving the removal as corporate arrogance, leading to boycotts and a forced, humiliating reintroduction of the old formula at higher cost. The cost of delay is low compared to the cost of reversal. The plan lacks a kill criterion for brand sentiment, relying solely on taste metrics. There is no edge here; being first to delete your own heritage is not an advantage. The absence of a fallback product creates a one-way door with no exit.

Blockers: The testing data contradicts the strategic action (blind taste ≠ brand choice); the plan ignores the stated risk of emotional attachment which invalidates the primary evidence.
Risks: Consumer revolt, bottler rebellion, permanent brand damage.
Edge: None identified; sensory win is negated by brand loss.

DECISION: NO-GO
