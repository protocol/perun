The claim rests on paying for what is currently free and abundant. The steelman: a premium, mobile-native short-form service with superior tech and talent can overcome subscription fatigue where others failed. However, the proposal fails the "do nothing" and "cheaper alternative" tests. Existing platforms already host abundant free short-form content; user-generated and ad-supported models dominate attention. Adding a paywall to short-form video contradicts current market behavior without evidence of unique demand.

The "orientation" feature (vertical/horizontal) is a minor UX improvement, not a defensible edge against entrenched competitors with massive distribution. High production budgets and A-list talent increase burn rate without guaranteeing retention in a format defined by low-commitment viewing. The pre-mortem is clear: the service launches, users try it due to brand name, but churn immediately because the content format does not justify a standalone subscription fee compared to free alternatives. The cost of delay is low; waiting for proven demand metrics is safer than betting $1 billion on an unproven category shift.

There is no named edge here that rivals lack. Studios and brands are investors, but they also compete for the same finite phone attention. Being first with "premium" short-form is not an advantage if the market wants "free" and "fast." The risks (no proven demand, price sensitivity, competition) are not just possibilities; they are current market realities that the proposal dismisses rather than solves. A capped pilot might be viable, but raising $1 billion for a full launch based on assumptions contradicts the evidence of subscription fatigue and free abundance.

Blockers: The core assumption that users will pay for short-form video when free alternatives exist is unsupported by current market data.
Risks: High churn, inability to recoup massive production costs, rapid depletion of capital.
Edge: None identified; talent and tech are table stakes, not differentiators.

DECISION: NO-GO
