**Steelman:** A $100 cartoon-house shell simplifies computing for novices on high-end PCs, creating an immediate bridge before a major OS update arrives.

**Attack & Options:**
The proposal fails the "cheaper alternative" and "timing" tests.
1.  **Hardware Mismatch:** Requiring a "high-end PC" in 1995 excludes the vast majority of "new home users" who own standard machines. The addressable market is too small to justify development.
2.  **Cannibalization & Obsolescence:** With a major OS interface release imminent, shipping a separate $100 shell risks confusing the brand and becoming instant legacy ware. Users will wait for the built-in OS update rather than pay extra for a temporary fix.
3.  **Better Way:** The "do nothing" or "guide-only" option is superior. Integrating simplified tutorials into the existing interface costs less, reaches all hardware tiers, and aligns with the upcoming OS strategy. A separate product creates fragmentation.
4.  **Pre-mortem:** Six months post-launch, the major OS update renders the shell redundant. Sales collapse, support costs rise for a deprecated product, and the "patronizing" feedback from power users damages the brand's professional reputation.

**Blockers:**
*   **Fact:** The target demographic (new home users) predominantly lacks the required "high-end PC."
*   **Fact:** An imminent major OS release with similar goals makes a separate paid product strategically unsound.

**Risks:**
*   Market rejection due to perceived childishness by decision-makers (parents).
*   Zero third-party ecosystem at launch reduces long-term value.

**Edge:**
None identified. Being first with a cartoon interface is not an edge if the hardware base doesn't exist and a free, integrated competitor (the upcoming OS) is months away.

**Decision Logic:**
The plan requires hardware the users don't have, solves a problem the core product will solve for free shortly, and risks brand dilution. It does not beat "do nothing" or "integrate into OS."

DECISION: NO-GO
