**Steelman:** Acquiring the struggling handset manufacturer for €5.4B secures the only remaining hardware partner for the niche OS, preventing its defection to the dominant rival and enabling tight integration to improve user experience.

**Attack:** The core diagnosis is flawed. The proposal assumes hardware ownership solves an *app-ecosystem* problem. However, developers flock to platforms with existing user scale, not integrated supply chains. Buying a failing division with falling sales adds massive integration drag (workforce, culture, manufacturing losses) without addressing the root cause: the lack of applications. The "edge" is negative; the company inherits a bleeding asset while the market leader's network effect widens. The cost of delay is high, but the cost of this specific action is likely total capital loss without gaining the desired ecosystem lift. A cheaper alternative is investing directly in developer incentives or licensing the OS to other manufacturers rather than owning the liability of a single failing factory.

**Blockers:** None explicitly proven false yet, but the strategic fit is weak. The plan fails the "upside" test: owning hardware does not automatically generate apps.
**Risks:** Massive integration failure, continued sales decline, alienation of other handset partners who may fear competition or abandonment, burning cash on a sinking ship.
**Edge:** None identified. Being the "only integrated option" for a low-single-digit share OS is a liability, not an asset, unless the hardware can miraculously outperform rivals despite the app gap.

The proposal conflates control with competitiveness. Without a credible mechanism to reverse the app deficit through hardware ownership, this is a defensive panic move that mortgages the company's future on a dying manufacturing line. The €5.4B would be better spent attacking the ecosystem gap directly.

DECISION: NO-GO
