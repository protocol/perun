The claim is that merging with the smaller carrier creates sufficient value to justify a $36 billion price tag despite severe technological incompatibility. The steelman: acquiring the target's high-value business segment immediately secures a lucrative revenue stream that organic growth cannot match, justifying the integration burden.

However, the "better-way" analysis reveals a fatal flaw in the cost-benefit structure. The proposal admits the networks use different, incompatible technologies and the target is limited for data. In 2005, the industry trajectory is decisively shifting toward data-centric 3G standards. Integrating a legacy, voice-only push-to-talk network requires running parallel infrastructure indefinitely or forcing a costly, high-churn migration of the very customers being acquired. The "cheaper alternative" is to license or build a push-to-talk application layer on the buyer's existing data network, avoiding the $36 billion capital outlay and the operational drag of maintaining obsolete hardware.

The pre-mortem is clear: the integration costs balloon as legacy equipment fails and skilled engineers for the old technology retire. The "goodwill" asset becomes an impairment charge when the legacy customer base shrinks faster than anticipated due to service degradation during the messy transition. The cost of delay is negligible compared to the risk of immediate capital misallocation; waiting allows the buyer to observe if the push-to-talk feature can be replicated software-first.

There is no named "edge" here that requires ownership of the incompatible network. The buyer's distribution and brand are sufficient to capture the business market if the service can be delivered natively. The proposal relies on the assumption that physical network ownership is the only way to serve these customers, a premise contradicted by the emerging software trends of the era. The blocker is the lack of a viable migration path that preserves the margin profile of the acquired users without sinking capital into a dying technology stack. The risks are not merely "execution difficulty"; they are structural obsolescence baked into the asset being purchased.

DECISION: NO-GO
