**Steelman:** Replacing the formula with a objectively superior-tasting product will reverse share loss by winning the functional battle for taste.

**Attack:** The proposal commits a category error: it treats cola as a commodity defined solely by blind sensory data, ignoring that the brand's value is rooted in heritage, nostalgia, and identity. The testing methodology is fatally flawed; by withholding the fact that the original would be discontinued, it measured preference for a *new option*, not acceptance of a *replacement*. Consumers can like two things simultaneously; forcing a choice by deleting one triggers psychological reactance, transforming passive preference into active outrage.

**Better Way:** Launch the sweeter formula as a distinct new product line alongside the original, allowing market data to determine cannibalization organically rather than engineering a forced extinction. Alternatively, conduct a second round of testing explicitly stating the original would vanish to measure true acceptance of the loss.

**Pre-mortem:** Assuming failure, the cause is a mass boycott driven by the perception that the company arrogantly deleted a cultural icon. The "sweeter taste" advantage becomes irrelevant when the product is unavailable due to distribution chaos and public relations fires. The lack of a fallback product leaves zero room for error.

**Blockers:**
1. **Methodological Fraud:** The core evidence (taste tests) is invalid because it concealed the primary consequence (discontinuation), rendering the data useless for this specific strategic decision.
2. **No Edge:** Being "sweeter" is not a defensible edge when the cost is alienating the existing base that defines the brand's market position.

**Risks:** Irreversible damage to brand equity; bottler revolt; permanent loss of loyalists who cannot return to the original.

**Edge:** None identified. The proposal relies on a physical attribute (sweetness) to solve an emotional problem (brand loyalty), which is a mismatched strategy.

**Kill Criteria:** If the testing does not explicitly measure reaction to the *removal* of the original, the plan cannot proceed. This condition is not met.

**Decision:** The plan fails the blocker test. The evidence cited does not support the specific action of total replacement because the testing protocol excluded the variable of loss. Approving this based on current data is a high-risk gamble with no safety net.

DECISION: NO-GO
