Steelman: Launching four months early in May 1995 at $399 secures a critical "first-mover" mindshare advantage before the competitor's September arrival, forcing the market to define the generation on our terms despite limited inventory.

**Blockers:** NONE. The proposal explicitly accepts the risks of retailer retaliation, sparse software, and high price as the cost of the strategy. No internal fact contradicts the feasibility of shipping to *some* retailers by May.

**Risks:** 
1. **Developer Revolt:** Releasing before tools are mature or devs are ready creates a "ghost town" launch, damaging long-term ecosystem trust.
2. **Price Perception:** At $100 above the rival, early adopters may perceive the console as overpriced relative to its incomplete library.
3. **Channel Alienation:** Excluding major retailers for a limited rollout invites permanent distribution sabotage when volume scales.

**Edge:** Time. In console wars, being the first 32-bit system in US homes defines the narrative. A September launch cedes four months of exclusive marketing dominance to the rival, allowing them to set the baseline for "next-gen" performance and pricing before this console arrives. The rival's lower price is irrelevant if they are not on shelves.

**Decision Logic:**
The proposal is a high-risk, high-reward "one-way door" intended to seize the temporal advantage. The risks listed (price, games, devs) are execution challenges inherent to *any* early launch, not specific blockers that make *this* plan impossible. The strategy relies on shock value: announcing and shipping immediately at a trade show creates a media event that a standard September launch cannot match. Waiting for perfect conditions (more games, lower price, dev alignment) guarantees losing the "first" title to the competitor. The edge (being first) outweighs the operational friction.

DECISION: GO
