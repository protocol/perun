The claim is that a pre-revenue grocery startup should commit $1B to automated warehouses across a dozen cities before validating unit economics in its single launch market. The steelman argues that speed and infrastructure scale are necessary to secure first-mover advantage and deter competitors in a winner-take-all market.

However, this plan fails the blocker test immediately. The proposal explicitly admits the company has "no operating track record" and has not yet "learning the unit economics." In a low-margin business like groceries, where success depends entirely on customer density and operational efficiency, scaling unproven models is fatal. The risks cited (capital intensity, thin margins, density needs) are not hypothetical; they are known industry killers that require empirical data to solve.

The "better way" is clear: validate the model in the first city, achieve positive unit economics, and then expand. A cheaper, safer alternative exists: leasing existing space and using third-party logistics to test demand without billion-dollar sunk costs. The cost of delay (waiting to learn) is negligible compared to the cost of failure (building the wrong infrastructure in twelve cities simultaneously).

There is no edge presented. Being first with unproven technology in a capital-intensive field is not an advantage; it is a liability. The proposal relies on the assumption that venture capital replaces the need for validated learning, which contradicts basic business logic. The pre-mortem is obvious: the company runs out of cash while trying to fix fundamental operational flaws across too many locations, unable to pivot because its capital is locked in specialized, unused warehouses.

This is a one-way door decision with irreversible consequences based on zero evidence of viability. The lack of a proven pilot makes the $1B commitment a blocker. No amount of funding justifies skipping the learning phase in a low-margin, operationally complex industry.

DECISION: NO-GO
