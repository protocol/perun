**Steelman:** Launching a $10,000 graphical computer in 1983 captures the high-end office market by offering an intuitive interface that justifies the premium over text-based competitors.

**Attack & Analysis:**
The proposal fails the blocker test on three verified facts provided in the text:
1.  **Price Disadvantage:** At $10,000, the unit costs "several times" that of leading business PCs. Without a proven, exclusive productivity advantage, office buyers will reject this cost disparity.
2.  **Performance Deficit:** The machine "runs slowly." For business users prioritizing throughput, a slow, expensive machine is inferior to a fast, cheap one.
3.  **Ecosystem Void:** "Little third-party software" exists. A business computer without applications has no utility, regardless of interface quality.

**Better Way:**
The "do nothing" or "delay" option is superior. A cheaper machine with a similar interface is already "being developed inside the same company." Launching the expensive, slow model now cannibalizes resources and brand reputation before the viable, cost-effective alternative is ready. The correct move is to halt this specific configuration and accelerate the internal cheaper project.

**Kill Criteria & Edge:**
*   **Blocker:** The existence of a superior internal alternative (cheaper, similar interface) combined with the current unit's high price and low speed creates an immediate financial loss risk.
*   **Edge:** None. Being first with a slow, expensive, software-poor product is not an edge; it is a liability. "Strategic necessity" does not override the fact that the product is objectively worse than both current market leaders and the company's own upcoming pipeline.
*   **Risk:** Launching could cement a reputation for overpriced underperformance, making future adoption of the graphical interface harder.

**Conclusion:**
The proposal asks to commit to a product that is more expensive, slower, and less useful than current market options, while ignoring a better internal solution in progress. This violates basic economic logic and product strategy. The plan must be rejected to prevent sunk costs in a losing proposition.

DECISION: NO-GO
