The claim is that launching two social-networking phones on a new, store-less OS via a single carrier with expensive data plans will succeed with teenagers in 2010. The steelman posits that the acquired brand's loyalty and a streamlined social experience outweigh the lack of apps and high costs.

However, the attack reveals fatal blockers. First, the "no application store" constraint contradicts the core behavior of the target demographic (teenagers on social networks) in 2010, where third-party apps are becoming the primary driver of smartphone adoption. Rivals already possess large catalogs; offering none is not a differentiation strategy but a disqualification. Second, the pricing model (high-cost data plan) targets a demographic with limited disposable income, creating a barrier to entry that the brand alone cannot overcome. Third, building a new in-house OS instead of leveraging the company's existing mobile platform fractures development resources and delays time-to-market, compounding the risk of the two-year sunk cost fallacy. The argument that "two years are already sunk" is an economic error; it justifies throwing good money after bad rather than cutting losses or pivoting to the existing platform.

There is no "edge" here. Being first with a specific brand does not compensate for removing the fundamental utility (apps) and affordability required by the user base. The risks are not hypothetical; they are structural flaws in the value proposition. The cheaper, safer alternative is to integrate the acquired messaging technology into the company's existing mobile platform and OS, launching on multiple carriers with competitive data options.

The proposal fails the blocker test: the stated facts (no app store, high cost, new OS) directly contradict the requirements for success in the 2010 teenage smartphone market. The plan does not answer how it will compete with rivals offering thousands of apps at lower price points.

Blockers: No app store in a app-driven market; high data cost for low-income demographic; fragmented OS strategy.
Risks: Carrier exclusivity limits reach; sunk cost bias driving poor architecture choices.
Edge: None identified; brand loyalty is insufficient to overcome functional deficits.

DECISION: NO-GO
