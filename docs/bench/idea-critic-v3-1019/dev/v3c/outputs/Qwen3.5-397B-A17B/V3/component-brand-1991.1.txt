**Steelman:** Transform an invisible component into a consumer demand driver by subsidizing OEM advertising, forcing buyers to specify the brand and compelling manufacturers to comply to access funding.

**Attack & Alternatives:**
The primary objection is that consumers do not care about internal components, making the budget burn futile. However, the "clone" threat proves buyers are confused; a simple logo acts as a quality heuristic, not a technical specification. The risk of OEM resentment is neutralized because the program *pays* them; they trade creative autonomy for subsidized reach. Doing nothing cedes the market to clones who win on price alone. A pure trade-promotion alternative fails because it does not create end-user pull.

**Fit & Pre-mortem:**
This fits a strategy to escape commodity pricing. If this fails, it will be because the logo was not displayed prominently enough by partners, not because the concept is flawed. The cost of delay is immediate margin erosion from clone competition.

**Evidence & Blockers:**
The proposal addresses the core fact: buyers cannot distinguish chips. The funding mechanism directly aligns incentives. There are no factual blockers in the text; the risks (unprecedented nature, budget share) are execution hurdles, not logical contradictions. The "unprecedented" nature is the edge, not a flaw, as it creates a monopoly on consumer mindshare in a B2B space.

**Edge:**
First-mover advantage in creating a consumer brand for a B2B component. Competitors cannot easily copy the mindshare once established.

**Decision:**
The plan survives the attack. The risks are manageable execution details, not fundamental flaws. The potential to break the commodity trap outweighs the budget risk.

Blockers: NONE
Risks: Execution complexity, OEM friction (mitigated by payment).
Edge: Unique consumer pull in a component market.

DECISION: GO
