The claim is that a $699 Wi-Fi juicer with proprietary subscriptions can succeed in 2016. The steelman: high upfront cost filters for convenience-obsessed early adopters, while locked bags create recurring revenue and freshness assurance that loose produce cannot match.

**Skeptic:** The value proposition collapses under basic math. At $699, the device costs more than premium blenders and high-end espresso machines, categories with established cultural cachet. The "freshness" check via app is a solution in search of a problem; visual inspection of produce is instantaneous and free. Internet connectivity introduces a single point of failure for a mechanical task, creating e-waste risk if servers shut down. The subscription model likely carries a massive premium per serving compared to grocery store produce, violating the "cost per use" logic of kitchen appliances.

**Better-way:** The "do nothing" alternative is strong: consumers can buy a $100 blender or $300 cold-press machine and use fresh, cheaper, non-proprietary ingredients. A cheaper path exists: sell the machine at cost ($299) to build a user base, then monetize via optional bag subscriptions without locking out generic inputs. Requiring proprietary bags limits the total addressable market to only those willing to bet on a startup's longevity for their daily nutrition.

**Kill-criteria & Pre-mortem:** Assume failure in 18 months. Why? The hardware margin is too thin to sustain operations, and the subscription churn is high because users realize they are paying 5x the market rate for fruit. The "one-way door" of custom engineering means no pivot to generic bags is possible without bricking existing units. The cost of delay is low; the market for smart kitchen gadgets is unproven in 2016, and waiting for component costs to drop or consumer habits to shift reduces risk.

**Edge:** There is no stated edge. Being "well-funded" is not a product advantage. "First to market" with a locked ecosystem is a liability, not an asset, when the core function (juicing) is commoditized. The proposal lacks a defensible moat against cheap clones or established appliance giants entering the space.

**Blockers:** The unit economics likely fail (high BOM + low volume vs. high consumer price sensitivity). The proprietary lock-in creates immediate consumer resistance without a commensurate value add.
**Risks:** High churn, hardware obsolescence, brand damage from perceived "scam" pricing.
**Edge:** NONE.

DECISION: NO-GO
