**Steelman:** Launching a $10,000 graphical computer in 1983 captures the high-end office market before competitors, leveraging superior usability to justify a premium despite lower raw speed.

**Attack & Analysis:**
The proposal fails the blocker test on three verified facts provided in the prompt. First, the price is "several times that of leading business PCs." In a rational B2B market, a product offering *lower* performance ("runs slowly") at a *massive* premium cannot win without a monopolistic lock-in or killer application, neither of which exists yet ("little third-party software").

Second, the "cheaper machine with a similar interface" being developed internally creates a fatal cannibalization risk. Launching the expensive, slower unit first invites immediate internal competition that renders the initial launch obsolete upon the cheaper unit's release. This violates the principle of capturing maximum value; the company should instead delay the premium launch or pivot resources to the cheaper, faster-to-market alternative.

Third, the lack of software ecosystem is a structural blocker, not just a risk. Hardware without applications provides no business utility, regardless of interface quality. At $10,000, early adopters require immediate productivity gains, which cannot be delivered without software.

**Alternatives:**
1.  **Do Nothing:** Preserve capital while the internal cheaper option matures.
2.  **Internal Pivot:** Redirect efforts to the cheaper internal machine to undercut competitors rather than overpricing a slower device.
3.  **Delayed Launch:** Wait for software ecosystem development before committing to a high-price launch.

**Edge Assessment:**
The proposal claims no unique edge. "Graphical interface" is not an edge if a cheaper internal version exists and software is missing. Being first with a slow, expensive, unsupported product is a liability, not an advantage.

**Blockers:**
1.  Price is multiple times higher than competitors for inferior speed.
2.  Internal substitute exists at a lower price point.
3.  No software ecosystem to drive utility.

**Risks:**
Total market rejection, brand damage from launching an overpriced underperformer, and immediate internal obsolescence.

**Decision:**
The plan fails as proposed due to insurmountable price/performance mismatch and internal conflict.

DECISION: NO-GO
