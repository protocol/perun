**Steelman:** A mandatory client solves critical update/cheat failures and pivots the business from one-off sales to a recurring platform, securing long-term distribution control.

**Attack & Alternatives:**
The proposal fails the "do nothing" test only if current losses exceed adoption friction. In 2003, mandatory online activation is unproven for mass-market PC gaming; broadband penetration is low (~20% US), and retail partners hold significant leverage. A "client-only" mandate risks immediate revenue collapse if retailers delist products or users reject the barrier.

However, "do nothing" guarantees continued revenue leakage from piracy and inability to patch exploits, eroding the core asset (game integrity). Alternative "optional patcher" models fail to stop cheating effectively. The "store" vision is a distant upside; the immediate necessity is operational survival.

**Blockers:** None fatal. The risk of retailer pushback is real but manageable if the publisher's titles are "very popular" (high leverage). Broadband limits suggest the client must support dial-up or hybrid physical/digital updates, but this is an implementation detail, not a strategic blocker. The "dislike" of accounts is a temporary friction versus the permanent value of a secured ecosystem.

**Edge:** The publisher owns "very popular" IP. This demand inelasticity forces retailers and users to accept the new terms, providing the distribution leverage competitors lack. Most rivals cannot force a client; this publisher can because their product is essential.

**Kill Criteria:** If retailer delisting exceeds 30% of volume within 6 months, or if active installs drop >15% post-mandate due to connectivity issues, pivot to a hybrid physical-key/digital-auth model. Leading metric: % of active users successfully updating via client within 48 hours of release.

**Verdict:** The strategic necessity of controlling updates and cheating outweighs the transitional friction. The IP strength mitigates the channel risk.

DECISION: GO
