Steelman: Committing now secures a decade-long technological monopoly and defines the brand as the future of mobility before regulators force the issue.

However, the proposal fails the blocker test. The plan relies on unproven nickel-metal-hydride durability for volume production while admitting "no near-term demand signal" and "cheap fuel." In the mid-1990s, consumer willingness to pay a premium for efficiency without regulatory coercion or high fuel prices is hypothetical, not factual. The "bold public schedule" creates irreversible reputational liability if the novel power-split transmission fails in the field—a high probability given the unproven battery chemistry.

The "do nothing" alternative maintains current margins while allowing competitors to absorb the R&D risk of early hybridization. A "better way" exists: develop the technology quietly as a limited fleet or compliance car to validate durability and real-world costs before a mass-market commitment. Mass production requires proven unit economics; this proposal bets the corporation on a technology that may work physically but fails commercially due to cost and lack of demand.

The stated edge ("first for volume production") is not an edge if the market does not want the product. Being first to fail publicly is a liability, not an advantage. The risks (cost, reliability, warranty) are not merely "risks" but active blockers because the proposal offers no kill criteria, no pilot phase, and no evidence that the target user cares. Committing to mass production before solving the durability equation and proving demand violates basic investment hygiene.

Blockers: No proven battery durability for volume use; no demand signal at required price point; cheap fuel removes immediate consumer incentive.
Risks: Massive warranty exposure, brand damage from reliability failures, sunk R&D if market rejects premium.
Edge: None proven; "first mover" status is negative without product-market fit.

This is a one-way door with a high probability of catastrophic financial loss based on assumptions rather than verified facts. The plan must be revised to include a pilot program with specific durability milestones and a validated willingness-to-pay study before mass production commitment.
