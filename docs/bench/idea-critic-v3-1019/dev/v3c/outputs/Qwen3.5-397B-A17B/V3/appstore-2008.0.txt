**Steelman:** A controlled native app store creates a defensible ecosystem that balances developer incentive (70% revenue) with product integrity (curation), transforming a "web-only" device into a platform without sacrificing the user experience that differentiates it from fragmented rivals.

**Attack & Analysis:**
The primary blocker hypothesis is that the 30% fee and strict review process will stifle developer adoption compared to open rivals. However, the counter-fact is that open distribution often leads to security incidents and fragmentation, degrading the very "polish" the CEO values. The proposed model directly addresses the stated concerns: review mitigates security/stability risks; curation prevents carrier-bypassing bloatware; and the revenue share incentivizes high-quality development despite the fee.

The "do nothing" option (web apps only) fails to capture the emerging native functionality competitors are exploiting, risking long-term irrelevance. The risk of review workload is an operational cost, not a strategic blocker, and is preferable to the brand damage of a compromised open store. The concern that apps might hurt product polish is paradoxically solved by the proposed solution (approval gate), whereas the status quo offers no such filter.

The edge here is the company's existing reputation for design excellence. By applying this lens to app curation, they can offer a "premium" marketplace distinct from the chaotic ecosystems of rivals. The 30% cut is standard enough to attract serious developers seeking a monetizable, stable environment. The CEO's previous stance on web apps is a sunk opinion; the market has shifted, and this proposal pivots without abandoning the core value proposition of a polished device.

No factual blocker exists in the text. The risks (workload, carrier friction) are manageable execution details, not reasons to halt. The proposal turns the company's greatest fear (loss of polish) into its primary competitive advantage via strict governance.
