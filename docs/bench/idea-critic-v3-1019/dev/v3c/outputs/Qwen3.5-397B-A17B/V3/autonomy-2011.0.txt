**Steelman:** Acquiring the target instantly grants software capability and analytics revenue, bypassing years of internal development to satisfy the new CEO's strategic pivot.

**Attack:** The proposal fails the blocker test on three verified facts. First, the price is indefensible: paying 11x revenue and a 64% premium for a company with questioned financial reporting violates basic capital discipline, especially when the buyer's own finance chief objects. Second, the "lack of software experience" creates a near-certain integration failure; hardware cultures rarely successfully absorb high-growth software firms without massive value destruction. Third, the due diligence window ("a few weeks") is insufficient to validate accounting irregularities flagged since 2009. Buying suspected fraud at a record premium is not a strategy; it is gambling with shareholder capital.

**Alternatives:** The "do nothing" or "build/buy smaller" options are superior. Organic build or acquiring smaller, verified assets avoids the fraud risk and cultural clash at a fraction of the cost. The target's alleged growth may be artificial; waiting for verified audited accounts costs less than the potential write-down of $11.7 billion.

**Pre-mortem:** Assume the deal closes. Six months later, an accounting scandal breaks, revealing inflated revenues. The stock crashes, the CEO is ousted, and the integration stalls because the hardware team cannot manage software talent. The $11.7 billion becomes a massive goodwill impairment.

**Edge:** There is no edge. Paying more than anyone else for a risky asset when you lack the core competency to run it is a losing position. Speed (closing quickly) is a liability here, not an advantage, as it bypasses necessary scrutiny of the accounting fraud allegations.

**Blockers:** 
1. Finance chief objection + 11x revenue multiple on unverified books = Capital destruction.
2. Known accounting questions since 2009 + rushed due diligence = High probability of fraud.
3. Zero software experience = High probability of integration failure.

**Risks:** Cultural clash, overpayment, regulatory scrutiny, fraud discovery post-close.

**Decision:** The combination of extreme overpayment, specific accounting red flags, and lack of operational fit creates an unavoidable blocker.

DECISION: NO-GO
