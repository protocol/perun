The claim is that launching the world's cheapest car at $2,500 from a new factory will successfully transition scooter families to cars. The steelman: capturing an untapped mass market by offering the absolute lowest price point creates a new category, leveraging first-mover advantage to dominate volume before competitors react.

However, the proposal fails the blocker test. The "cheapest" positioning is a strategic liability, not an edge. In automotive markets, low price without a counterbalancing narrative of "smart simplicity" often signals inferior safety and status. Families upgrading from scooters prioritize safety and dignity; a brand explicitly marketed as "the cheapest" invites skepticism about crashworthiness and reliability, directly contradicting the core need of protecting a family. This is not a risk; it is a fundamental mismatch between the value proposition (lowest cost) and the customer's primary motivation (safe upgrade).

Furthermore, the "edge" of being first is nullified by the existence of cheaper, proven alternatives: scooters and used cars. The proposal offers no evidence that the total cost of ownership (fuel, maintenance, insurance) of this new car undercuts a used sedan or multiple scooters enough to justify the safety concerns. Without a verified advantage in total cost or a repositioning away from "cheapest" toward "safe and affordable," the plan cannot win. The land protests add execution risk, but the marketing strategy is the immediate blocker.

A better way exists: position the vehicle as a "family safety upgrade" at a competitive price, letting the market discover it is the cheapest, rather than leading with a label that devalues the product. As proposed, leading with "cheapest" guarantees a race to the bottom on perception that volume cannot fix.

Blockers: Marketing as "cheapest" contradicts the family safety motive; no total-cost advantage proven over used cars.
Risks: Land protests, quality perception.
Edge: None stated that overcomes the "cheap" stigma.

DECISION: NO-GO
