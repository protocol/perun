The proposal attempts to enter a saturated market dominated by an entrenched leader with a loyal ecosystem. The core claim relies on hardware differentiation (larger screen, wireless sharing) and marketing spend to overcome lateness to market. However, the plan contains a fatal internal contradiction: it abandons the copy-protection standard the company previously championed to partners. This strategic flip alienates existing label and device allies without guaranteeing their content will be available under the new, looser terms. If labels restrict sharing as feared, the primary hardware differentiator becomes useless, leaving a late, expensive device with no unique content.

The "better way" analysis suggests that competing directly on hardware and store infrastructure against a first-mover with network effects is a low-probability bet. The cost of building a store, securing licenses, and funding marketing is immense, while the "do nothing" or "partner" alternatives avoid these sunk costs. The proposal admits no clear price advantage, relying instead on features that may be legally blocked by content providers.

The pre-mortem is clear: the device launches, labels refuse to enable wireless sharing due to piracy concerns, the ecosystem remains fragmented, and the marketing budget burns without converting users locked into the competitor's format. The "edge" cited is merely spending money and having a larger screen, which are not sustainable advantages against a platform lock-in. The risk of alienating partners while failing to secure content is a blocker, not a manageable risk. The plan fails the fit test because it fights a war on two fronts (hardware and content) without a decisive advantage in either, specifically undermining its own prior diplomatic capital regarding copy protection.

DECISION: NO-GO
