**Verdict: NO-GO**

The proposal fails the blocker test. It mandates an irreversible, high-blast change (one-way door) executed with zero validation and zero communication.

**Blockers:**
1.  **Unverified Core Assumption:** The plan assumes the "contemporary" aesthetic drives value more than "recognition." The prompt explicitly states the old square is "what the brand is recognized by." Destroying the primary recognition asset without testing contradicts the goal of a "mature" retailer, which relies on trust and consistency.
2.  **Execution Flaw:** Rolling out "at once with no advance announcement" eliminates the ability to manage narrative or mitigate backlash. Given the raised risk of "instant" social media backlash, this approach guarantees confusion before clarification can occur.
3.  **Missing Evidence:** The design was "not tested with customers." For a mature brand, alienating the loyal base (the stated risk) without data is negligence, not boldness.

**Risks:**
*   Immediate loss of brand equity and customer trust.
*   Viral backlash framing the brand as out-of-touch or arrogant.
*   Operational chaos across channels if the rollout encounters friction.

**Edge:**
The proposal offers no edge. "Looking contemporary" is a subjective goal achieved by many; destroying recognition is a unique disadvantage. Being first to confuse your customers is not a strategy.

**Analysis:**
The "better way" is a staged pilot or a soft launch with customer feedback loops. The cost of delay (waiting for testing) is negligible compared to the cost of a botched global rebrand. The pre-mortem is clear: the brand launches the new logo, loyalists revolt citing loss of identity, sales dip, and the company is forced to walk back the change, looking incompetent.

This plan bets the company's visual identity on an untested hypothesis while ignoring specific, known risks. It violates the principle that high-reversibility costs require high-certainty evidence. There is no evidence here, only assumption.

DECISION: NO-GO
