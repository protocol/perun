The claim is that a mass-market economy manufacturer can successfully penetrate the luxury segment by launching a distinct brand with a standalone dealer network and a single, under-priced flagship sedan.

Steelman: Creating a legally and operationally separate entity with unique branding and dealerships isolates the new luxury identity from the parent's economy reputation, allowing the vehicle to be judged on its own engineering merits while undercutting incumbents on price to gain initial traction.

However, critical blockers exist in the proposal as stated. First, the "single flagship model" strategy creates a fatal gap in the product line; luxury buyers expect a range of body styles and immediate availability upon brand launch. A single sedan cannot sustain a dealership network's profitability or justify the massive fixed costs of a separate retail footprint. Second, pricing "well below" established European leaders signals inferiority rather than value in the luxury psyche, potentially confirming the fear that buyers will not pay premium prices for an economy maker's output. The proposal admits development has "already cost heavily," suggesting sunk cost fallacy may be driving the decision rather than current market viability.

The "better way" analysis reveals a flaw in the "separate dealer network" mandate. Building physical stores from scratch in 1989 requires immense capital and time, delaying revenue while burning cash. A phased approach using select existing high-end partners or a hybrid sales model would reduce the break-even point. Furthermore, launching with only one model exposes the brand to catastrophic failure if that specific vehicle encounters quality issues or misses market taste; a multi-model launch or a platform-sharing strategy with a premium partner would mitigate this concentration risk.

The pre-mortem indicates the brand launches, dealers struggle to sell volume on a single model, the low price devalues the brand perception, and the parent company drags the new division down with it. The "edge" of being cheaper is not a sustainable luxury advantage without a proven heritage of quality, which the proposal admits is currently lacking. The risks of brand dilution and financial hemorrhage from underutilized dealers outweigh the potential upside of a single-model entry.

DECISION: NO-GO
