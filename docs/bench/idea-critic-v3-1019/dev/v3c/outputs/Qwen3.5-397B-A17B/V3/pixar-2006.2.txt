**Steelman:** Acquiring the studio secures the industry's most valuable animation pipeline, instantly replacing a failing internal unit and denying the asset to rivals, justifying the $7.4B premium through long-term franchise dominance rather than near-term film count.

**Attack:** The valuation relies entirely on the continuity of specific creative leaders and a single-film annual cadence. If the "culture clash" causes key talent to depart post-acquisition, the buyer pays $7.4B for a brand with no production engine, while diluting existing shareholders significantly via an all-stock deal. The "one film a year" model offers insufficient diversification; a single box-office failure post-merger would crater the combined stock price, making the debt-free but equity-heavy purchase disastrously expensive.

**Alternatives:**
1.  **Do Nothing:** Risk the studio partnering with a rival, cementing the conglomerate's animation decline.
2.  **Co-production Only:** Cheaper, but fails to secure exclusive control or fix the internal unit's structural failures.
3.  **Staged Earn-out:** Buy a minority stake first with options to acquire later based on performance; reduces upfront risk but may be rejected by the studio seeking full exit liquidity.

**Fit:** Aligns with the north star of dominating family entertainment but assumes the studio's culture can survive integration without losing its creative spark.

**Pre-mortem:** Two years post-deal, the founder leaves due to corporate interference, the next two films underperform, and the stock price halves, revealing the premium was paid for talent that refused to be managed.

**Decision Logic:**
*   **Blockers:** None definitively proven; risks are high but manageable if leadership is retained.
*   **Edge:** The conglomerate has the distribution scale to maximize the studio's hits globally, an asset the studio lacks alone.
*   **Kill Criteria:** If key creative leaders do not sign multi-year employment contracts concurrent with the deal, the acquisition must stop.

The strategic necessity of owning the premier animation IP factory outweighs the financial risk, provided the human capital is legally locked in. Without the studio, the conglomerate's animation division faces irrelevance.

DECISION: GO
