The claim is that replacing the flagship formula with a sweeter alternative, validated by blind taste tests, will reverse market share loss. The steelman: sensory preference drives purchase, so optimizing for taste in isolation maximizes volume.

However, the evidence contains a fatal flaw. The testing methodology (blind taste tests, surveys, focus groups) explicitly excluded the core variable: the removal of the original product. Consumers tested the *liquid*, not the *decision*. The proposal assumes taste is the sole driver of value, ignoring the "emotional attachment" and "brand heritage" risks explicitly raised. In this category, the brand identity is a feature as potent as sweetness. By withdrawing the original entirely, the plan converts a product preference into a relational betrayal.

The "better way" is clear: introduce the sweeter formula as a line extension (New Cola) while retaining the Original. This captures the taste-preference segment without alienating the heritage segment. The current plan fails the "do nothing" comparison because it risks active boycott and reputational damage that maintaining the status quo or extending the line does not.

The pre-mortem is straightforward: Launch day brings immediate backlash from loyalists who feel robbed of their choice. The media narrative shifts from "taste improvement" to "corporate arrogance." Bottlers face angry consumers with no alternative to offer within the brand family. The lack of a fallback product turns a manageable segmentation issue into an existential crisis. The cost of delay (waiting to test a two-product strategy) is negligible compared to the irreversible brand damage of a forced swap.

There is no edge here; being first to remove a legacy product is a liability, not an advantage. The "sweeter wins" data is true only in a vacuum that does not exist in the market. The plan relies on an assumed premise (taste = purchase) that contradicts the stated risk (emotional attachment).

Blockers: The testing methodology invalidates the core conclusion by hiding the withdrawal of the original.
Risks: Consumer boycott, brand erosion, bottler revolt.
Edge: None identified; the plan creates a unique vulnerability.

DECISION: NO-GO
