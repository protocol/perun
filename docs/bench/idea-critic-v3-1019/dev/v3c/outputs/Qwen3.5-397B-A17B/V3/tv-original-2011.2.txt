The claim is that commissioning two full seasons of a prestige drama without a pilot, based solely on viewing data, will succeed where traditional broadcasters require pilots. The steelman: existing data reveals a specific, underserved audience for this director/star combination, making a pilot an unnecessary expense that delays capturing subscriber growth and differentiating the brand from licensed-content competitors.

However, critical blockers exist. First, the "viewing data" from a service built on licensed content predicts interest in *other people's* shows, not necessarily original productions. There is no evidence the current user base will pay for or watch an unknown original property, regardless of attached talent. Second, the financial risk is extreme: committing to two full seasons (26 episodes) before a single frame is shot removes the industry-standard "kill point" after a pilot. If the show fails, the entire investment is sunk with no chance to pivot after episode one. Third, the distribution model (all-at-once release) is unproven for prestige drama; binge-watching may undermine the cultural conversation and long-term retention that weekly releases generate.

The proposal lacks a "go-if" condition or a kill criterion. It assumes the data translates directly to original content success without a bridge. A cheaper, safer alternative exists: commission a pilot or a limited initial order (e.g., 6 episodes) to validate the audience response and production quality before committing to 26 episodes. The current plan bets a "large share of the content budget" on an unverified hypothesis regarding original content viability and release cadence.

While the edge (data-driven greenlight) is innovative, it is currently theoretical. The lack of a pilot eliminates the primary mechanism for de-risking high-cost scripted television. The risk of alienating subscribers with a costly flop that consumes the budget for other content is too high given the unproven nature of the service as an original producer. The plan fails the blocker test because it ignores the fundamental difference between licensing data and original production viability.

DECISION: NO-GO
