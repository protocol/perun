The proposal asserts that a paid, mandatory, console-wide online service can succeed despite zero prior operational experience, low broadband adoption, and a market expectation of free play. The steelman is that a unified, high-quality network with voice chat and friends lists creates a defensible premium ecosystem that rivals lacking central coordination cannot match, turning infrastructure into a recurring revenue moat.

However, the skepticism is severe. The "better way" analysis reveals a critical flaw: the proposal demands a mandatory subscription in an environment where the primary constraint (broadband penetration) is not yet solved. Forcing a paid gate on a nascent market risks stalling software sales, which are the console's true profit engine. The "do nothing" or "publisher-led" alternatives avoid capping the user base while the market matures. The pre-mortem is clear: the service launches to a tiny fraction of users, developers refuse to support the extra cost/complexity for such a small audience, and the company bleeds cash on underutilized servers it doesn't know how to run.

The strongest attack is the mismatch between the "mandatory" nature of the service and the "few homes have broadband" reality. A paid wall further shrinks the addressable market to a vanishing point. The company has no edge in running hosting operations; this is a core competency gap, not a minor hurdle. The risks (operational inexperience, market readiness, price sensitivity) are not merely potential; they are stated facts that directly contradict the viability of a *mandatory* paid model *now*.

There is no named edge that overcomes the lack of broadband and the operational void. Being first to charge for a service few can use is not an advantage. The proposal fails the blocker test: the stated facts (low broadband, no ops experience, expectation of free) show the plan fails as proposed because the economics and adoption curves do not support a mandatory paid gate at this stage.

DECISION: NO-GO
