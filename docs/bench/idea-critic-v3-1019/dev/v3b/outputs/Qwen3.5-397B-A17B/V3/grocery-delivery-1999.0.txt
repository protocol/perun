**Steelman:** Secure dominant market share in 1999 by locking in real estate and infrastructure before competitors, betting that scale will eventually fix thin margins and operational inefficiencies.

**Blockers:**
1. **Unit Economics Unknown:** The proposal explicitly admits to scaling before learning unit economics. In a low-margin business, this guarantees that every additional order likely increases losses exponentially rather than linearly.
2. **Capital Misallocation:** Committing $1 billion to fixed assets (warehouses, trucks) based on a single metro's early data removes all flexibility to pivot when the model inevitably requires adjustment.
3. **Density Failure:** The model relies on high customer density for 30-minute slots to be profitable. Expanding to "over a dozen" cities simultaneously dilutes capital and operational focus, ensuring no single city reaches the critical density required for viability before funds run out.

**Risks:**
- Total capital exhaustion within 18 months due to burn rate on unused automation.
- Inability to service debt or raise follow-on funding once early operational failures become public.
- Technology or process obsolescence locked into expensive physical infrastructure.

**Edge:**
None identified. Being first to build the wrong model at scale is a liability, not an asset. No evidence suggests this specific team has an advantage in predicting grocery demand patterns without operating data.

**Better Way:**
Limit expansion to the current metro until positive unit economics are proven for three consecutive months. Use third-party logistics or smaller, manual fulfillment centers to test new cities before committing $1B to automation.

**Kill Criteria:**
Stop immediately if the current metro fails to achieve positive contribution margin per order within six months. Do not expand to a second city until the first is profitable on a variable cost basis.

**Verdict:**
This plan violates basic capital efficiency principles for low-margin industries. It substitutes hypothesis for evidence with irreversible, billion-dollar commitments. The lack of a proven operating model makes this a bet on luck, not strategy.

DECISION: NO-GO
