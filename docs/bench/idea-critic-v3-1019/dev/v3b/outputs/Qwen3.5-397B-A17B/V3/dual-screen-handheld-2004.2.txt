**Steelman:** Differentiate via novel interaction (dual-screen/touch) and price to expand the market beyond core gamers, rather than losing a spec war against a well-funded rival.

**Analysis:**
The proposal correctly identifies that competing directly on graphics against a well-funded rival with superior media features is a losing strategy for a company whose strength lies in handheld dominance, not raw power. Matching the rival leads to a "red ocean" of spec comparisons where the proposer is disadvantaged.

The "gimmick" risk is real but necessary; without it, the product is merely a weaker version of the competitor. The dual-screen approach creates a unique value proposition that cannot be directly compared on a spec sheet, forcing the market to evaluate new types of gameplay rather than pixel counts. Keeping the older handheld on sale mitigates the risk of alienating the core base who may resist the new form factor, while the lower price point addresses the cost concern of the second screen and targets the "broadened audience."

Developer confusion is a temporary hurdle solvable by strong first-party titles demonstrating the hardware's potential, which is a standard pattern for platform shifts. The "unproven" nature of touch games is precisely the opportunity to define a new genre before the rival can react. The cost addition of a second screen is offset by the strategic necessity of differentiation and the targeted lower price point relative to the high-end rival.

**Blockers:** None. The facts provided show a clear strategic divergence that leverages the proposer's market position while avoiding a direct hardware deficit.
**Risks:** Developer adoption lag and consumer perception of gimmickry. These are execution risks, not fundamental flaws in the strategic direction.
**Edge:** First-mover advantage in dual-screen interaction and a price advantage over the premium rival.

DECISION: GO
