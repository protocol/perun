The claim is that a data-backed, all-at-once release of a high-budget original series will succeed where traditional pilot testing and weekly releases are the norm. The steelman: proprietary viewing data reveals a specific, underserved demand for this director-star combination, making a pilot redundant and a binge release superior for retention.

However, critical blockers exist. First, the "data" is historical viewing of *licensed* content, not original productions; inferring success for a never-before-attempted format (straight-to-series, binge-drop) from this dataset is an unverified assumption, not evidence. Second, the cost represents a "large share" of the budget; a single failure could cripple the service, yet there is no phased approach (e.g., one season order) to mitigate this financial risk. Third, the "all-at-once" model has zero market precedent for prestige drama; the assumption that audiences prefer this over weekly anticipation is unproven and could negatively impact long-term subscription retention and cultural buzz.

The proposal fails the "cheaper alternative" test. A standard pilot or a single-season order would validate the creative team and the release format at a fraction of the cost, preserving capital if the hypothesis is wrong. The current plan bets the company on untested assumptions about production scalability and consumer behavior without a safety net. The "edge" cited is data that cannot logically predict the outcome of a fundamentally new product category and distribution method.

While the ambition is clear, the lack of a pilot removes the primary industry mechanism for de-risking creative failures, and the financial exposure is disproportionate to the verified evidence. The risks (financial ruin, format failure) are concrete, while the edge (data applicability) is theoretical. A "go-if" condition requiring a single-season commitment or a pilot episode would be prudent, but as proposed, the plan is reckless.

DECISION: NO-GO
