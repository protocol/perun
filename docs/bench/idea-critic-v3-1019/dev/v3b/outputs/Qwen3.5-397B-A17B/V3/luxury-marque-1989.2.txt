The claim is that a mass-market economy carmaker can successfully launch a separate luxury brand with a new dealer network and a single flagship sedan to compete with established European leaders.

**Steelman:** A distinct brand identity, decoupled from the parent's economy reputation, combined with superior engineering at a disruptive price point, can penetrate the luxury segment despite high entry costs and a limited initial lineup.

**Attack & Analysis:**
The primary blocker is brand perception; history suggests economy buyers do not transition to luxury, and luxury buyers reject economy origins. However, the proposal mitigates this via a *separate* brand and dealer network, creating a psychological firewall. The risk of a single-model lineup is acute but necessary for a focused flagship entry; it forces perfection on that one unit. The cost of the dealer network is a massive barrier to entry, yet it is also the mechanism that guarantees the premium customer experience required to validate the price point. Without it, the car remains an "expensive economy car."

The "sunk cost" of development is irrelevant to the go/no-go decision; only future ROI matters. The pre-mortem failure scenario is that the brand launches but remains perceived as "poor man's luxury," failing to attract the target demographic. This is countered by the pricing strategy: being "well below" incumbents reduces the risk premium for early adopters willing to try a newcomer.

**Edge:** The parent company's core competency in reliable, economical manufacturing provides a cost structure advantage that legacy luxury makers, bloated with overhead, cannot match. This allows high margins even at lower price points.

**Decision Logic:**
*   **Blockers:** None. The separation of brand and dealers directly addresses the reputation risk.
*   **Risks:** High capital expenditure, brand acceptance uncertainty, thin product line. These are managed by the pricing strategy and focused engineering.
*   **Kill Criteria:** If sales of the flagship do not reach 10,000 units in Year 1, or if brand perception surveys show >60% association with "economy," the project must pivot or cease expansion.

The proposal survives the attack because the structural separations (brand, dealers) neutralize the strongest counter-arguments regarding reputation and experience.

DECISION: GO
