The claim is that splitting the DVD and streaming businesses into separate brands, accounts, and billing systems will allow each to pursue optimal strategies despite a recent 60% price hike for the combined service.

**Skeptic:** The proposal ignores the immediate context: customers are already reeling from a massive price increase. Splitting the services now forces them into two separate logins and bills, compounding the friction and perceived cost. This looks less like strategic clarity and more like a forced breakup to hide the DVD decline, likely triggering mass churn. The loss of shared ratings degrades the value proposition for users who enjoy both formats.

**Better-way:** The alternative is to keep the unified brand but offer tiered pricing or a transitional "grandfathered" combined plan to stabilize the base. A single account with optional service toggles preserves data synergy while allowing distinct marketing messages. Doing nothing (reverting the price hike) might retain more revenue than a chaotic split.

**Kill-criteria:** If the split causes a drop in total active subscribers greater than the cost savings of separating the operations within Q4 2011, the strategy fails. The inability to migrate user queues and ratings automatically is a fatal execution flaw.

**Pre-mortem:** Six months post-split, the new DVD brand is unknown, streaming growth stalls due to bad press, and customers cancel both services out of frustration with the dual-billing complexity. The "strategic freedom" gained does not offset the brand equity destroyed.

**Edge:** There is no clear edge here; the move appears defensive rather than offensive. It solves an internal management problem by creating a massive external customer experience problem. The "declining" DVD business still generates cash flow that streaming needs; isolating it risks accelerating its death without a guaranteed streaming upside.

**Decision:** The proposal lacks a mitigation strategy for the compounded customer anger from the price hike and the forced separation. The operational friction (two logins, no shared data) outweighs the theoretical management benefits at this specific moment.

DECISION: NO-GO
