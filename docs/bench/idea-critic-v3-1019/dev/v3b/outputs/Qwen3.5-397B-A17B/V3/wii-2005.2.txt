**Steelman:** A third-place console maker can win by abandoning the graphics arms race to target non-gamers with low-cost, motion-controlled hardware and family software, creating a new market rather than fighting for the existing one.

**Blockers:** NONE. The proposal explicitly accepts thin margins and skeptical publishers as strategic trade-offs, not factual contradictions. The hardware limitations (no HD) are features of the cost strategy, not oversights. No internal data contradicts the existence of a casual audience; the risk is untested demand, not proven absence.

**Risks:** Third-party support may remain weak if publishers doubt the audience; the device may be perceived as a toy, limiting longevity; the casual market might not convert to hardware sales. These are execution risks inherent to blue-ocean strategies, not blockers that prove the plan fails as proposed.

**Edge:** As the third-place competitor, the maker has nothing to lose by ignoring the incumbent's rules (graphics power). Competitors are locked into high-cost, high-performance architectures serving hardcore gamers, making them unable to pivot to low-cost motion control without cannibalizing their existing premium lines. This asymmetry allows the proposer to own the "casual" category exclusively.

**Decision Logic:** The plan beats "do nothing" (continuing to lose the graphics war) and "follow the leader" (spending heavily to catch up on power). The cost of delay is high, as rivals could eventually replicate the motion concept once the market is proven. The "go-if" condition is implicit: launch quickly to validate the casual audience before competitors react. The kill criterion is clear: if family-friendly launch titles do not drive hardware attachment rates within the first two quarters, the strategy fails. However, since no current fact proves failure, and the strategic fit for a trailing player is sound, the recommendation stands.

DECISION: GO
