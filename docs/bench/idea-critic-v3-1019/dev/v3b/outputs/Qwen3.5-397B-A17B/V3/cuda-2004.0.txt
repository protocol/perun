**Steelman:** Embedding general-purpose compute capability in every consumer GPU creates a ubiquitous, low-cost supercomputer network, positioning the company as the foundational hardware provider for a future scientific computing revolution before competitors recognize the shift.

**Attack:** The proposal demands raising unit costs and diverting engineering resources from the core gaming rivalry to serve a non-existent market. In 2004, scientific users rely on hacked graphics APIs; there is no compiler, no standardized language, and no committed enterprise customer base. The "better way" is to wait for clear demand signals or partner with universities on specific workstation cards rather than subsidizing R&D across the entire mass-market volume. The cost of delay is minimal compared to the risk of bleeding margin in a cutthroat gaming race against a focused rival. Without a paying market, the "edge" is purely speculative theory, not a demonstrated asset. The pre-mortem is clear: the company burns cash on features gamers don't want, loses the graphics race due to distraction, and the scientific community adopts a different architecture once one finally emerges with proper software support.

**Blockers:** NONE (No factual contradiction in the proposal itself, only high risk).
**Risks:** High unit cost erosion, loss of focus in core gaming segment, zero immediate ROI, unproven software ecosystem adoption.
**Edge:** NONE stated (First-mover advantage is theoretical; no existing asset guarantees win).

The proposal fails the "beat do nothing" test. It bets the company's core profitability on a hypothetical future while ignoring the immediate existential threat of the gaming rival. A "go-if" condition would require at least one major scientific lab committing to fund the toolchain development, which is absent. As proposed, this is an unfunded mandate on every consumer, lacking a kill criterion or a phased pilot. It is a classic "build it and they will come" fallacy applied to expensive silicon.

DECISION: NO-GO
