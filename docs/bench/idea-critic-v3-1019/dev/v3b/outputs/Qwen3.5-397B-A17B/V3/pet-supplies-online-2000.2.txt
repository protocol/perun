The proposal describes a "blitzscale" strategy reliant on perpetual capital availability and negative unit economics to purchase market share. The steelman is that speed creates an unassailable monopoly before competitors can react, allowing future price hikes to recoup losses.

However, the attack reveals fatal structural flaws. First, the plan depends on selling products below cost while incurring high shipping expenses on heavy, low-margin goods. This creates a deeply negative contribution margin per unit; scaling volume accelerates cash burn rather than building value. Second, the strategy assumes capital markets will remain open indefinitely to fund these losses. The proposal itself admits this risk, yet the entire plan collapses if funding ceases even briefly. Third, buying a competitor while both parties burn cash on TV ads and below-cost pricing consolidates losses, not profits. There is no path to profitability described, only a race to see who runs out of money last.

The "edge" claimed is speed, but without a mechanism to eventually achieve positive unit economics, speed only ensures a faster crash. The pre-mortem is clear: the IPO fails due to investor skepticism over negative margins, or the company goes bankrupt within 18 months as capital markets tighten and the cash burn from TV ads and below-cost sales becomes unsustainable. The risks are not merely "high"; they are existential and inherent to the core mechanics of the plan.

A better way would be to demonstrate a path to positive unit economics on a subset of products or regions before attempting a national expansion and IPO. The current plan lacks any kill criteria or metrics to validate demand beyond paid acquisition, making it a gamble rather than a strategy. Since the proposal relies on assumptions (open capital markets, future monopoly pricing) that contradict current evidence (negative margins, high shipping costs) and offers no safeguard against the primary failure mode (running out of cash), it cannot be approved.

DECISION: NO-GO
