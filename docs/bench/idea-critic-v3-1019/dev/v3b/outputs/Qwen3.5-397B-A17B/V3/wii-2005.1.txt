Steelman: A third-place console maker can win by abandoning the graphics arms race to serve an untapped casual market with a low-cost, motion-controlled device, accepting lower hardware margins to drive massive software attachment rates.

**Blockers:** NONE. The proposal acknowledges thin hardware margins but relies on a volume-based software model, which is a known viable strategy in the industry. No factual contradiction exists in the premise that a non-HD device can function for casual gaming.

**Risks:** Third-party publisher skepticism, perception as a "toy," and the unproven nature of the motion interface for mass markets are significant. However, these are execution risks, not structural blockers. The lack of high-definition output is a deliberate constraint to maintain low cost, not an oversight.

**Edge:** As the current third-place holder, the company has little to lose by ignoring the hardcore segment dominated by rivals. Their specific advantage is the freedom to pivot entirely to a "blue ocean" of non-gamers without cannibalizing a dominant existing user base. Rivals are locked into high-cost HD development cycles, creating a opening for a cheaper, differentiated experience.

**Pre-mortem:** If this fails, it will be because the motion controller proves unintuitive for families or the software library fails to launch with a "must-have" title. The kill criterion is clear: if initial sell-through does not exceed the combined install base growth of rivals within 12 months, the strategy is invalid.

**Decision:** The proposal beats "do nothing" (continuing to lose the HD race) and "copy rivals" (spending heavily to catch up in a saturated segment). The cost of delay is high, as rivals are solidifying the HD standard now. While the risks are real, the potential upside of creating a new market category outweighs the certainty of stagnation in the current trajectory. The thin hardware margin is acceptable if it drives the necessary install base for software profits.

DECISION: GO
