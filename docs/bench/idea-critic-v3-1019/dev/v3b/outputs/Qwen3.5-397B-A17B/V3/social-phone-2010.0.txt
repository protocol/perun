The claim is that launching two social-networking phones on a new OS, via a single carrier with a mandatory high-cost data plan and no app store, will successfully capture the teenage market in 2010.

Steelman: Leveraging an acquired messaging brand allows a focused, carrier-subsidized entry into the teen segment, bypassing the complexity of a full app ecosystem initially.

However, the proposal fails the blocker test. The target demographic (teenagers) is highly price-sensitive, yet the mandatory data plan costs as much as a full smartphone plan, removing any cost advantage. Furthermore, the primary value proposition for this demographic in 2010 is access to existing social applications and games. Launching with **no application store** while rivals offer large catalogs creates an immediate utility deficit that a "messaging phone" brand cannot overcome. The decision to use a new in-house OS instead of the company's existing mobile platform fragments development resources and eliminates any chance of legacy app compatibility, increasing risk without adding user value.

The "sunk cost" of two years of development is a fallacy; continuing to invest in a product with a broken value proposition (high price, low utility) compounds the loss. The "better way" is to utilize the existing mobile platform to ensure app availability or delay launch until an app ecosystem is viable. The current plan offers no competitive edge; "strategic necessity" or sunk costs are not edges. The specific facts provided (high cost relative to teen budget, zero apps vs. rival catalogs) directly contradict the core requirement for market adoption.

Blockers: 
1. Price point (data plan) exceeds target demographic budget while offering less utility than competitors.
2. Zero app availability at launch contradicts the primary usage driver for the target segment (social networks).

Risks: Carrier exclusivity limits reach; new OS increases maintenance overhead.
Edge: None identified; acquired brand is insufficient to offset functional deficits.

This plan guarantees failure by offering a more expensive, less capable product to a discerning, trend-driven audience.

DECISION: NO-GO
