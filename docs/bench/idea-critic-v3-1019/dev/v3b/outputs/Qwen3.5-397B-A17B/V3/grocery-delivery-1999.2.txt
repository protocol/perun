Steelman: Aggressively building nationwide infrastructure now secures unbeatable scale and speed, deterring competitors before the market matures.

However, this claim collapses under basic arithmetic and historical precedent. The proposal commits $1 billion to fixed assets across a dozen cities while admitting zero knowledge of unit economics in the single launch city. Groceries operate on razor-thin margins; profitability relies entirely on high customer density and operational efficiency. Building "highly automated, very large" warehouses before validating demand in the first metro creates massive stranded assets if the model fails.

The "learn by doing" principle is violated. Entering new cities rapidly without a proven playbook in the first city guarantees that every new location will burn cash faster than the last. The 30-minute delivery promise requires extreme local density which cannot be manufactured by capital alone; it must be organically grown. If the first city fails to achieve positive unit economics, expanding to a dozen more simply multiplies the loss rate.

This is a classic "get big fast" error common in the 1999 dot-com bubble. The risks are not hypothetical; they are structural to the plan. Capital intensity becomes a liability, not a moat, when the underlying business model is unproven. A cheaper, safer alternative exists: validate the model in the first city, achieve positive unit economics, then replicate the success. The proposed plan bets the entire company on an unproven hypothesis with no off-ramp.

Blockers: The plan requires scaling to 12+ cities before knowing if the business works in one. This contradicts the fundamental rule of startup validation. No edge is presented other than speed, which is useless if the direction is wrong.
Risks: Total capital depletion within 18 months; inability to pivot due to sunk costs in specialized automation.
Edge: None identified; speed without validation is a disadvantage.

DECISION: NO-GO
