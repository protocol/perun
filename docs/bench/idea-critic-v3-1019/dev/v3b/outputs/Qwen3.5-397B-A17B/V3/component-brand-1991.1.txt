The claim is that a component manufacturer can drive demand by branding directly to end-users who cannot see the product. The steelman: making the invisible visible forces computer makers to adopt the chip to satisfy customer pull, creating a monopoly-like preference despite commodity similarities.

**Skeptic:** The premise relies on consumers caring about an internal component they cannot inspect. In 1991, no precedent exists for component branding; failure means burning a large budget on consumer confusion. However, the "clone" threat suggests computer makers are losing differentiation; they may welcome a brand that distinguishes their machines from generic clones, mitigating the resentment risk.

**Better-way:** Alternatives include trade-only marketing or price cuts. These fail to solve the core problem: clone makers copying part numbers. Only consumer pull breaks the "part number" commoditization. Doing nothing cedes the market to clones. The proposed co-op advertising model is superior because it leverages existing computer-maker ad spend rather than building a standalone media empire, reducing total cost while ensuring logo placement.

**Kill-criteria:** The plan fails if computer makers refuse to display the logo or if consumer inquiries do not translate to retailer requests. The "unprecedented" nature is a risk, not a blocker; the cost of delay is high as clones proliferate monthly. The edge is unique: no other component supplier is attempting this, offering first-mover advantage in defining the category. The budget share is high, but the alternative is irrelevance against clones.

**Blockers:** NONE. The risks (resentment, unproven concept) are real but do not contradict the logic that consumer pull is the only escape from part-number commoditization.
**Risks:** Wasted budget if consumers ignore the logo; channel conflict if major makers boycott.
**Edge:** First-mover ability to turn a commodity spec into a consumer brand, forcing retailers to stock compliant machines.

DECISION: GO
