The claim is that a closed, curated native app store will succeed despite the CEO's prior public stance that web apps are sufficient and despite existing rival ecosystems with open distribution.

**Steelman:** A single, company-run store with a 30% fee and strict review creates a trusted, high-quality ecosystem that protects device stability and brand polish, solving the security risks of open sideloading while offering developers monetization they lack in web apps.

**Attack:**
1.  **Strategic Contradiction:** The CEO recently argued web apps are sufficient. Launching native apps contradicts this core narrative without explained new evidence, risking credibility.
2.  **Competitive Disadvantage:** Rivals already have open ecosystems. Developers prefer open distribution; a 30% tax and strict review may deter early adoption compared to free, open alternatives.
3.  **Operational Bottleneck:** Reviewing every app creates a massive workload and a single point of failure. Rejection delays could kill developer momentum.
4.  **Carrier Friction:** Apps bypassing carrier services invite retaliation from the network operators essential for sales and connectivity.
5.  **Product Risk:** Untrusted code inherently threatens the "polish" the proposal aims to protect; a single malicious or buggy approved app breaks the promise.

**Better Way:** Continue pushing web apps (consistent with CEO vision) or allow sideloading with warnings (lower operational cost, faster ecosystem growth) rather than building a costly review bureaucracy.

**Kill Criteria:** If the review process cannot scale to handle 100+ apps/week within 3 months, or if top 50 developers refuse the 30% fee, the model fails.

**Verdict Logic:** The proposal lacks an "edge." It enters a market with open competitors using a slower, more expensive, and contradictory model. The "polish" argument is weak against the reality of review bottlenecks and the CEO's own recent dismissal of native apps. The strategic flip-flop and operational burden outweigh the potential revenue, especially without a unique hook to draw developers away from open platforms. The risk of carrier backlash and the high cost of building a review team make this a "no-go" in its current form without a pilot or a clear explanation for the CEO's reversal.

DECISION: NO-GO
