**Steelman:** Launching a high-end, mouse-driven computer at $10,000 in 1983 targets a niche willing to pay for superior usability despite performance and software gaps, betting on a future shift away from command-line interfaces.

**Blockers:**
1.  **Price Disparity:** At $10,000, the unit costs several times more than leading business PCs. Without a definitive productivity multiplier proven by existing software (which is scarce), the ROI calculation fails for standard office work.
2.  **Internal Cannibalization:** A cheaper machine with a similar interface is already in development internally. Launching the expensive version first undermines the upcoming cost-effective solution and confuses the sales channel.
3.  **Performance & Ecosystem:** Slow execution and little third-party software render the machine impractical for serious business tasks, regardless of interface innovation.

**Risks:**
*   Market rejection due to price-to-performance ratio.
*   Developer apathy due to low initial install base.
*   Strategic dilution by releasing a premium product before a mass-market ready alternative exists.

**Edge:**
The proposal claims an edge in "graphical interface and mouse," yet the existence of a cheaper internal machine with the *same* interface negates this as a unique differentiator justifying the 3x+ price premium. Fear of falling behind in GUI technology is not an edge if the implementation is slower and more expensive than competitors.

**Analysis:**
The proposal fails the "beat do nothing" and "beat alternatives" tests. The "do nothing" option allows the cheaper internal project to mature, offering the same interface benefit at a viable price point. The "cheaper alternative" (the internal project) reaches the same upside (GUI adoption) with significantly lower financial risk and broader market accessibility. The current plan commits real money to a product with no software ecosystem, poor speed, and a prohibitive price tag, while actively working against the company's own upcoming strategic asset. There is no stated fact showing why this specific expensive configuration can win against established cheaper PCs or the internal cheaper alternative.

**Kill Criteria:**
If the company cannot demonstrate that office workers will pay a 300% premium for a slower machine with no software over existing options, the launch must stop. The leading metric would be pre-orders from enterprise clients at the $10k price point; absent these, the project is a sunk cost waiting to happen.

DECISION: NO-GO
