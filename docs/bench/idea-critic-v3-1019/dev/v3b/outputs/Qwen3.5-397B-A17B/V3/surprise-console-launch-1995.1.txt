**Verdict: REVISE**

**Steelman:** Launching in May 1995 captures the "first-to-market" mindshare before the rival's September arrival, securing early adopters despite higher costs and limited inventory.

**Attack:** The proposal fails the "Edge" test. Being first means nothing if the product cannot be played. Shipping with "few games" and a machine that is "hard to program" creates a negative feedback loop: early buyers encounter a barren library, generating bad word-of-mouth that poisons the brand before the mass market even arrives. The $399 price point ($100 above the rival) is indefensible without a robust software lineup to justify the premium. Retailer retaliation is a secondary concern; the primary blocker is offering a broken value proposition (high price, low utility) to the very users whose enthusiasm is required to survive the competitor's launch.

**Better Way:** Do not launch the full console in May. Instead, use the May trade show for a "technology showcase" or a limited "developer preview" kit. This secures the press narrative of being "first" and "advanced" without exposing the brand to consumer backlash over missing games. Delay the consumer shipment to align with a critical mass of software titles, even if it means launching closer to the competitor. A two-door strategy: open the door to press/developers in May, but keep the consumer door closed until the library is sufficient to justify the $399 price.

**Kill Criteria:** If the number of playable, retail-ready titles at launch is fewer than five, the consumer launch must be delayed. The leading metric is "software attach rate" in the first 30 days; if it falls below 1.5 games per console, the strategy is failing.

**Decision:** The current plan guarantees a high-price, low-content failure that cedes the market to the rival on quality grounds. The proposal must be revised to decouple the announcement from the general consumer shipment.

DECISION: NO-GO
