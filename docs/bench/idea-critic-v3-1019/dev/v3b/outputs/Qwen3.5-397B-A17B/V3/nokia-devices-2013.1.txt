**Steelman:** Acquiring the struggling handset maker secures the primary hardware channel for the minority OS, preventing its defection to the dominant rival and enabling tight integration to improve user experience.

**Attack:** The core failure mode is misdiagnosis. The proposal assumes hardware ownership solves an ecosystem deficit. However, the market leader's dominance stems from network effects (apps and developers), not just hardware variety. Buying a declining hardware division with falling sales does not automatically attract developers; it merely adds a fixed cost base to a software company. The €5.4 billion price tag consumes capital that could otherwise subsidize developer incentives or marketing. Furthermore, integrating a large, culturally distinct hardware workforce poses massive execution risks that could distract from the primary software mission. If the app gap remains post-acquisition, the company owns a shrinking hardware business without solving the root cause of user abandonment.

**Alternatives:**
1.  **Do Nothing:** Allow the manufacturer to switch platforms. This risks immediate loss of the primary device vector but preserves cash.
2.  **Strategic Partnership:** Deepen ties via revenue sharing without ownership, avoiding integration drag.
3.  **Acquisition (Proposed):** High cost, high integration risk, unproven ability to fix the app gap.

**Fit & Pre-mortem:** In a two-horse race where the rival has "most" apps, owning the hardware is a defensive moat that may become a money pit. If the acquisition fails, the company is left with obsolete inventory and a demoralized hardware division, having spent its war chest on a symptom rather than the disease (lack of apps). The "edge" is unclear; no asset guarantees developer migration simply because the OS and phone are under one roof.

**Blockers:** None explicitly proven in the text, but the strategic logic is fragile.
**Risks:** Massive integration failure, continued app drought, alienation of other handset partners.
**Edge:** Prevents immediate defection of the main hardware partner.

The plan bets on vertical integration solving a horizontal network effect problem. Without a concrete mechanism to force developer adoption via hardware ownership, this is a costly gamble on a declining asset. However, if the manufacturer switching to the rival is an existential threat that guarantees immediate irrelevance, the acquisition acts as a desperate survival brake. Given the "low single-digit" share, status quo is likely fatal.

DECISION: GO
