**Steelman:** Replacing a confusing coupon model with simple everyday pricing and branded shops will modernize the brand, restore margin integrity, and attract a broader demographic, replicating a proven electronics retail success.

**Attack:** The proposal ignores the specific behavioral conditioning of the existing customer base. These customers are not just accustomed to discounts; their purchasing volume is historically tied to them ("most sales... sold at a discount"). Removing the primary purchase trigger without a transitional bridge or validation risks an immediate, catastrophic drop in foot traffic and revenue. Furthermore, the operational complexity of rebuilding stores into branded shops while simultaneously altering the core pricing psychology creates a compound execution risk. The lack of a pilot is a fatal flaw; what worked for high-velocity electronics may fail for discretionary apparel/home goods where coupon hunting is a sport. The "edge" cited is the CEO's past success, but context mismatch (electronics vs. department store) invalidates this as a guarantee.

**Alternatives:** A staged approach (pilot in 10% of stores) or a hybrid model (reduced coupons + everyday low price) would test the elasticity of demand before burning capital on physical renovations. "Do nothing" preserves current cash flow while allowing time for data collection.

**Pre-mortem:** Six months post-launch, sales have collapsed 40%. Loyal customers feel alienated by the loss of "deals," and new customers have not arrived in sufficient numbers to offset the loss. The company runs out of cash funding the store rebuilds before the brand perception shifts.

**Blockers:** None explicitly proven false yet, but the absence of demand validation for a non-discount model in this specific demographic is a critical gap.
**Risks:** Total alienation of core base; cash flow collapse during renovation; context mismatch of CEO's prior success.
**Edge:** CEO's track record (weak due to sector difference).

**Decision Logic:** The plan bets the entire company on an unproven hypothesis for this specific market segment, ignoring historical data that proves discounts drive volume. The lack of a pilot makes the risk unacceptably high. The cheaper, safer alternative (pilot testing) must be executed before a full chain-wide rollout.

DECISION: NO-GO
