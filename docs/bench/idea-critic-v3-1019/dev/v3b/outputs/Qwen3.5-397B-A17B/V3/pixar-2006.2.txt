The claim is that acquiring the studio for $7.4 billion is the optimal path to secure future hits and revitalize the conglomerate's animation division.

**Steelman:** Buying the studio is the only way to prevent a rival from securing the talent, instantly replacing a failing internal unit with a proven hit-maker, and aligning incentives so creative leaders prioritize the conglomerate's long-term portfolio over short-term deal economics.

**Attack:** The price represents an extreme premium for a low-volume asset (one film/year), creating massive dependence on a few individuals whose departure would leave the buyer with worthless goodwill. The "culture clash" risk is understated; inserting external leaders over an existing internal unit often triggers talent exodus from both sides. Furthermore, making the founder a major shareholder creates a governance conflict where one individual holds disproportionate sway over the conglomerate's strategic direction.

**Better Way:** Instead of a full buyout, structure a profit-sharing joint venture or a long-term distribution exclusivity deal with first-look rights. This secures the content pipeline and blocks rivals without the $7.4 billion balance sheet risk or the integration trauma of a total merger. A staged acquisition with earn-outs tied to specific film performance would also mitigate the valuation risk.

**Kill Criteria:** If the studio releases two consecutive box-office failures post-acquisition, or if the key creative leaders depart within 18 months, the thesis collapses. The deal assumes perpetual creative dominance, which is historically rare in animation.

**Decision:** The proposal fails the "cheaper alternative" test. The risks of overpaying for a volatile, low-volume asset and the governance complications of empowering a single founder outweigh the benefits when a structured partnership could achieve similar content security. The lack of a "go-if" condition regarding valuation caps or earn-outs makes this an irresponsible all-in bet.

DECISION: NO-GO
