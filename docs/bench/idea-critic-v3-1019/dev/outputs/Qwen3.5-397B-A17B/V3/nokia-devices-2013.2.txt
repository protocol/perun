**Steelman:** Acquiring the struggling handset maker secures the primary hardware channel for the minority OS, preventing its defection to the dominant rival and buying time to solve the app ecosystem deficit through vertical integration.

**Attack:** The core failure mode is misdiagnosis. The proposal assumes hardware ownership fixes an app-ecosystem problem. However, developers flock to platforms with users, not specific hardware brands. If the acquired division's sales are already falling due to the lack of apps, buying the sinkhole does not stop the leak; it merely adds the cost of a failing manufacturing operation to the software company's balance sheet. The €5.4 billion price tag drains capital that could otherwise subsidize developers or marketing. Furthermore, integrating a large, culturally distinct hardware workforce often slows decision-making, precisely when speed is needed to catch the market leader. The "edge" here is defensive (stopping a switch) rather than offensive (creating unique value), and history shows that defensive acquisitions in fast-moving software markets often result in owning a shrinking asset while the platform war is lost on merit.

**Better Way:** Instead of a full acquisition, a tight strategic partnership with exclusive licensing or subsidized production could secure the hardware channel without the massive integration risk and fixed costs. Alternatively, investing the €5.4 billion directly into a developer fund to bridge the app gap might address the root cause (lack of software) rather than the symptom (hardware switching).

**Kill Criteria:** If the acquired division's market share continues to drop post-integration despite OS exclusivity, or if developer adoption rates do not increase within 18 months, the thesis fails. The risk of cultural clash and distraction is high.

**Verdict:** The proposal conflates controlling hardware with solving a network effect problem. Without a concrete plan to make the hardware profitable or instantly attractive to developers beyond mere availability, this acquisition likely turns a software challenge into a hardware turnaround crisis. The falling sales indicate the brand is already damaged; buying it assumes the OS alone can reverse consumer preference, which is unproven.

DECISION: NO-GO
