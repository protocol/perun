The proposal attempts to pivot from a hardware specification war to a novel interaction model. The steelman is that competing directly on graphics against a well-funded rival with superior media features is a losing battle for a company already dominating the handheld space; therefore, the only path to victory is to change the definition of the product entirely.

However, the claim faces immediate blockers under the current constraints. The proposal relies on "lower price" while introducing "two screens" and "touch-sensitive" technology. In 2004, touch screens and dual-display setups were premium, power-hungry components. Adding a second screen and a touch digitizer inherently increases the Bill of Materials (BOM) compared to a single-screen device. Without verified evidence that the cost of these specific components has dropped sufficiently to undercut a rival focusing on standard, albeit stronger, graphics, the "lower price" claim contradicts the "unusual hardware" reality. You cannot add complexity and reduce cost simultaneously without a specific, unproven manufacturing breakthrough or heavy subsidy.

Furthermore, the risk of developer confusion is not merely a "risk" but a potential fatal flaw. A dual-screen touch interface requires a fundamentally different software architecture than existing handhelds. If the installed base of developers cannot easily port games, the library will remain thin. A handheld without software is a gimmick, regardless of price. The proposal admits touch-screen games are "unproven"; betting the company's next flagship on an unproven interaction model, while simultaneously claiming it will be cheaper than a proven high-end rival, lacks a credible edge. The "edge" cited is avoiding a spec war, but the proposed solution introduces higher execution risk and likely higher unit costs without a guaranteed software ecosystem to drive adoption.

The pre-mortem is clear: the device launches at a higher price point than anticipated due to dual-screen costs, developers ignore it due to the unique architecture, and it is dismissed as an expensive toy rather than a gaming platform. The proposal fails to demonstrate how the "lower price" is achievable given the added hardware complexity.

DECISION: NO-GO
