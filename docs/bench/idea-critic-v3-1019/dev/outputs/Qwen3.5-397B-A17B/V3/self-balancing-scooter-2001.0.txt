The claim is that launching a $5,000 self-balancing transporter with 40,000-unit capacity in 2001 will succeed based on inventor reputation and publicity. The steelman posits that high visibility and novelty will overcome regulatory ambiguity and price resistance, creating a new urban mobility category.

However, the proposal fails the blocker test. The "unclear legal status" on sidewalks and roads is not a mere risk; it is a fundamental barrier to the product's primary use case. Without a defined legal framework, the device cannot be legally operated in the very spaces it is designed for, rendering the 40,000-unit factory capacity immediately unusable for individual consumers. Furthermore, the high price ($5,000 in 2001 is equivalent to roughly $8,500 today) combined with "unproven uses beyond institutional buyers" and social stigma ("riders may feel conspicuous") suggests demand is vastly overstated. The proposal relies on "heavy publicity" as an edge, but publicity is not a product advantage or a solved distribution channel; it cannot manufacture a market where legal and utility barriers exist.

The alternative of "do nothing" or waiting for regulatory clarity and institutional validation is superior. Launching now invites immediate regulatory crackdowns, unsold inventory, and brand damage. The "edge" cited is merely being first with an unproven, legally precarious product. A cheaper, staged approach involving pilot programs with institutional buyers to resolve legal status and prove utility is missing. The current plan commits to massive fixed costs (factory size) before validating the core assumption that individuals can and will legally use the device.

Blockers: Legal ambiguity prevents primary use; price exceeds proven demand; no institutional validation.
Risks: Social stigma, limited utility, regulatory bans.
Edge: NONE (Publicity and fame are not structural advantages against legal bans).

DECISION: NO-GO
