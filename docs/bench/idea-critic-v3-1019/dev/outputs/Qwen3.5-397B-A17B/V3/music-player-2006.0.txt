The proposal attempts to dislodge a dominant incumbent by attacking its strongest fortifications: ecosystem lock-in and content licensing. The "steelman" argument is that superior hardware (larger screen) and a unified wireless ecosystem will lure users away from the market leader. However, the critical failure point is the abandonment of the copy-protection standard previously championed by the company. This alienates existing partners (labels and device makers) whose content is essential for the store's viability. Without label support, the "larger screen" and "wireless sharing" are features for an empty library.

The "do nothing" or "partner" alternative is superior: leveraging the existing ecosystem rather than fracturing it. The proposal admits to being late to market with no price advantage, relying entirely on a marketing budget to overcome network effects—a historically low-probability strategy in platform markets. The risk of label restrictions on sharing is not merely a risk; it is a near-certain blocker given the industry's stance in 2006. The plan lacks a credible path to secure content rights after undermining the very protection standards labels demanded.

The "edge" claimed is hardware superiority and marketing spend. Neither constitutes a sustainable competitive advantage against a entrenched platform with exclusive content deals. Hardware specs are easily copied; marketing cannot buy a network effect if the content pipeline is severed by partner retaliation. The proposal fails the blocker test: the contradiction between dropping copy-protection and needing label support creates an unsolvable dependency loop. The plan assumes labels will ignore the company's previous advocacy for strict DRM to support a new, looser standard, which contradicts the stated risk of label restriction.

There is no viable kill criterion that saves this specific approach because the core strategy (abandoning DRM to enable sharing) directly triggers the primary failure mode (loss of content). A "go-if" condition cannot be met because the prerequisite (label buy-in) is negated by the proposal's own mechanism.

DECISION: NO-GO
