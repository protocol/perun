**Claim:** Launch a sporty, customizable coupe/convertible on existing compact underpinnings with aggressive marketing to target young postwar buyers.

**Steelman:** This plan leverages sunk R&D costs to create a high-margin "halo" product that captures an emerging demographic segment without the capital expense of a clean-sheet platform, using customization to mask economy origins.

**Analysis:**
The "unproven demand" risk is mitigated by the sheer size of the baby boom cohort entering the market; even a small conversion rate yields volume. The "cheap look" risk is directly addressed by the proposed long-hood/short-deck styling and extensive options list, which psychologically decouples the car from its economy donor. The "heavy spend" risk is a two-way door: if the launch fails, the brand absorbs the marketing cost but retains the tooling for future iterations; if it succeeds, the payoff dwarfs the ad spend.

**Alternatives:**
1.  *Do nothing:* Cede the youth market to competitors, risking brand irrelevance as the demographic shifts.
2.  *Clean-sheet sports car:* Prohibitive cost and time-to-market; misses the immediate demographic wave.
3.  *Minor styling tweaks to existing sedan:* Fails to signal "sporty," likely resulting in mediocre sales.

**Edge:** The proposal uniquely combines low marginal production cost (shared parts) with high perceived value (styling/options), creating a favorable unit economics profile that competitors with dedicated sports platforms cannot match on price.

**Kill Criteria & Metrics:**
If pre-orders or first-quarter sales do not cover the variable cost of the launch campaign within six months, halt further advertising spend and evaluate platform reuse. The leading metric is "options take-rate" to confirm the customization strategy is driving margin.

**Verdict:**
The plan transforms a potential liability (an economy platform) into an asset by targeting a specific, growing demographic with a high-margin product. The risks are manageable and explicitly countered by the proposal's own mechanics (styling, options). The cost of delay exceeds the cost of failure.

DECISION: GO
