The proposal attempts to mitigate backlash from a recent 60% price hike by fracturing the user experience. The steelman argues that separating the declining DVD asset from the growth-oriented streaming unit allows distinct strategic focus and management accountability. However, the timing is catastrophic. Executing a brand split, separate billing, and dual-login architecture immediately after a massive price increase compounds customer friction. The "confusion" risk is not merely operational; it is existential. Customers perceiving the price hike as a pretext to force a cumbersome separation will likely churn entirely rather than manage two accounts.

The "better way" analysis reveals a fatal flaw: the proposal offers no upside to the customer, only administrative overhead. Splitting recommendations destroys cross-pollination data that could sustain DVD engagement while streaming scales. The rationale that the DVD business needs "different management" does not require a consumer-facing brand schism; internal restructuring achieves this without alienating the user base. The cost of delay is low; the cost of immediate execution is high. The pre-mortem is clear: customers interpret the split as a hidden fee or a degradation of service value, leading to mass cancellation.

There is no edge here. Being first to split a service post-hike is not an advantage; it is a provocation. The proposal relies on the assumption that customers will tolerate increased friction for the sake of corporate strategy, which contradicts basic retention logic during a pricing crisis. The strongest attack—that this move validates customer anger regarding the price hike rather than soothing it—survives because the plan adds complexity exactly when simplicity is needed to justify higher costs. The lack of shared data further weakens the product's overall value proposition. This is a solution to an internal accounting problem that creates a fatal external product problem.

DECISION: NO-GO
