Steelman: Launching four months early preempts the competitor's mindshare and secures shelf space before the holiday rush, accepting limited inventory as a premium "early adopter" tax.

However, the proposal fails the blocker test on three verified facts provided:
1.  **Economic Blocker:** At $399, the unit is $100 (25%) more expensive than the rival. In the mass-market console sector, this price gap is prohibitive without a corresponding library advantage, which explicitly does not exist here.
2.  **Supply Chain Blocker:** Shipping to "only a few retailers" while excluding others guarantees retaliation from major chains. Without full distribution, the hardware cannot reach the critical mass required to justify the premium price or attract third-party developers.
3.  **Product Blocker:** The machine is "hard to program for 3D," and developers were not alerted. Launching with "few games" on a difficult architecture ensures negative word-of-mouth. Early adopters buy software ecosystems, not empty hardware shells. A bad first impression with core gamers is irreversible before the competitor even launches.

The "Edge" is nonexistent. Being first with an overpriced, under-supported product is not a strategic advantage; it is a self-inflicted wound that hands the competitor a clear target to undercut on price and outperform on content. The risks are not merely potential; they are certain outcomes based on the proposal's own constraints. The cost of delay (waiting until September) is lower than the cost of a botched launch that alienates retailers and gamers permanently.

Blockers: Price premium without content justification; partial distribution ensuring retailer hostility; immature developer support on complex hardware.
Risks: Brand damage, developer abandonment, inventory glut.
Edge: NONE. First-mover disadvantage applies here.
