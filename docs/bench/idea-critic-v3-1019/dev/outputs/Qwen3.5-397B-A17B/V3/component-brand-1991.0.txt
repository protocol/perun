**Steelman:** Transform an invisible component into a consumer demand driver by subsidizing OEM advertising, forcing buyers to specify the brand, thereby locking out clones despite identical part numbers.

**Attack:**
1.  **Unprecedented Channel:** Component manufacturers do not advertise to end-users. If buyers cannot distinguish chips, they will not ask for specific brands, rendering the "pull" strategy ineffective.
2.  **Channel Conflict:** Computer makers may resent ceding brand control or adhering to strict ad guidelines, potentially leading to boycotts or half-hearted participation.
3.  **Budget Drain:** Diverting a large share of the marketing budget to subsidies reduces funds for R&D or direct sales support. If sales do not rise immediately, the program becomes an unsustainable cost center.
4.  **Measurement Lag:** Benefits may not appear in sales data quickly enough to justify the spend before the budget is exhausted.

**Better Ways:**
*   *Do Nothing:* Maintain status quo; risk losing share to clones as differentiation erodes.
*   *Direct OEM Incentives:* Pay manufacturers per unit sold rather than for ads, avoiding consumer confusion and channel friction.
*   *Technical Certification:* Create a visible "certified" seal for systems meeting performance standards, avoiding the cost of a full consumer campaign.

**Fit & Pre-mortem:**
The strategy assumes consumers care about internal components, which is unproven. If the campaign launches and buyers remain indifferent, the company burns cash with no leverage over OEMs. The "one-way door" risk is low (campaigns can stop), but the financial bleed is high.

**Edge:**
The only edge is the *potential* to create a category where none exists, making the chip the default choice. However, the proposal admits benefits "may not show in sales," indicating a lack of concrete evidence that consumer demand can be manufactured for an invisible product. The risks (unprecedented approach, high cost, channel resentment) are immediate and factual; the upside is speculative. Without a pilot or guaranteed OEM buy-in, the plan bets the marketing budget on an unverified consumer behavior.

**Blockers:** None explicitly fatal yet, but the lack of proven consumer interest in components is a massive gap.
**Risks:** High budget waste, OEM backlash, zero ROI.
**Edge:** Potential category creation (speculative).

The proposal relies on creating demand for a product users currently ignore, using an untested channel, at high cost, with admitted uncertainty on results. This fails the "must beat do nothing" test without a smaller-scale validation.

DECISION: NO-GO
