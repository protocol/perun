Steelman: Committing now secures a decade-long lead in efficiency technology before regulators force the issue, turning unproven tech into a defensible moat.

However, the proposal fails the blocker test. The plan relies on a "bold public schedule" for 1997 while admitting battery durability is "unproven" and the power-split transmission is "novel." In automotive engineering, these are not mere risks; they are known failure points that guarantee missed deadlines or catastrophic warranty exposure if rushed. The "cheap fuel" and "no demand signal" facts confirm the market will not pay the premium required to absorb the inevitable early-adopter costs and reliability glitches.

Better-way: The "do nothing" option preserves capital until battery chemistry matures. A superior alternative is a limited technical pilot or leasing program to gather durability data without binding the corporation to a mass-production cliff-edge. This captures the learning upside without the binary risk of a full launch.

Pre-mortem: It is 1998. The launch was delayed two years due to battery yield issues, or the cars were recalled en masse for transmission failures. The brand is associated with unreliable "experimental" tech, and competitors entered the market later with cheaper, proven components, stealing the narrative of "reliable innovation." The cost of delay is negligible compared to the cost of a failed flagship launch.

Edge: NONE. Being first with unproven tech in a market with no demand is not an edge; it is suicide. "Strategic necessity" is not an edge when the underlying physics and economics are not yet viable. The proposal offers no asset, cost advantage, or proven demand to offset the massive execution risk.

Blockers:
1. Unproven battery durability contradicts the requirement for mass-production reliability.
2. No demand signal + cheap fuel = negative unit economics at launch scale.
3. Novel transmission + fixed public date = high probability of public failure.

Risks: Warranty insolvency, brand damage from reliability issues, capital waste on immature supply chains.

This is a one-way door with a high probability of failure. The plan must be revised to decouple the public commitment from the technical readiness.

DECISION: NO-GO
