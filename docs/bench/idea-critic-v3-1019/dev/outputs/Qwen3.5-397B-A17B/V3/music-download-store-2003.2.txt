**Steelman:** A trusted hardware vendor can monetize piracy by offering a legal, convenient, per-song alternative at a fixed price, leveraging exclusive hardware integration to drive ecosystem lock-in despite higher unit costs than free.

**Attack & Analysis:**
The primary blocker hypothesis is that consumers will not pay $0.99 when free options exist. However, free file-sharing carries high friction (search time, virus risk, legal fear, poor metadata). The proposal addresses this by selling *convenience* and *safety*, not just files. The "own computers only" restriction is not a bug but a feature: it creates a seamless hardware-software loop that rivals cannot easily replicate without their own devices.

Regarding margins: while per-song margin is thin, the strategic value lies in selling the high-margin portable player. The store is a loss leader or break-even utility to move hardware. The risk of labels withdrawing licenses is mitigated by the vendor's brand prestige and the labels' desperate need for a revenue model to replace declining CD sales.

The subscription rival strategy fails the "ownership" psychological test prevalent in 2003; users prefer owning a curated library over renting access, especially given bandwidth limitations of the era. The "do nothing" option guarantees ceding the digital music future to chaotic, unmonetized piracy.

**Edge:** The vendor controls the entire user experience from purchase to playback, ensuring reliability that fragmented PC-based pirates cannot match. This vertical integration is the unique asset.

**Kill Criteria:** If major labels refuse licensing within 6 months, or if attachment rate (songs bought per device) falls below 5 songs/device in the first quarter, the model fails.

**Verdict:** The plan converts a threat (piracy) into a hardware sales engine by solving the usability gap of illegal downloading. The risks are manageable given the strategic necessity of defining the digital music standard.

DECISION: GO
