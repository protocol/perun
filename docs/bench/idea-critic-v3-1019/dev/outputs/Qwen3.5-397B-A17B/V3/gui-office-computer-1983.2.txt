**Steelman:** Launching a high-end, mouse-driven personal computer at $10,000 in 1983 targets a niche of early-adopter enterprises willing to pay a premium for superior usability, betting that the graphical interface creates a defensible moat against text-based competitors.

**Attack & Analysis:**
The proposal fails the "cheaper alternative" and "market fit" tests immediately.
1.  **Price Dislocation:** At $10,000, the unit costs several times more than leading business PCs. In 1983, this price point exceeds the threshold for mass office deployment, restricting the total addressable market to a tiny fraction of potential buyers.
2.  **Performance Deficit:** The machine runs slowly. For office workers, speed is a primary productivity metric. A slow, expensive machine offers a negative ROI compared to faster, cheaper terminals or existing PCs.
3.  **Ecosystem Void:** "Little third-party software" means the hardware has no utility beyond basic functions. Business adoption relies on specific applications (spreadsheets, word processing); without them, the hardware is a paperweight.
4.  **Internal Cannibalization:** A cheaper machine with a similar interface is already in development internally. Launching the expensive, slower version first destroys the value of the upcoming internal project and confuses the sales channel. The "do nothing" option (waiting for the cheaper internal model) offers the same interface upside with better cost/performance metrics.

**Blockers:**
*   **Fact:** The price is several times higher than competitors while offering lower speed.
*   **Fact:** Lack of software renders the advanced interface useless for most business tasks.
*   **Fact:** An internal substitute exists that solves the interface need at a lower cost.

**Edge:** NONE. Being first with a slow, expensive, software-starved product is not an advantage; it is a liability that cements a reputation for poor value. The "strategic necessity" of having a GUI does not justify launching a flawed implementation when a better one is imminent internally.

**Verdict:**
The plan commits real money to a product that cannot compete on price, performance, or utility. The existence of a cheaper internal alternative makes this launch actively destructive to the company's broader strategy. There is no kill criterion that saves this specific configuration; the product itself must change before any go-market decision.

DECISION: NO-GO
