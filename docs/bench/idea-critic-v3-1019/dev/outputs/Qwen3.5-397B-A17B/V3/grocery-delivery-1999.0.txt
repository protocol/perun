The claim is that a pre-revenue grocery startup should commit $1B to automated warehouses across a dozen cities before validating unit economics in its single launch market. The steelman argues that speed and infrastructure scale are the only defenses against incumbents, aiming to lock in density before competitors react.

However, this plan fails the blocker test immediately. The proposal explicitly admits "no operating track record" and "low-margin" dynamics while proposing an irreversible, capital-intensive expansion. In 1999, internet penetration and online adoption rates were insufficient to guarantee the customer density required for 30-minute grocery delivery profitability. The "edge" cited is merely being first and well-funded; size and speed are not edges when the underlying model (30-minute slots at low margins) is unproven. History shows that similar ventures from this era collapsed due to the exact mismatch of high fixed costs and unverified demand.

The "better way" is obvious: validate the model in the first city, prove positive unit economics, and then scale. A cheaper, safer alternative exists: lease smaller, manual fulfillment centers to test delivery density and slot adherence without billion-dollar sunk costs. The cost of delay (waiting to learn) is negligible compared to the cost of failure (bankruptcy from over-expansion).

This is a one-way door decision with catastrophic downside. The pre-mortem is clear: the company runs out of cash because the anticipated volume in new cities fails to materialize quickly enough to cover the massive depreciation and operational costs of automated warehouses. The risks listed (capital intensity, thin margins) are not just risks; they are active blockers when combined with zero evidence of success. There is no fact in the proposal showing why this specific team can succeed where the physics of low-margin logistics usually forbid it.

Blockers: The plan commits irreversible capital before validating the core hypothesis (unit economics), contradicting the stated lack of track record.
Risks: Demand uncertainty, margin compression, execution complexity.
Edge: NONE. Being first with an unproven, costly model is not an advantage.

DECISION: NO-GO
