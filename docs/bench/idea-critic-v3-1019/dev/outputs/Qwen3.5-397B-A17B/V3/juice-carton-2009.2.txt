**Steelman:** A bold, minimalist redesign modernizes the brand, signaling premium quality to attract new demographics while retaining core product value, justified by a massive advertising push to override old cues.

**Attack:** The proposal ignores the primary failure mode: shelf recognition. For a loyal, older demographic, the "orange with straw" is not decoration; it is the functional identifier. Removing it creates a "invisible product" risk. The plan relies entirely on advertising to retrain muscle memory instantly upon a nationwide, one-day switchover. This is a one-way door with catastrophic downside: if recognition fails even partially, sales drop immediately, and the product cannot be "found" to recover. Advertising budgets often fail to compensate for broken shelf cues in CPG (Consumer Packaged Goods). The "vertically rotated name" further reduces readability at a glance. There is no pilot, no A/B test in select regions, and no phased rollout to validate the new cue's efficacy before committing national inventory. The cost of a failed nationwide launch (unsold inventory, lost shelf space, brand confusion) vastly exceeds the cost of a staged test. The proposal assumes the new design *works* without evidence, betting the entire brand equity on an unproven aesthetic hypothesis against a known, functional legacy cue.

**Better Way:** Run a regional pilot (e.g., 5% of stores) for 8 weeks. Measure sell-through and conduct intercept surveys to verify recognition speed. If recognition drops, iterate the design before a national rollout.

**Kill Criteria:** If pilot sell-through drops >5% vs. control regions or recognition time increases by >2 seconds, halt the redesign.

**Verdict:** The proposal lacks a safety mechanism for a high-risk, one-way door change affecting a vulnerable demographic. It bets the company on an untested assumption that advertising can instantly overwrite years of visual conditioning.

DECISION: NO-GO
