**Claim:** Launch a $10,000 graphical business computer in 1983 despite high cost, slow performance, scarce software, and internal competition.

**Steelman:** This machine offers a revolutionary interface that will define the future of office work, justifying a premium price for early adopters seeking a strategic advantage before competitors arrive.

**Attack & Analysis:**
The proposal fails the blocker test immediately. The price ($10,000) is several times higher than leading business PCs, which already serve the market effectively. In 1983, office managers prioritize compatibility and speed for spreadsheets and word processing; a "slow" machine with "little third-party software" provides no immediate utility to offset the massive cost premium. The value proposition relies entirely on a future ecosystem that does not yet exist.

Furthermore, the existence of a cheaper, similar machine in development within the same company destroys the edge. Why launch a premium, inferior product now when a cost-effective version is imminent? Launching the expensive model risks cannibalizing the future cheaper model's launch or alienating customers who feel cheated when prices inevitably drop. The "strategic necessity" of being first is negated by the product's functional deficits (speed, software). Being first with a slow, expensive, unsupported tool is not an edge; it is a brand liability.

**Blockers:**
1.  **Price/Value Mismatch:** Cost is multiples of competitors while offering lower performance and software support.
2.  **Internal Conflict:** A cheaper alternative is already in the pipeline, making this launch strategically redundant and potentially damaging.
3.  **No Immediate Edge:** Slowness and lack of software negate the graphical interface's benefit for current business needs.

**Risks:** Market rejection due to price; reputation damage from poor performance; internal channel conflict.

**Edge:** NONE. Being first with a deficient product is not an advantage. The internal cheaper option holds the real future edge.

**Decision:** The plan commits real resources to a product that cannot win on current facts and undermines the company's own future offering. It fails to beat "do nothing" (wait for the cheaper internal model).

DECISION: NO-GO
