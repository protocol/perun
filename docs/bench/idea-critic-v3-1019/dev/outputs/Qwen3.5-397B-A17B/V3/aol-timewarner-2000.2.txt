The proposal relies on the assumption that dial-up dominance will persist long enough to monetize media convergence, yet the plan itself admits broadband is replacing dial-up. The "edge" cited is the internet provider's distribution, but that distribution is technologically obsolete within the projected integration timeline. The valuation premium paid by the media conglomerate is based on volatile stock prices at a market peak, creating a massive downside risk if the bubble bursts before synergies materialize.

The "better way" analysis reveals a fatal flaw: the plan requires the cable network to deliver high-speed access, yet explicitly states network access "isn't guaranteed." Building a strategy on an unsecured dependency while paying a peak valuation for a decaying asset (dial-up) fails the blocker test. The cultural clash between a fast-moving internet firm and a traditional media giant, combined with regulatory uncertainty, introduces execution risks that the proposal acknowledges but does not mitigate.

The pre-mortem is clear: if broadband adoption accelerates faster than predicted (a near certainty given the trend), the internet provider's valuation collapses, leaving the combined entity with massive goodwill impairment and no competitive moat. The "content plus distribution" thesis only works if the distribution channel remains relevant; here, the primary channel (dial-up) is the very thing being disrupted. The plan attempts to merge a dying business model with a slow-moving incumbent at the worst possible valuation point.

There is no verified edge that survives the shift to broadband. The "strategic necessity" of combining now is outweighed by the certainty of the dial-up decline. The lack of guaranteed cable access removes the proposed solution to that decline. This is a classic case of paying for past growth while ignoring future obsolescence.
