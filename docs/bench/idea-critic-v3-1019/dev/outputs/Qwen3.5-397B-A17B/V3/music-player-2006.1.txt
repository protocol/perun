The proposal attempts to dislodge a dominant incumbent by attacking its strongest fortifications: ecosystem lock-in and content licensing. The "steelman" argument is that superior hardware (larger screen) and a unified store/subscription model can overcome lateness to market. However, the facts provided reveal fatal structural flaws.

First, the proposal explicitly abandons the copy-protection standard the company previously championed to partners. This is not merely a technical shift; it is a betrayal of established trust with labels and device makers, likely triggering immediate legal retaliation and content blackouts. Without label support, a music store cannot function.

Second, the "wireless song sharing" feature directly conflicts with the stated risk that labels will restrict sharing. Building a core value proposition on a feature that rights-holders are poised to block renders the product non-compliant before launch.

Third, entering a market where the leader holds an enormous share and loyal ecosystem, while being late, offering no price advantage, and lacking a content library due to the DRM schism, offers no viable path to profitability. The "large marketing budget" cannot fix a product that lacks the essential content license to operate legally or competitively. The proposal fails the "edge" test: it possesses no unique asset, cost advantage, or demand signal that rivals lack. In fact, it actively destroys its own distribution channel (partner trust) while entering a saturated market.

The risks are not hypothetical; they are inherent contradictions in the strategy. Abandoning the industry DRM standard while relying on that same industry for content is a self-defeating maneuver. The lack of price advantage and lateness to market removes the only other potential vectors for entry. This plan guarantees high expenditure with a near-certain probability of zero market penetration due to content unavailability.

DECISION: NO-GO
