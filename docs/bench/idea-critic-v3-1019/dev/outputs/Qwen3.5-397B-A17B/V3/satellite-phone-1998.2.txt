The proposal fails the blocker test on unit economics and market fit. The core claim—that international business travelers will pay $3,000 for handsets and several dollars per minute in 1998—is contradicted by the stated fact that ground cellular networks are "spreading fast." As terrestrial coverage expands, the value proposition of a bulky, indoor-incompatible satellite device collapses precisely when the company needs rapid subscriber growth to service its massive debt.

**Blockers:**
1.  **Price vs. Value:** The $3,000 handset and high per-minute cost create a barrier too high for a niche market, especially when a cheaper, superior alternative (ground cellular) is rapidly becoming available in the very regions (business hubs) the target audience frequents.
2.  **Debt Service Mismatch:** The capital structure ($5B borrowed) demands immediate, massive scale. The stated limitations (bulky hardware, poor indoor performance, high price) physically prevent the adoption rate required to meet debt obligations.
3.  **Channel Readiness:** The explicit admission that "sales channels are not ready" guarantees a delayed revenue ramp, which is fatal for a highly leveraged entity requiring fast growth.

**Risks:**
The pre-mortem is clear: the company launches, fails to acquire enough subscribers to cover interest payments due to the limited total addressable market (TAM), and defaults before achieving profitability. The "edge" is nonexistent; being first with an inferior, expensive product while a better technology scales organically is a liability, not an advantage.

**Edge:** NONE. The proposal relies on a temporary gap in terrestrial coverage that is closing faster than the satellite network can monetize it. There is no defensible moat against the spreading ground networks.

This is a classic case of solving a hard technical problem (global coverage) while ignoring the economic reality (cost and usability). The plan does not beat "do nothing" or a staged pilot because the fundamental unit economics do not work at the proposed price point against the stated competitive threat. The debt load makes any "wait and see" approach impossible, yet the market conditions dictate that waiting for costs to drop or technology to improve is the only logical path. Proceeding now locks in catastrophic failure.

DECISION: NO-GO
