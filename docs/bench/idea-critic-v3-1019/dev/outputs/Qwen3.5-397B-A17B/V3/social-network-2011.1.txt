The claim is that integrating a new social layer across existing products will overcome network effects and displace a dominant incumbent. The steelman: leveraging massive distribution in search, mail, and video to force-feed a superior product (circles, hangouts) can jumpstart network effects where organic growth failed.

However, the proposal fails the blocker test. The primary blocker is the company's own history: three previous failed social attempts prove that distribution alone does not create a social graph. Users tolerate search and mail for utility; they do not tolerate them for social obligation. Forced integration risks degrading the core utility of these products, driving users to competitors who offer unbundled, focused experiences. The "differentiators" (circles, hangouts) are features, not a fundamental shift in user behavior sufficient to break the incumbent's lock-in. Tying executive bonuses to adoption creates a perverse incentive to prioritize vanity metrics over genuine engagement, likely leading to the same "ghost town" outcome as previous attempts.

The "do nothing" alternative preserves the core business's reputation and utility. A "better way" would be to incubate social features organically within specific verticals (e.g., photo sharing only) without forcing a unified identity layer, allowing demand to pull the product rather than pushing it via integration. The cost of delay is low; the market leader is not threatening the core search business. The cost of failure is high: user backlash against core products and further reputational damage in social.

There is no named edge here that rivals lack. Being big, having money, or owning distribution channels is not an edge in social networking; it is often a liability when users perceive the move as coercive. The proposal relies on "strategic necessity" and fear of falling behind rather than a proven mechanism for shifting user behavior. The risks (annoying users, repeating history) are not just possibilities; they are patterns established by the company's own track record. Without a concrete mechanism to overcome the "why switch?" hurdle beyond "we will force it," the plan is destined to repeat the previous three failures.

DECISION: NO-GO
