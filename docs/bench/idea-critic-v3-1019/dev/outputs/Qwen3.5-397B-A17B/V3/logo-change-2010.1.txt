Steelman: A sudden, total rebrand to a minimalist aesthetic will signal modernity and attract a younger demographic faster than a gradual evolution.

However, this proposal fails the blocker test. The plan explicitly ignores verified evidence of brand equity: the "dark blue square" is the primary recognition asset. Removing it entirely in favor of a "small blue square" and untested typography destroys the visual anchor loyal customers use to identify the brand. The proposal admits no customer testing occurred; launching an untested core identity across every channel simultaneously is a one-way door with catastrophic reversal costs.

The "no advance announcement" strategy amplifies the risk. In 2010, social media was already potent enough to organize immediate, visible backlash from alienated core customers before the company could explain its rationale. The proposal offers no edge other than the hope that "new equals better," which is not a strategic advantage but a gamble. A cheaper, safer alternative exists: a phased rollout starting with digital channels or a specific sub-line, accompanied by customer validation of the new mark's recognition value.

The pre-mortem is clear: the launch triggers a viral campaign (#BringBackTheSquare) citing the loss of heritage, sales dip among the core demographic, and a forced, expensive reversion to the old logo within months, damaging credibility further. The cost of delay (waiting to test) is negligible compared to the cost of failure (total brand confusion).

Blockers: 
1. No evidence the new mark retains recognition value; the core asset (large square) is removed.
2. Zero customer testing on a high-stakes, irreversible change.
3. "Big bang" rollout prevents course correction.

Edge: NONE. Being first to confuse your customer base is not an edge.

Kill Criteria: If a pilot test with 500 loyal customers shows <80% unaided recall of the new mark compared to the old, stop immediately.

DECISION: NO-GO
