**Steelman:** Launching a premium, mouse-driven personal computer at $10,000 in 1983 targets high-value office workflows where graphical efficiency justifies a 3-4x price premium over text-based competitors, assuming early adopters prioritize interface innovation over raw speed or software volume.

**Attack & Analysis:**
The proposal fails the blocker test on three verified facts provided in the prompt. First, the **price** ($10,000) is several times higher than leading business PCs, creating an immediate barrier to entry without a proven value proposition to offset the cost difference. Second, the machine **runs slowly**, directly undermining the productivity gains required to justify the premium price for office workers. Third, there is **little third-party software**, rendering the hardware useless for diverse business needs regardless of interface quality.

Crucially, an internal **cheaper alternative** with a similar interface is already in development. This represents a dominant "better way." Launching the expensive, slow unit now cannibalizes the future cheaper product or forces an immediate, costly pivot once the internal competitor arrives. The "edge" claimed (graphical interface) is not unique to this specific proposal since the internal cheaper machine offers it too.

**Kill Criteria Check:**
- **Blocker:** The existence of a cheaper internal alternative with similar capabilities makes the $10,000 price point indefensible.
- **Risk:** High price + low speed + low software = market rejection.
- **Edge:** NONE. The proposed unit has no advantage over the internal cheaper alternative; it is strictly inferior in cost and performance.

The only path to a "GO" would be if the $10,000 machine offered unique hardware capabilities the cheaper version could not match, but the prompt states the cheaper version has a "similar interface" and implies it solves the core value prop without the downsides. Proceeding as proposed guarantees financial loss and strategic confusion.

DECISION: NO-GO
