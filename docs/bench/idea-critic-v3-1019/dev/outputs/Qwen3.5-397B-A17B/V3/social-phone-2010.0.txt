The proposal fails the blocker test on three verified facts provided in the text. First, the target demographic (teenagers) faces a cost barrier: the mandatory data plan equals a full smartphone plan, which is explicitly noted as "high for teenagers." This contradicts the economic reality of the user base. Second, the value proposition is hollow at launch; rivals possess large app catalogs, while this proposal offers "no application store." For users who "live on social networks," an app ecosystem is not a feature but the primary utility. Launching without one renders the device non-competitive against established rivals. Third, the technical strategy ignores existing assets; the plan builds a "new in-house operating system" despite the company owning a mobile platform, unnecessarily increasing risk and fragmentation when speed to market is critical.

The "sunk cost" of two years of development is a fallacy in this context; continuing to invest in a product with no app store and prohibitive costs for its users guarantees further loss, not recovery. The "better way" is to leverage the acquired maker's brand on the company's existing mobile platform, negotiate a youth-tier data plan, and ensure app parity before launch.

The strongest attack is the lack of an application store. Without apps, the phone cannot serve teenagers who live on social networks, regardless of messaging features. The proposal offers no mitigation for this fatal gap. The edge is undefined; being first with a limited device is not an advantage, and the acquired brand cannot overcome the functional deficit of no apps and high cost.

Blockers: High cost for target demographic; no app store at launch; ignoring existing mobile platform.
Risks: Rival dominance in apps; carrier lock-in reducing appeal.
Edge: NONE.

DECISION: NO-GO
