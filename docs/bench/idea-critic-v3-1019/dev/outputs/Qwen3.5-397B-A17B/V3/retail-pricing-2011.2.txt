The claim is that replacing a coupon-dependent model with everyday low pricing (EDLP) and branded shops will reverse sales declines for an older, discount-trained demographic. The steelman: this eliminates margin erosion from promotions and modernizes the experience to attract higher-value shoppers, leveraging the CEO's prior electronics success.

However, the skeptic hat identifies a fatal blocker: the proposal explicitly states most sales historically come from discounted items and the customer base is "coupon-driven." Removing the primary purchase trigger for the core constituency without a verified alternative demand source contradicts the stated facts. The "better-way" analysis shows "do nothing" or a limited pilot in select stores offers a safer path to validate price elasticity before betting the chain. A full-chain rollout is a one-way door with extreme reversal costs; the cost of delay (waiting for pilot results) is negligible compared to the risk of immediate revenue collapse.

The pre-mortem is clear: loyal customers perceive the price change as an increase, stop visiting, and new customers do not arrive fast enough to fill the void because the brand equity is tied to discounts, not the CEO's previous electronics model. The plan lacks an edge; past success in a different category (electronics) with a different demographic does not transfer to mid-market apparel/home goods without proof. The absence of a pilot is a procedural failure that turns a strategic risk into a guaranteed operational blind spot.

This is not a risk to be managed with kill criteria; it is a fundamental mismatch between the strategy and the verified customer behavior. The plan fails the blocker test because its own evidence (sales history) contradicts its core mechanism (removing discounts).

DECISION: NO-GO
