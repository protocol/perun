**Verdict: NO-GO**

The proposal fails the "Edge" test. The claim relies on "easier tools" and "cheaper discs" as differentiators, but these are table stakes, not a defensible moat against incumbents with locked-in ecosystems and beloved first-party franchises. The proposal admits zero experience as a platform holder, yet attempts to enter a market defined by network effects and high switching costs.

The "better-way" analysis reveals a fatal flaw in the "do nothing" vs. "go" comparison. Entering this market requires subsidizing hardware losses to build a user base, a strategy that demands massive capital reserves and a pipeline of hit software to sustain momentum. The proposal offers no evidence of secured "killer apps" or exclusive developer commitments that would compel gamers to switch from established brands. Without a named asset (e.g., a proprietary hit franchise, unique hardware capability, or existing distribution dominance) that rivals lack, the "Edge" is NONE.

The pre-mortem is clear: launching without a distinct software advantage results in a ghost town library. Developers will not prioritize a new, unproven platform over incumbents with installed bases, creating a vicious cycle where lack of games drives low sales, which further repels developers. The risk of an incumbent launching a superior 3D system simultaneously compounds this, likely rendering the new entrant obsolete before launch.

While the shift to CD manufacturing is logically sound for the future, executing it as a *new platform holder* without a proven software engine or unique market leverage is a speculative bet with a high probability of total loss. The "cheaper" argument does not offset the "no experience" and "no library" blockers. This is a classic case of solving a manufacturing problem while ignoring the ecosystem reality.

DECISION: NO-GO
