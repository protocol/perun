**Steelman:** A premium, connected juicer creates a recurring revenue stream by solving convenience and freshness verification for health-conscious consumers, leveraging hardware subsidies via high-margin subscriptions.

**Attack:**
1.  **Unit Economics Failure:** At $699, the device targets early adopters, but the recurring cost of proprietary bags likely exceeds fresh produce, creating a "razor-and-blade" model where the blades are overpriced consumables. In 2016, consumers are increasingly skeptical of mandatory subscriptions for basic appliances.
2.  **Unnecessary Complexity:** Requiring Wi-Fi to verify "freshness" (a physical state) introduces a single point of failure. If servers dip or connectivity lags, the core function halts. This is a solution in search of a problem; expiration dates are printable.
3.  **Market Friction:** The countertop footprint is significant for a single-use device. The market for $699 juicers is niche; adding a mandatory subscription shrinks the total addressable market further. Competitors offer cheap, offline juicers where users supply their own fruit.
4.  **Pre-mortem:** The device fails because the subscription churn is high once users realize the per-juice cost is 3x-5x retail produce prices. The hardware becomes an expensive paperweight if the subscription lapses or the company pivots.

**Better Way:** Sell the machine at cost ($300-$400) with an optional app for recipes, removing the connectivity gate for basic operation. Allow loose produce input to lower the barrier to entry, monetizing only via optional pre-packed bags.

**Kill Criteria:**
*   **Blocker:** The marginal cost of a proprietary bag exceeds $4.00 (making a single juice >$5.00), rendering it non-competitive against smoothie shops.
*   **Edge:** NONE. Being first to market with "smart" juicing is not an edge when the smart feature adds friction without tangible safety benefits. High funding is not an edge; it is runway to burn on customer acquisition costs that cannot be recovered.

**Decision Logic:** The proposal relies on a forced ecosystem (hardware + mandatory subscription + connectivity) that penalizes the user for every interaction. The value proposition (convenience) is negated by the price premium and technical fragility. The "freshness check" is a vacuous metric easily solved by packaging technology without internet dependency. The plan fails the "cheaper alternative" test immediately: a standard juicer + grocery store fruit.

DECISION: NO-GO
