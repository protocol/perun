**Steelman:** Differentiate via novel interaction (dual-screen/touch) and price to expand the market beyond core gamers, rather than losing a spec war against a superior graphics rival.

**Attack & Analysis:**
The primary blocker hypothesis is that unusual hardware will fracture the developer ecosystem, resulting in a software drought. However, keeping the older handheld on sale mitigates this: core gamers and developers retain a stable, proven platform, while the new device targets a distinct, broader demographic. This creates a "good-better" segmentation rather than a forced migration.

The risk of the device being dismissed as a "gimmick" is real but is the necessary cost of avoiding a direct confrontation where the rival holds an insurmountable advantage in graphics and media. Matching the rival's specs would likely result in a higher-priced, me-too product with no unique selling proposition. The "gimmick" is actually the only viable wedge for market expansion.

Regarding cost: while two screens add bill-of-materials expense, the proposal explicitly targets a lower price point than the rival. This implies a strategic sacrifice of margin per unit to drive volume and software attachment rates among non-core users, a proven strategy for platform holders. The unproven nature of touch-screen games is not a reason to abstain; it is the opportunity to define a new category before the competitor does.

**Edge:** The company dominates handheld gaming, providing the distribution leverage and first-party IP necessary to validate the new control scheme. The rival is focused on fidelity; this proposal attacks the "blue ocean" of casual and innovative gameplay that high-fidelity hardware often ignores.

**Verdict:**
No factual blocker exists. The risks (developer confusion, gimmick perception) are managed by the dual-platform strategy and the necessity of differentiation. The plan beats "do nothing" (losing relevance) and "match specs" (losing the spec war). It moves the north star of audience expansion.

DECISION: GO
