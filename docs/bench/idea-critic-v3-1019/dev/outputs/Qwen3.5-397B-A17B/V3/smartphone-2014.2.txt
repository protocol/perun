The claim is that a 3D display and visual search can overcome app scarcity and carrier exclusivity to justify flagship pricing in a saturated two-platform market.

**Skeptic:** The proposal assumes novelty drives adoption over ecosystem utility. In 2014, "app gap" is a proven killer for third platforms; mainstream apps are not a "nice-to-have" but the primary definition of a smartphone for mass markets. A forked OS without these apps reduces the device to a media viewer, contradicting the "smartphone" value proposition. The "3D-like" feature is a gimmick with no established user demand, while the "one-touch" identifier exists in competitor apps already available on dominant platforms.

**Better-way:** The "do nothing" option preserves capital. A cheaper alternative is licensing the visual search technology as an app for existing iOS/Android devices, capturing the commerce upside without the billions required for hardware, carrier subsidies, and OS maintenance. Staging the launch as a low-cost experiment rather than a flagship competitor reduces downside risk.

**Kill-criteria:** The plan fails if it cannot secure mainstream apps before launch (stated as missing). It fails if the price exceeds subsidized competitors without offering core utility. The "edge" cited (3D display, visual search) does not offset the fundamental lack of platform utility. Being first with 3D is not an edge if the market has not asked for it; fear of falling behind in hardware is not a strategy.

**Verdict:** There is no named edge that solves the blocker: the absence of mainstream apps on a new OS at a premium price point. The risks (app gap, saturation, exclusivity) are not hypothetical; they are structural facts of the 2014 market that the proposal acknowledges but does not solve. The "one-touch" feature is replicable on existing devices, removing the unique value prop. This is a high-cost bet with a near-certain failure mode based on the stated facts.

DECISION: NO-GO
