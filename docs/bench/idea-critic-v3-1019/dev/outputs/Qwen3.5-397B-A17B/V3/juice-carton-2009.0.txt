**Steelman:** A modernized, minimalist design will signal premium quality and attract new, younger demographics, outweighing the temporary confusion for existing loyalists through aggressive advertising.

**Attack:** The proposal fails the "shelf cue" test. For a fast-moving consumer good, the primary orange image is not mere decoration; it is the critical heuristic shoppers use to locate the product in seconds. Removing this cue to introduce abstraction (a glass, a cap shape, rotated text) directly attacks the mechanism of purchase for the core "older, loyal" base. The risk is not just "confusion"; it is immediate sales collapse as loyalists grab competitors' recognizable cartons. The "large advertising campaign" is a costly patch for a self-inflicted visibility wound, not a strategic edge. The proposal offers no evidence that the aesthetic gain converts to enough new volume to offset the guaranteed friction for the existing base.

**Better Way:** Retain the iconic orange-with-straw as the dominant visual anchor. Introduce modern elements (minimalist typography, the orange cap) as secondary accents or on a "limited edition" run to test reception without jeopardizing the core revenue stream.

**Kill Criteria:** If a blind shelf-test shows >15% increase in time-to-identification for current users, the design is dead. If the projected ad spend exceeds the margin gain from a hypothetical 5% new customer acquisition, the ROI is negative.

**Edge:** NONE. Being first to confuse your own customers is not an advantage. "Strategic necessity" of a refresh is not an edge without data showing the current design is losing share due to aesthetics rather than distribution or taste.

**Verdict:** The plan removes the single most valuable asset (recognition) to chase an unproven aesthetic upside, relying on expensive advertising to fix the resulting friction. It bets the company on a hypothesis that contradicts basic behavioral economics of retail shopping.

DECISION: NO-GO
