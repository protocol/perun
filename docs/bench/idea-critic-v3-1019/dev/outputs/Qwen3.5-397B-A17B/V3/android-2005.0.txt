**Steelman:** Acquiring the startup secures a free, open mobile OS to democratize mobile internet access, directly expanding the searchable surface area and protecting long-term ad revenue against walled gardens.

**Attack & Alternatives:**
The primary blocker is the lack of a direct revenue model and the extreme difficulty of displacing entrenched handset makers and carriers who control distribution. However, "doing nothing" carries the superior risk: if mobile web becomes dominated by proprietary OSs (like Symbian or future iOS/Android equivalents), the search company could be locked out entirely, destroying its core business. Building an OS internally is slower and lacks the startup's head start. Licensing an existing OS forfeits the "open" leverage needed to woo manufacturers.

**Fit & Pre-mortem:**
This aligns with the north star of "organizing the world's information," extending it to the pocket. If this fails, it will be because carriers blocked deployment or handset makers refused to adopt a free OS they couldn't differentiate. Yet, the cost of delay is catastrophic; waiting for the market to mature means entering as a supplicant rather than a platform architect.

**Evidence & Edge:**
While current figures show zero revenue, the strategic edge is not hardware profit but the preservation of the search monopoly. The "free" aspect is the only wedge strong enough to fracture carrier/handset oligopolies. The $50M price is negligible compared to the lifetime value of preventing a competitor from controlling the mobile gateway. The edge is the unique ability to monetize via search ads rather than device sales, allowing a pricing model (free) that hardware-centric rivals cannot match without cannibalizing their own units.

**Decision:**
No factual blocker exists; the risks are executional, not fundamental. The alternative (irrelevance in mobile) is worse than the cost of the bet. The kill criterion is clear: if no major handset maker commits within 24 months, pivot or shut down the project.

DECISION: GO
