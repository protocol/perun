Steelman: Acquiring the dominant code-hosting platform secures the industry's central nervous system, neutralizing a potential rival while capturing the world's largest developer graph before a free alternative gains critical mass.

However, the claim fails the blocker test. The proposal admits the target is loss-making with no permanent CEO, yet offers no specific turnaround plan or named successor to stabilize operations. More critically, the core asset is a community defined by active distrust of the buyer. In open source, community trust is the primary moat; without it, the platform becomes a hollow shell as users migrate to the cited free competitor. The proposal asserts the target will "run independently," but this is a structural hope, not a verified mechanism to prevent defection among a hostile user base.

The "edge" is undefined. Being first or large is not an edge when the acquisition triggers the very risk (defection) that destroys value. The $7.5 billion price tag relies on revenue growth that assumes retention, yet the stated facts suggest retention is the single point of failure. A cheaper, safer alternative exists: wait for the leadership vacuum to resolve or for the free competitor to falter, then acquire at a lower multiple with less cultural friction.

The pre-mortem is clear: six months post-close, key maintainers move to GitLab, enterprise contracts stall due to antitrust fears, and the buyer is left paying a premium for a declining asset. The cost of delay is negligible compared to the cost of buying a revolt. Without a concrete, verified plan to install trusted leadership immediately and legally firewall the brand beyond a vague promise of independence, the deal destroys more value than it creates. The lack of a named edge and the presence of a specific, unaddressed blocker (community hostility + leadership vacuum) mandate rejection.

DECISION: NO-GO
