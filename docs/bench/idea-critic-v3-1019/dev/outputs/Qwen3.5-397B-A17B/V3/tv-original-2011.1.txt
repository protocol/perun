The claim is that a data-driven, all-at-once release of two seasons without a pilot will succeed where traditional models fail. The steelman: existing viewing data provides sufficient confidence to bypass the pilot gate, securing exclusive content to differentiate the service before competitors react.

However, critical blockers exist. First, the premise that "viewing data" predicts success for *original* prestige drama is unproven; historical data reflects licensed content consumption, not demand for a specific new IP. Second, the financial structure is a one-way door with catastrophic failure mode: committing to two full seasons (26 episodes) removes the standard industry "out" clause after a pilot or partial season. If the first few episodes flop, the company is locked into paying for 20+ more episodes of a dead asset. Third, the "all-at-once" model has zero market validation; binge-watching behavior is an assumption, not a verified metric.

The "better way" is clear: produce a pilot or a limited initial order (e.g., 6 episodes) to test both the content quality and the release mechanism. This preserves capital while gathering real-world evidence. The proposal's reliance on "data" as a substitute for market testing is a category error; data describes the past, while this venture creates a new category.

The edge cited is "data," but without a proven correlation between that data and original drama success, it is not an edge—it is a guess dressed as science. The risks (uncertain audience, massive sunk cost) are not mitigated by kill criteria because the commitment is total upfront. There is no off-ramp.

Blockers:
1. No evidence linking existing viewing data to success in original prestige drama.
2. Financial commitment to 26 episodes eliminates the ability to stop production if early reception is poor.
3. Zero validation for the "all-at-once" distribution model.

Edge: NONE (Data is unproven for this specific use case).

This proposal bets the company's content budget on unverified assumptions about production risk and consumer behavior. It fails the blocker test by removing all safety mechanisms (pilots, staggered orders) based on flawed extrapolation.

DECISION: NO-GO
