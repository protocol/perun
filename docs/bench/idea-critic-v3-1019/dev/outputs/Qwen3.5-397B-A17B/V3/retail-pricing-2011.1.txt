The claim is that replacing a coupon-dependent model with everyday low pricing and branded shops will reverse falling sales for a mid-market department store chain. The steelman: eliminating discount complexity restores margin and modernizes the brand, leveraging the CEO's prior success in electronics to attract new customers.

However, the skeptic hat identifies a fatal blocker. The proposal explicitly states that "most of the chain's sales have historically come from items sold at a discount" and the customer base is "older, coupon-driven." These customers are trained to wait for promotions. Removing the very mechanism that drives the majority of revenue, without a pilot to test elasticity or customer retention, assumes the brand equity is strong enough to override decades of conditioned behavior. The CEO's success in *electronics* (a different category with different purchase cycles and brand loyalty) does not guarantee transferability to *department store apparel/home goods* for an aging demographic.

The "better way" analysis shows the "do nothing" or "staged pilot" options are superior. A full chain-wide rollout in January, immediately following a 2011 appointment, ignores the risk of immediate revenue collapse. If the hypothesis is wrong, the chain lacks the liquidity to recover because the change is total and simultaneous. The cost of delay (waiting for a pilot result) is negligible compared to the risk of total systemic failure.

The pre-mortem is clear: six months post-launch, sales plummet because the core customer base stops buying, waiting for discounts that never come, while new customers do not arrive fast enough to fill the gap. The "edge" cited is the CEO's past success, but this is not a product advantage or a unique asset for *this* specific customer base; it is a track record in a different sector. There is no evidence presented that *this* specific customer base wants everyday pricing; all evidence suggests the opposite.

This is a one-way door with no parachute. The plan fails the blocker test: the stated facts (sales rely on discounts) contradict the core claim (removing discounts will work) without a verified bridge (pilot data).

DECISION: NO-GO
