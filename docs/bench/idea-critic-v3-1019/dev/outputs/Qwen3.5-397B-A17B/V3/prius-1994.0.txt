**Steelman**: Committing now secures a decade-long technological moat, defining the category before regulators force the issue, despite near-term losses.

**Attack**: The proposal fails the "edge" test. With cheap fuel and no regulatory mandate, the customer value proposition is negative (higher cost, unproven reliability) for zero immediate upside. The "first mover" advantage is illusory without a market; it is merely "first loser" if the technology fails or adoption lags. The novel transmission and unproven NiMH batteries represent massive warranty liabilities that could bankrupt the division before volume scales. A cheaper, safer alternative exists: parallel development of components without a fixed 1997 mass-production deadline, allowing the market or regulation to mature first.

**Pre-mortem**: By 1998, the car launches, suffers battery failures in cold climates, costs 20% more than rivals, and sells only to fleet buyers via heavy subsidies. The brand takes a reliability hit, and the stock price tanks due to R&D overspend.

**Blockers**: 
1. **No Edge**: Cheap fuel and lack of regulation mean no consumer pull; being first offers no defensive advantage against incumbents who can copy成熟的 tech later.
2. **Unproven Core**: Battery durability is unknown; a mass launch invites catastrophic warranty exposure.
3. **Financial Mismatch**: High R&D/cost vs. zero near-term demand signal violates basic ROI logic for a volume program.

**Risks**: Technical failure of the power-split device; supply chain immaturity for NiMH; brand damage from early reliability issues.

**Kill Criteria**: If a pilot fleet cannot demonstrate <2% battery degradation over 2 years by Q4 1996, the program stops.

**Verdict**: The plan bets the company on a solution searching for a problem. It lacks a stated fact showing why *this* company, *now*, can win. The correct move is component development, not a bold public production commitment.

DECISION: NO-GO
