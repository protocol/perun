### Executive Summary: Cash Flow vs. Valuation Trade-off

Given your current burn rate of $120,000/month and $1.5M cash reserve, you have approximately **12.5 months of runway** under the current "sell-outright" model. Switching to a leasing model without additional capital would critically endanger the company before your Series B closes.

#### Option A: Continue Selling Outright (Current Model)
*   **Upfront Cash Inflow:** +$900 per unit (minus $400 COGS = +$500 gross contribution per unit immediately).
*   **Recurring Revenue:** $200/month/software only.
*   **Runway Impact:** Neutral to Positive. Hardware sales subsidize your operational burn.
*   **Series B Narrative:** Strong hardware adoption, but lower ARR multiple. Investors see capital efficiency.

#### Option B: Switch to Leasing ($50/mo hardware + $200/mo software)
*   **Upfront Cash Inflow:** $0.
*   **Cash Outflow:** -$400 per unit (manufacturing cost) immediately upon deployment.
*   **Recurring Revenue:** $250/month total.
*   **Runway Impact:** Severe negative shock. For every new studio leased, you spend $400 cash upfront to generate $250/month revenue. It takes **1.6 months** just to recover the hardware cost, ignoring operational burn.
*   **The Math:** If you lease to 10 new studios/month (aggressive growth), you incur an extra **$4,000/month** in pure hardware cash outflow, plus the existing $120k burn. While this boosts ARR (Annual Recurring Revenue) from $48k/year/studio to $60k/year/studio, it accelerates your cash depletion.
*   **Capital Requirement:** Your estimate of needing $500k in working capital is accurate. Without it, leasing 1,250 units (a modest scale-up) would consume $500k in inventory costs alone before collecting significant rent.

#### Recommendation: Do Not Switch to Leasing Pre-Series B

**1. Cash Preservation is Paramount**
With only 12.5 months of runway, your primary goal is reaching the Series B close with growth metrics intact, not running out of cash. Leasing ties up scarce liquidity in inventory (a hard asset) rather than fueling growth or product development. The "cash drag" poses an existential risk if the fundraising timeline slips by even three months.

**2. Valuation Nuance**
While leasing increases ARR (which often commands higher SaaS multiples), Series B hardware investors are sophisticated. They will discount a valuation if they see you burning cash to buy your own revenue. A "capital-efficient" growth story with lower ARR but positive unit economics on hardware often trades at a better *realized* valuation than a cash-burning leasing model that requires emergency bridge financing.

**3. Hybrid Strategy for Series B**
Present the leasing model as a **post-funding growth lever**. Use the Series B proceeds to fund the inventory required for leasing. This shows investors you have a plan to maximize LTV (Lifetime Value) and stickiness once capitalized, without jeopardizing the company's survival today. Keep selling outright to maintain runway.

#### Tax and Legal Structure
**Yes, you absolutely need a tax advisor and legal counsel before drafting lease agreements.**
*   **Tax Implications:** Leasing changes how you recognize revenue (operating lease vs. sales) and depreciate assets. It may trigger sales tax complexities in different jurisdictions that differ from outright sales.
*   **Legal Risk:** A 3-year term creates a liability on your balance sheet. You need robust contracts covering damage, early termination, and repossession rights. Doing this without specialized counsel could create hidden liabilities that scare off Series B investors during due diligence.

**Final Verdict:** Maintain the outright sales model to protect your 12-month runway. Pitch the leasing model in your Series B deck as the primary use of funds to scale recurring revenue post-close. Secure tax/legal advice now to prepare the lease structures, but do not execute them until the new capital is in the bank.
