Based on the activity you described—holding third-party funds in interest-bearing accounts and sharing the generated yield with users—you are likely engaging in activities that trigger strict regulatory requirements in the United States.

**1. The Core Issue: Taking Deposits vs. Escrow**
Traditional escrow services hold funds in non-interest-bearing accounts or pass through interest strictly according to the contract without the escrow agent retaining a share or using the pool for yield generation as a business model. When you actively manage these funds to generate yield and share it as a "loyalty incentive," regulators may view this as **taking deposits** rather than simple escrow.
*   **Banking Charter:** Under federal law (and mirrored in most states), accepting deposits from the public for the purpose of lending or investing is the definition of banking. If you are pooling client funds to earn interest, you risk being classified as an unchartered bank.
*   **Trust Licenses:** In states like **New York** and **Delaware**, holding funds for others often requires a **Trust Company Charter** or a specific **Money Transmitter License** if the funds are viewed as transmitting value. New York, in particular, has stringent regulations under the Department of Financial Services (NYDFS) regarding virtual currencies and money transmission, which can extend to digital asset escrow.

**2. State-Specific Nuances**
*   **Delaware:** While Delaware is friendly to corporate entities, it strictly regulates entities holding funds in trust. You would likely need a license as a **Delaware Trust Company** or a statutory trust arrangement supervised by the state.
*   **New York:** Operating in NY without a license while holding customer funds can lead to immediate cease-and-desist orders. The "BitLicense" framework or a Money Transmitter license may be applicable depending on how the domain assets and funds are classified.
