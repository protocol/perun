**Verdict: Do not implement a pure success-based fee (contingency fee) for grant writing.**

Your potential client is correct: charging a percentage of the grant award (a contingency fee) is widely prohibited and creates severe compliance risks for your non-profit customers.

**Why Success-Based Fees Are Problematic**
1.  **Ethical Codes:** The Association of Fundraising Professionals (AFP) and the Grant Professionals Association (GPA) explicitly prohibit members from accepting compensation based on a percentage of charitable contributions. Many non-profits require their vendors to adhere to these codes.
2.  **Foundation Guidelines:** Major foundations (e.g., Ford, Rockefeller) and federal grant guidelines often explicitly forbid using grant funds to pay contingency fees. They view this as an inappropriate use of charitable assets.
3.  **Administrative Caps:** Even if not explicitly banned, many grants cap "administrative" or "fundraising" costs at 10–15%. A 10% success fee would consume your client's entire allowable administrative budget, leaving no room for actual operations, which makes the grant unsustainable.
4.  **Conflict of Interest:** It creates a perception that the writer is prioritizing high-value grants over the mission-fit or likelihood of success, potentially jeopardizing the non-profit's reputation.

**Alternative Pricing Structures to Share Upside**
To align your incentives with client success without violating compliance, consider these hybrid models:

**1. Base Retainer + Performance Bonus (Fixed Amount)**
Charge a lower monthly base fee (e.g., $49/mo) to cover platform access and drafting, plus a **fixed dollar bonus** upon award notification.
*   *Why it works:* It is not a "percentage" of the grant, so it avoids the specific language of contingency fee prohibitions. It is framed as a "completion bonus" or "outcome incentive" rather than a cut of the funds.
*   *Structure:* $49/mo + $500 fixed bonus per grant awarded over $10k.

**2. Tiered Success Fees Based on Grant Size (Not Percentage)**
Instead of a flat 10%, create tiered fixed fees triggered by grant thresholds.
*   *Why it works:* It decouples the fee from a direct percentage calculation.
*   *Structure:*
    *   Grants <$25k: No extra fee (included in subscription).
    *   Grants $25k–$100k: One-time fee of $1,000.
    *   Grants >$100k: One-time fee of $2,500.
    *   *Note:* Ensure the contract states this is for "extended implementation support" post-award to further distance it from a fundraising commission.

**3. "Implementation & Reporting" Add-On**
Shift the value proposition. Charge the standard subscription for *writing*, but offer a mandatory "Grant Management & Reporting" package triggered only upon winning.
*   *Why it works:* Foundations generally allow funds to be used for grant *management*, compliance reporting, and impact tracking. You are selling a service required to fulfill the grant terms, not a commission for getting the money.
*   *Structure:* $79/mo subscription + $99/mo "Compliance & Reporting" module (activated only when a grant is won).

**4. Value-Based Flat Fee per Application**
Charge significantly higher fees for complex, high-value grant applications compared to small ones, determined *before* the outcome.
*   *Why it works:* The price is set based on the complexity and potential value of the work (value-based pricing), not the outcome.
*   *Structure:* Small grants ($5k): Included in plan. Large federal grants ($500k+): $2,000 flat fee per application due to complexity.

**Recommendation**
Given your current stability ($3,800 monthly profit), do not risk your reputation or your clients' funding eligibility with a contingency model. Adopt **Model #1 (Base + Fixed Bonus)**. It lowers the barrier to entry for smaller non-profits while allowing you to capture upside from big wins, all while remaining compliant with AFP/GPA ethics and foundation rules.

*Ensure your Terms of Service explicitly state that clients are responsible for verifying fee acceptability with their specific grantors.*
