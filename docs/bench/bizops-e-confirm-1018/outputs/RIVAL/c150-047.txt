However, I can outline standard commercial practices for Hardware-as-a-Service (HaaS) and robotic deployments regarding software maintenance to help you prepare for that legal consultation:

**Standard Industry Practices for Software Maintenance**

1.  **Bundled vs. Separated Maintenance:** In HaaS models, critical safety patches and regulatory compliance updates are almost universally bundled into the base lease fee. Separating these creates significant risk; if a client declines a "paid" safety update and an incident occurs, your company could face heightened negligence claims for knowingly operating unsafe equipment. Feature upgrades (new drink recipes, UI changes) are often separated or offered as tiered add-ons, but core functionality and safety are typically included to ensure the "service" remains operational.

2.  **Service Level Agreements (SLAs):** Instead of vague promises, contracts usually define specific performance metrics. This includes uptime guarantees (e.g., 99% availability) and response times for critical software failures. The contract should distinguish between "critical" updates (safety/security) and "non-critical" updates (features).

3.  **Limitation of Liability Clauses:** Standard contracts often include caps on liability (e.g., limited to the total amount paid in the last 12 months) and explicit exclusions for consequential damages (lost profits, business interruption). They also typically include indemnification clauses where the client agrees to hold the provider harmless for issues arising from client negligence (e.g., failing to provide power or internet required for updates).

4.  **"As-Is" and Disclaimer Language:** While you cannot disclaim liability for gross negligence or willful misconduct, contracts often state that software is provided "as-is" regarding non-critical features, with no guarantee that specific features will remain available or compatible with third-party systems indefinitely.

5.  **Obsolescence and Upgrade Paths:** To address your fear of obsolescence, agreements often include a "technology refresh" clause. This allows the provider to swap out older hardware for newer models during the lease term, sometimes for an additional fee or as part of a renewed agreement, rather than guaranteeing that the initial hardware will remain state-of-the-art for three years.

**Key Considerations for Your Discussion with Counsel**

*   **Duty to Update:** Establish whether you have a contractual or statutory duty to push updates automatically or if the client must approve them. Automatic updates reduce the risk of client error but increase your responsibility for any bugs introduced.
*   **End-of-Life Definitions:** Define what constitutes "functional." Does the robot need to make every new type of coffee, or just operate safely?
*   **Force Majeure:** Ensure the contract covers scenarios where external factors (internet outages, power issues) prevent software updates.

You should immediately consult with an attorney to draft these provisions, specifically focusing on how to balance your need to limit liability with the necessity of maintaining safe, functioning robots in public spaces.
