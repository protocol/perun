The primary red flag is the direct linkage of your compensation to "public utility revenues" or savings derived from public assets. In many states, including Ohio, statutes strictly prohibit private entities from holding a lien on, or receiving a direct percentage of, public funds generated by essential infrastructure. This structure may be legally interpreted as an unauthorized lease of public assets or an illegal privatization of a governmental function, as it effectively grants your company an ownership interest in the utility's financial performance.

The specific mechanism of taking "10% of water savings" is problematic because "savings" are often viewed as public funds that cannot be diverted to private profit without explicit legislative authorization. Municipalities generally lack the authority to enter into contracts that pledge future public revenue to private parties outside of standard bond measures or approved tax increment financing. Furthermore, auditors and state regulators may flag this as a "contingent fee" arrangement that creates a conflict of interest, potentially violating public procurement codes that require fixed, transparent pricing for services.

1.  **Reframe the Payment Mechanism:** Instead of claiming a percentage of "savings" (which implies a share of public revenue), structure the fee as a variable service charge based on verified performance metrics. This shifts the legal characterization from "profit sharing" to "payment for verified results."

2.  **Decouple from Direct Revenue:** Do not language the contract as taking a cut of the utility's budget. Instead, frame the payment as a cost avoidance measure where the utility pays a fee only if the net result is positive cash flow. The legal argument is that the city is paying for a *service* (leak detection and reduction), not selling a portion of its water rights or revenue stream.
