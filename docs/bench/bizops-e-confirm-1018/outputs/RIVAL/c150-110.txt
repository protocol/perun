### Liability Assessment: Tool vs. Billing Agent

Currently, your model ($0.15/claim, doctor approval required) attempts to position CodeAssist as a **decision-support tool**. However, liability often hinges on "control" and "intent" rather than just final human clicks. If your AI systematically suggests higher-paying codes that a busy rural doctor rubber-stamps without independent verification, regulators may argue CodeAssist is the *de facto* billing agent, especially if your marketing emphasizes revenue maximization.

**Specific Liability Scenarios Where CodeAssist Could Be Held Responsible:**

1.  **Aiding and Abetting False Claims:** If investigators find your algorithm is trained or tuned to prioritize higher-reimbursement codes over clinically accurate ones, and you know doctors are approving them blindly, you could be liable for knowingly submitting false claims. The "doctor approval" defense fails if the approval is perfunctory due to your system's design.
2.  **Improper Inducement (Anti-Kickback Statute):** If your pricing or algorithm effectively guarantees higher revenue for the clinic (e.g., "Our AI finds missed revenue"), regulators may view your service as an illegal kickback. You are providing a service that directly generates profit sharing via increased billing, which can be construed as remuneration for referrals.
3.  **Negligent Design/Training:** If your training data contains biased upcoding patterns and you fail to audit the output for compliance, you could be liable for negligence. Unlike a calculator, an AI that "learns" or suggests specific clinical codes assumes a higher duty of care.
4.  **Corporate Practice of Medicine (CPOM):** In some jurisdictions, if your AI dictates clinical documentation requirements to ensure code validation, you may be accused of interfering in the practice of medicine by directing how care is documented for billing purposes.

### Essential Compliance Questions for Legal Counsel

Before expanding to hospital networks, your attorney must address these specific questions to define the "line" between tool and agent:

1.  **Audit Trail & Reliance:** What specific logs must we maintain to prove the physician exercised *independent medical judgment* rather than rubber-stamping our suggestion? Does the UI need to force a "reason for selection" confirmation from the doctor?
2.  **Algorithmic Transparency:** Are we legally required to disclose the weighting factors our AI uses to suggest codes? If an insurer audits a clinic, must we surrender our model's logic to prove it wasn't designed to upcode?
3.  **Contractual Indemnification:** Can we contractually shift all upcoding liability to the clinic via Terms of Service, or does federal law void such waivers when the vendor's tool is the primary driver of the error?
4.  **Marketing Boundaries:** What specific claims about "revenue uplift" or "missed code recovery" cross the line from feature benefit to illegal inducement under the Anti-Kickback Statute?
5.  **Data Training Consent:** Does using clinic data to retrain our model to find "higher paying" patterns violate patient privacy laws or payer contracts regarding secondary use of billing data?

**Immediate Recommendation:** Halt any marketing language promising revenue increases. Implement a mandatory "clinical justification" step in your UI before a code can be submitted, ensuring the doctor actively validates the suggestion. Retain counsel immediately to draft a compliance framework that treats your AI as a high-risk medical device rather than simple software.
