You are asking for a depreciation strategy for the MediBot X1, but there is a fundamental mismatch in your premise: **depreciation schedules are not chosen based on management preference, investor optics, or software update cycles alone.** They are dictated by tax law (IRS guidelines) and accounting standards (GAAP) based on the asset's "useful life."

Here is the breakdown of how each approach affects your financials and the reality of your situation:

### 1. The 12-Year Mechanical Life Approach
If you depreciate over 12 years, you treat the robot as a long-term infrastructure asset.
*   **Annual Expense:** $10,000 per unit ($120k / 12).
*   **5-Year Impact:** You recognize only $50,000 of expense per unit over five years.
*   **Taxable Income:** Higher taxable income because your deduction is smaller. You pay more taxes now.
*   **Book Value:** Remains high on the balance sheet. After 5 years, the book value is $70,000 per unit.
*   **Investor Optics:** This shows strong asset longevity and a healthy balance sheet, which appeals to investors looking for stable infrastructure. However, if the software becomes obsolete in 3 years, auditors may force a write-down, claiming the "useful life" was overstated.

### 2. The 3-Year Software Cycle Approach
If you depreciate over 3 years, you argue the asset's useful life ends when compliance fails without updates.
*   **Annual Expense:** $40,000 per unit ($120k / 3).
*   **5-Year Impact:** You recognize the full $120,000 expense in the first 3 years. Years 4 and 5 have $0 depreciation expense for the original cost.
*   **Taxable Income:** Lower taxable income in years 1–3 (saving cash on taxes). Higher taxable income in years 4–5.
*   **Book Value:** Drops to zero by year 3.
*   **Investor Optics:** This looks risky. A zero book value suggests the assets are worthless, even if they are physically functioning. It implies your technology becomes obsolete every three years, which contradicts your pitch of "long-term rural infrastructure."

### The Critical Flaw in Your Binary Choice
You cannot simply choose the 3-year schedule for the *entire* $120,000 hardware cost just because software updates are needed. The metal, motors, and chassis clearly have a 12-year life. The software component is distinct.

**The Correct Accounting Treatment (Component Depreciation):**
Under GAAP, you should separate the asset into components:
1.  **Hardware:** Depreciate the mechanical portion (e.g., $100k) over 12 years.
2.  **Software/Integration:** Depreciate the embedded software portion (e.g., $20k) over 3 years.
3.  **Future Updates:** Treat the major updates every 3 years as new capitalized assets (or expenses, depending on cost), depreciating *those* specific costs over the next 3-year cycle.

### Implications for Your Series A
*   **Matching the 3-year cycle for the whole unit** will destroy your balance sheet appearance before the raise. Investors will see assets hitting $0 value rapidly, questioning the durability of your business model.
*   **Matching the 12-year cycle for the whole unit** risks an audit adjustment if you cannot prove the software doesn't limit the hardware's utility.
*   **Component Depreciation** is the most attractive to investors. It demonstrates sophisticated financial management, accurately reflects the long-life hardware (supporting your "asset longevity" narrative), while correctly accounting for the rapid software iteration. It keeps book value stable while adhering to compliance realities.

**Recommendation:** Do not depreciate the full hardware cost over 3 years. Adopt component depreciation to maintain a strong book value for your Series A deck while remaining compliant. Consult a CPA immediately to allocate the $120k cost between hardware and software components correctly before finalizing your books.

*Note: Tax laws (like MACRS in the US) often mandate specific recovery periods regardless of your internal estimates; verify the IRS class life for robotic dispensing units.*
