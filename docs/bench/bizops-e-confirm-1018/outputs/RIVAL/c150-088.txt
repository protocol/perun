Determining whether your general professional liability policy covers post-audit exploits, especially those involving code modifications and alleged negligence, requires a formal analysis by a qualified insurance broker or attorney specializing in cyber risks and professional errors and omissions (E&O).

However, based on standard industry practices for smart contract auditing and professional liability, here are the specific exclusions and policy language areas you must immediately investigate with your carrier:

1.  **"Known Loss" or "Prior Acts" Exclusions:** Insurers often deny claims if the vulnerability existed and was arguably identifiable before the policy period began, or if the firm had prior knowledge of potential issues.
2.  **"Code Modification" or "Scope Creep" Clauses:** Most audit policies strictly limit liability to the specific commit hash or code version reviewed. If the founders modified the code "slightly" after your review, the insurer will likely argue the exploit occurred in un-audited code, voiding coverage. Look for language defining the "Scope of Services" and whether deviations nullify protection.
3.  **"Guarantee of Security" Exclusions:** Policies frequently exclude claims arising from any express or implied guarantee that the code is "bug-free" or "secure." If your report's "low risk" classification is interpreted by a court as a guarantee of safety rather than a professional opinion, coverage may be denied.
4.  **"Third-Party Beneficiary" Limitations:** Your policy likely covers claims made by your direct client (the project founders). It may explicitly exclude claims made by end-users (the people who lost funds) who are not parties to your audit contract, unless you have specific "cyber liability" or "technology E&O" extensions that cover third-party data loss or financial harm.
5.  **"Negligence" vs. "Gross Negligence/Willful Misconduct":** While standard negligence is typically covered, many policies have carve-outs for "gross negligence" or "reckless disregard." If the reentrancy bug was obvious and missed due to a lack of standard procedure, the insurer might attempt to classify it as gross negligence to deny the claim.
6.  **"Cryptocurrency/Digital Asset" Exclusions:** Some general professional liability policies have broad exclusions for losses related to digital assets, cryptocurrencies, or blockchain technologies, requiring a specialized cyber policy to fill the gap.

You should immediately notify your insurance carrier of the potential claim to preserve your rights, as delayed notification is a common ground for denial. Simultaneously, engage legal counsel to review the specific engagement letter signed with the project, as contractual liability caps and indemnity clauses there will interact directly with your insurance coverage limits.
