**Operational Thresholds for Contractor vs. Employee Status**

Under the Department of Labor's "economic reality" test, the primary distinction hinges on whether the worker is economically dependent on the employer or in business for themselves. Your specific risk lies in the intersection of commission structures and AI guidance. The following thresholds typically trigger reclassification:

1.  **Control over Work Performance:** If your AI dictates *how* stylists must select items (e.g., mandatory adherence to algorithmic rankings without discretion) rather than just suggesting options, this mimics managerial supervision.
2.  **Opportunity for Profit or Loss:** If stylists cannot influence their earnings through independent business decisions (e.g., choosing their own clientele, setting their own hours, or marketing their services independently) and rely solely on the volume of AI-suggested sales, they appear economically dependent.
3.  **Permanency of Relationship:** If the platform is the stylist's primary or exclusive source of income and the relationship is indefinite without the ability to work for competitors simultaneously, this suggests employment.
4.  **Integration:** If the styling service is integral to your core business rather than ancillary, scrutiny increases. Since StyleMatch *is* a styling app, this factor already leans toward employment; you must offset this with high levels of operational independence.

**Behavioral Controls to Avoid Implementing**

To maintain contractor status, strictly avoid implementing the following controls, as they demonstrate an employer-employee relationship:

*   **Mandatory Acceptance of AI Suggestions:** Do not require stylists to accept or prioritize specific AI-generated recommendations. They must have the unfettered right to reject algorithmic suggestions and curate lists based on their own judgment without penalty or de-platforming.
*   **Prescribed Work Hours or Quotas:** Do not set minimum login times, mandatory response windows, or minimum sales quotas. Contractors must define their own schedules and output levels.
*   **Scripted Client Interactions:** Do not provide mandatory scripts or strict communication protocols for interacting with shoppers. Stylists must control the tone and method of their client relationships.
*   **Exclusive Territory or Client Assignment:** Do not assign specific clients to stylists or restrict them from working with clients they find outside the app. They must be free to build their own book of business.
*   **Disciplinary Action for Methodology:** Do not penalize stylists for using their own sourcing methods or ignoring AI trends. Feedback should be limited to quality assurance regarding platform rules, not work methodology.

**Critical Unknown Variable: State-Level "ABC" Tests**

The most dangerous unknown variable is whether your operating states utilize the **"ABC Test"** (currently active in California, Massachusetts, New Jersey, and others) rather than the federal DOL guidelines.

Under the ABC Test, a worker is presumed an employee unless the company proves three strict conditions, the most lethal being **Prong B**: *"The worker performs work that is outside the usual course of the hiring entity's business."* Since StyleMatch's entire business model is providing styling services, your stylists are performing the core work of your company. In ABC states, it is nearly impossible to classify core-service providers as contractors, regardless of how much independence you grant them. This state-level standard overrides federal flexibility and could instantly invalidate your current model in key markets, necessitating immediate legal review before any funding due diligence begins.
