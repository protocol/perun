Determining broker status under the Commodity Exchange Act and CFTC regulations involves a complex, fact-specific legal analysis that requires a qualified attorney specializing in commodities law.

The distinction between a regulated broker and an unregulated technology platform generally hinges on the degree of involvement in the transaction execution and the handling of customer funds. Regulators typically examine whether the platform merely introduces parties (introducing broker) or actively solicits orders, negotiates terms, accepts customer money or margin, and guarantees trade performance. In voluntary carbon markets, this line is increasingly scrutinized as the CFTC has asserted broader authority over digital asset and environmental commodity platforms.

Key factors that often trigger broker classification include:
*   **Solicitation and Negotiation:** Actively seeking out buyers or sellers and negotiating price or quantity on their behalf, rather than simply displaying orders placed by users.
*   **Custody of Funds:** Holding customer funds in transit, even temporarily, rather than having funds settle directly between parties or through a regulated third-party clearing mechanism.
*   **Discretionary Authority:** Having the ability to execute trades without specific, real-time consent from the user for each transaction.
*   **Guarantees:** Acting as the counterparty to the trade or guaranteeing the fulfillment of the contract.

Regarding structural changes, common strategies used by platforms to mitigate regulatory risk (which must be vetted by counsel) include:
*   **Limiting Role to Matching:** Ensuring the platform functions strictly as a bulletin board where users set their own terms and the platform does not intervene in price discovery or negotiation.
*   **Third-Party Settlement:** Utilizing a regulated payment processor or clearinghouse to handle all fund transfers, ensuring the platform never takes custody of buyer or seller funds.
*   **Revising Terms of Service:** Explicitly stating that the platform is not a broker, does not guarantee trades, and acts solely as a technology provider connecting independent parties.
*   **Fee Structure Adjustment:** Changing from a percentage of the transaction value (which can look like a brokerage commission) to a flat subscription fee or a fixed technology fee per listing, though this alone is rarely sufficient to avoid classification if other broker-like activities persist.
