### Core Accounting Principle: The "Shorter of" Rule

Under US GAAP, the depreciation period for assets installed under a lease is generally determined by the **shorter of**:
1.  The asset's estimated useful life (20 years).
2.  The lease term (7 years).

However, the specific application depends entirely on **who owns the asset** and how the contract is structured.

### Scenario A: ClearView Glass Retains Ownership (Leased Asset)
If your contract states that ClearView retains title to the glass and the building owner is essentially "renting" the glass functionality:
*   **Classification:** The glass remains on your balance sheet as a fixed asset (or potentially a lease receivable if structured as a sales-type lease).
*   **Depreciation Schedule:** You depreciate the asset over its **20-year useful life**.
*   **Reasoning:** The lease term limits the *revenue* recognition period, but not the physical life of the asset you own. At the end of the 7-year lease, you still own an asset with 13 years of remaining life. You may need to assess impairment if the asset cannot be reused or sold after the lease ends.

### Scenario B: The Building Owner/Tenant Owns the Glass (Leasehold Improvement)
If the contract transfers title to the building owner upon installation (or immediately), or if you are acting as a contractor installing owner-supplied materials:
*   **Classification:** This is a **Leasehold Improvement** for the owner/tenant.
*   **Depreciation Schedule:** The owner must depreciate over the **shorter of the lease term (7 years) or useful life (20 years)**. In this case, **7 years**.
*   **Reasoning:** Since the asset is affixed to the building and cannot be removed without damage, the owner loses the benefit of the asset when the lease expires. Therefore, the economic life to the owner is limited to the lease duration.
*   **Your Revenue Recognition:** If you sold the glass to the owner, you likely recognize the revenue upfront (upon installation/transfer of control) and have no asset to depreciate.

### Scenario C: Sale-Leaseback or Financing Arrangement
If you sold the glass to the building owner and immediately leased it back, or if the arrangement is effectively a financing deal:
*   The accounting becomes complex (ASC 842). You must determine if the transaction is a true sale or a financing. If it is a financing, you keep the asset on your books and depreciate over **20 years**, while amortizing the "loan" over the lease term.
