### Strategic Allocation & Financial Model

**1. Immediate Inventory Allocation (The Rationing Protocol)**
You cannot fulfill 1,200 subscribers with 800 units. Continuing to refund 33% of orders monthly will destroy your reputation. You must immediately shift from a "first-come, first-served" failure model to a **Tiered Access Model**:

*   **Tier A (Guaranteed Supply):** Cap this tier at 750 units (leaving a 50-unit buffer for regulatory variance). Charge a premium price (e.g., $650–$750/month) for guaranteed delivery. This captures higher willingness to pay from pharmacies that cannot afford stockouts.
*   **Tier B (Probabilistic Supply):** Allocate the remaining 50 units to a lower-cost tier ($350–$400/month) with explicit terms stating delivery is "subject to quota availability." If allocation fails, issue credit toward the next month rather than cash refunds to preserve cash flow.
*   **Waitlist Strategy:** Stop accepting new paid subscribers immediately. Convert excess demand into a paid waitlist ($50 refundable deposit) to gauge true demand and generate non-dilutive cash flow without triggering fulfillment obligations.

**2. Cash Flow Solvency Model**
Your current unit economics are positive ($450 Price - $210 Variable Cost = $240 Contribution Margin), but the upfront supplier payment creates a gap.
*   **Current State:** To buy 800 units, you need $168,000 upfront. With 1,200 subscribers, you collect $540,000 monthly, but you are likely refunding ~$180,000 (400 units * $450) while losing future lifetime value.
*   **Optimized State:** By capping subscribers at 800 and raising the price to $600 (blended average), revenue becomes $480,000. Costs remain $168,000. You eliminate refund processing costs and retain the full $312,000 gross profit.
*   **Reserve Strategy:** Do not hold idle cash reserves. In a supply-constrained business, cash is best used to secure **supply chain financing** or negotiate **net-30 terms** with suppliers using your new, higher-margin contract stability as leverage. Holding cash against inflation is less critical than ensuring the liquidity to purchase the next month's quota.

**3. International Expansion & Compliance Risks**
Expanding your supplier network internationally to bypass DEA quotas is **not a viable short-term strategy** and carries extreme risk:
*   **Regulatory Reality:** The DEA quota (21 CFR § 1303) applies to the *importation and manufacturing* of controlled substances within the US, regardless of where the raw ingredient is sourced. You cannot legally import more than your quota allows, even from international suppliers.
*   **Compliance Flags for Counsel:**
    *   **Import Licenses:** Verify if your current DEA registration covers international sourcing of specific rare compounds.
    *   **Foreign Supplier Verification:** FDA requires rigorous verification for foreign drug suppliers; failure here risks import alerts.
    *   **Controlled Substances Act:** Attempting to route materials through third countries to evade quotas is a federal felony.
    *   **Currency & Sanctions:** International payments may trigger OFAC screening delays, worsening your cash flow timing.

**Recommendation:** Do not pursue international sourcing to increase volume. Instead, maximize revenue per unit through Tiered Pricing and use the resulting increased margin to negotiate better payment terms with domestic suppliers.

*Note: This model assumes your variable costs remain stable at higher volumes; verify if premium packaging for Tier A alters this. Consult legal counsel immediately before communicating any changes to "guaranteed" supply terms to existing subscribers.*
