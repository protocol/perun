**Lane R: Regulation-Dependent Routing**

Your question regarding whether holding client funds in a pooled account triggers money transmitter licensing requirements, and the distinction between payment processors and escrow agents, falls strictly under **Lane R**. The correct answer depends entirely on specific state statutes (all 50 US states plus DC), federal guidance from the Financial Crimes Enforcement Network (FinCEN), and binding legal determinations that cannot be resolved through arithmetic or general description.

**The Regulatory Regime**
The regime governing your operation is **State Money Transmitter Laws** and the **Federal Bank Secrecy Act (BSA)**.
*   **The Trigger:** In most jurisdictions, the act of receiving currency or funds from one person and transmitting them to another person or location, even within the same state, constitutes "money transmission." Holding funds in a **pooled account** where your entity controls the release triggers typically creates "control" over the funds, which is the primary factor regulators use to distinguish a money transmitter from a mere technology provider.
*   **Processor vs. Escrow vs. Transmitter:** There is no single federal definition that automatically exempts "escrow agents" or "payment processors" across all 50 states.
    *   **Payment Processors:** Often exempt only if they act strictly as an intermediary between a merchant and a financial institution without ever taking possession or control of the funds (i.e., funds settle directly to the merchant's bank account). Your model of holding funds in a pooled account suggests you *do* take control, likely disqualifying you from standard processor exemptions.
    *   **Escrow Agents:** Some states have specific escrow exemptions, but they often require the escrow agent to be a licensed attorney, a title company, or a specific licensed escrow agent, rather than a general business holding funds. In many states, "escrow" does not override money transmitter licensing if the entity is transmitting funds as a business.
*   **Interstate Commerce:** There is **no general "interstate commerce exemption"** that allows a company to bypass state money transmitter licensing while operating in multiple states. The US Supreme Court and various state courts have consistently upheld that states have the police power to regulate financial activities within their borders to protect consumers, regardless of the interstate nature of the business. Operating without a license in a state where you have customers exposes you to immediate cease-and-desist orders, civil penalties, and potential criminal liability.
