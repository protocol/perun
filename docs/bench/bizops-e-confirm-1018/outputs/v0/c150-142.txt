### Lane R: Regulatory Regime Identification (Route to Professional)
Your question regarding which schedule you *must* use and the resulting specific tax liability falls under **Lane R**. The correct answer depends on binding regulations that I cannot interpret for you.

*   **Regime:** US Federal Tax Law (Internal Revenue Code) and US GAAP (Generally Accepted Accounting Principles).
*   **Key Concepts to Discuss with your CPA:**
    *   **MACRS (Modified Accelerated Cost Recovery System):** The IRS typically assigns specific "recovery periods" to asset classes. You need to determine if the MedBot X1 is classified as "Medical Equipment" (often 5-year or 7-year property) or if the software component forces a different classification.
    *   **Component Depreciation:** Ask if you are required to "bifurcate" the asset—depreciating the mechanical hardware over one period (e.g., 15 years) and the software over a shorter period (e.g., 3-5 years), rather than choosing a single life for the whole unit.
    *   **Book-Tax Differences:** Your CFO is conflating two goals. Financial reporting (GAAP) aims to show economic reality to investors; Tax reporting (IRC) aims to minimize taxable income within legal limits. These often use different schedules simultaneously.
    *   **Section 179 / Bonus Depreciation:** Ask if the full cost can be deducted immediately in the year of purchase under current tax codes, which would override both the 4-year and 15-year arguments for tax purposes.

**Action:** Bring your manufacturing cost breakdown (hardware vs. software labor/materials) and the contract terms to a CPA. Ask specifically: "Does the IRS require componentization for robotic hardware with embedded software, and what is the correct MACRS class life for each component?"

### Lane A: Arithmetic Framework (Apply Your Own Numbers)
While I cannot calculate your specific tax liability without knowing your tax rate and applicable laws, I can provide the arithmetic structure to model the cash flow impact once you have the rules from your CPA.

**The Formula for Annual Depreciation Expense:**
$$ \text{Annual Expense} = \frac{\text{Cost Basis} - \text{Salvage Value}}{\text{Useful Life}} $$

**Scenario A: 15-Year Schedule**
*   **Assumption:** Straight-line depreciation, $0 salvage value.
*   **Annual Expense per Unit:** $\$85,000 / 15 = \$5,667$
*   **Total Annual Expense (12 units):** $\$68,004$
*   **Impact:** Lower annual expense $\rightarrow$ Higher reported net income $\rightarrow$ Higher taxable income (if book/tax match) $\rightarrow$ Higher immediate tax cash outflow.

**Scenario B: 4-Year Schedule**
*   **Assumption:** Straight-line depreciation, $0 salvage value.
*   **Annual Expense per Unit:** $\$85,000 / 4 = \$21,250$
*   **Total Annual Expense (12 units):** $\$255,000$
*   **Impact:** Higher annual expense $\rightarrow$ Lower reported net income $\rightarrow$ Lower taxable income (if book/tax match) $\rightarrow$ Lower immediate tax cash outflow (tax deferral).

**Decision Aid:**
1.  **Cash Flow Reality:** Depreciation is a non-cash expense. It only affects cash flow by reducing the taxes you pay.
2.  **The Trade-off:** The 4-year method front-loads tax savings (better cash flow now) but reduces reported earnings. The 15-year method smooths earnings but delays tax savings.
3.  **Next Step:** Do not guess the life. Request a "Component Depreciation Analysis" from your CPA. If the software is legally distinct, you may be able to expense the software portion over 4 years and the hardware over 15, optimizing both cash flow and earnings representation.

**Note:** The "platform replacement" every 4 years may be treated as a new capital expenditure or a repair expense depending on whether it adds functionality or just maintains security; this distinction is strictly legal/tax.
