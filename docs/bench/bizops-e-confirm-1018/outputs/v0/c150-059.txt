**Lane R: Regulation-Dependent Analysis (Route Required)**
Your core question—whether a specific technical setup (multi-signature with a dead-man's switch) avoids money transmitter classification—is a legal determination, not an arithmetic one. You cannot self-certify compliance to avoid bonding requirements.

*   **The Regime:** In the United States, money transmitter status is determined by state law (e.g., New York BitLicense, Florida Money Transmitter Act) and federal guidance (FinCEN). The definition often hinges on "acceptance and transmission" or "custody" of value.
*   **The Risk Factor:** Regulators increasingly look beyond the label of "non-custodial." If your service holds a key that provides *effective control* or the *ability to unilaterally move funds* (even if triggered by a death event), many jurisdictions may deem this "custody." The "dead-man's switch" mechanism implies you hold the power to execute the transfer upon a condition you verify. This verification and execution step is often where the "transmission" or "custody" trigger occurs.
*   **Jurisdictional Variance:** Wyoming, New York, and Florida have distinct definitions. New York, specifically, has aggressive interpretations of what constitutes custody and transmission. A structure that might pass scrutiny in Wyoming could trigger licensing requirements in New York.
*   **Action:** You must engage counsel to review your specific key management architecture and terms of service. Do not rely on general industry patterns; you need a formal legal opinion on whether your specific multi-sig implementation constitutes "custody" in your target states.
