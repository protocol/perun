**The Call**
Do not implement any AI features that dictate *how* a stylist selects items or *when* they work; immediately audit your current AI prompts to ensure they offer suggestions, not mandates. Your current model (20% commission, no stated hours) leans toward contractor status, but the "AI guidance" is the single highest-risk vector for reclassification under the Department of Labor's (DOL) "economic reality" test.

**The Numbers & Risk Analysis**
*   **Exposure:** $250,000 (estimated back taxes/benefits).
*   **Runway Buffer:** $90,000 cash on hand.
*   **Deficit if Reclassified:** -$160,000 (insolvent without emergency capital).
*   **Unit Economics Context:** 300 stylists × $400/mo = $120,000 total monthly payout. The $150,000 GMV implies a ~$500 average order value or multiple transactions per stylist.

**Tier 2: Operational Thresholds (The "Control" Test)**
The DOL and courts look at the "totality of circumstances," but **behavioral control** is the primary trigger. To maintain independent contractor status, you must avoid AI implementations that function as management tools.

*   **Avoid These AI Behaviors (High Risk):**
    1.  **Mandatory Acceptance:** AI that penalizes stylists (via lower ranking or access loss) for rejecting specific item recommendations.
    2.  **Pricing Dictates:** AI that sets the final sale price or strictly limits the stylist's ability to negotiate or bundle items.
    3.  **Schedule Enforcement:** AI that assigns specific working hours or requires minimum login times to access the tool.
    4.  **Scripted Interaction:** AI that forces stylists to use specific verbiage with clients, removing their personal voice.
    5.  **Exclusivity Loops:** AI that prevents stylists from recommending items from competitors or other platforms.

*   **Safe AI Behaviors (Low Risk):**
    *   Providing data-driven *suggestions* (e.g., "Users who liked X also bought Y") that the stylist can freely ignore.
    *   Offering market trend reports without tying them to performance penalties.
    *   Allowing stylists to curate their own lists entirely, using AI only as a search filter.

**What Flips the Call & The Trigger**
*   **The Flip Input:** The moment your algorithm transitions from "recommending" to "requiring" or "penalizing deviation."
*   **The Trigger:** If you launch a feature that automatically hides stylists from users if their acceptance rate of AI suggestions drops below 80%, you have effectively created an employee relationship.
*   **Rule of Thumb:** If the stylist cannot say "no" to the AI without economic consequence, they are likely an employee.
*   **Step This Week:** Review your AI logic trees. If any branch contains a "must," "required," or automatic penalty for non-compliance, flag it for removal. Replace with "suggested" workflows.

**Routing Note (Tier 3: Jurisdiction-Specific Variable)**
*   **The Unknown Variable:** **State-Level "ABC" Tests vs. Federal Guidelines.** While the federal DOL uses a flexible "economic reality" test, states like **California (AB5)**, **Massachusetts**, and **New Jersey** use a strict "ABC test." Under the ABC test, a worker is presumed an employee unless the company proves, among other things, that the worker performs duties *outside the usual course of the hiring entity's business*.
*   **The Conflict:** If regulators argue that "styling" is the core business of StyleMatch (rather than just providing a marketplace), you fail the ABC test regardless of how much freedom your AI allows. Federal guidelines might let you pass; California law might not.
*   **Action:** Before your next funding round, counsel must opine specifically on whether your platform qualifies for any state-specific marketplace exemptions (e.g., California's Prop 22 landscape or similar statutory carve-outs) to override the ABC test.
