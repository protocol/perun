The claim is that a computer manufacturer should launch a tablet in 2011 using a recently acquired mobile operating system, priced at parity with the market leader, despite having a tiny app catalog and negligible OS market share.

The steelman: Leveraging existing hardware manufacturing prowess and a transitional leadership window to force a third ecosystem into existence via heavy marketing and developer incentives, betting that content will follow distribution rather than precede it.

However, the attack reveals fatal flaws. First, pricing at parity with the dominant leader while offering a fraction of the applications violates basic consumer utility logic; without a unique hardware differentiator explicitly mentioned, the value proposition is negative. Second, the "small app catalog" is not a temporary hurdle but a structural death spiral for tablets in this era; users buy tablets for software, not just hardware build quality. Third, heavy investment in developer outreach cannot instantly solve the chicken-and-egg problem if the user base is unproven and shoppers view the product as risky.

The "do nothing" or "wait" alternative is superior: enter the market later with a matured OS and larger catalog, or price aggressively low to gain share before attempting parity. The proposal ignores the network effect barrier. The pre-mortem shows the device launching to poor reviews regarding app availability, leading to low sales, causing developers to abandon the platform further, resulting in a total write-off of the marketing spend.

The leadership transition adds execution risk precisely when steady hand guidance is needed to nurture a fragile ecosystem. There is no kill criterion or leading metric provided to monitor the ecosystem growth, only a blind bet on "heavy investment." The assumption that advertising can substitute for product-market fit in a platform business is unsupported by the facts given.

This is a classic "build it and they will come" fallacy applied to a two-sided market that requires critical mass to function. Without a distinct hardware advantage or a significantly lower price point to offset the software deficit, the proposal is destined to fail.

DECISION: NO-GO
