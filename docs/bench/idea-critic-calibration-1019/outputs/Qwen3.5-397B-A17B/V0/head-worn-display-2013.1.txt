The claim is that a $1,500 head-worn display with a camera can succeed as a mass-market everyday consumer product starting with an invitation-only early adopter program. The steelman is that exclusivity and high pricing will create aspirational demand strong enough to overcome privacy fears, utility gaps, and social stigma, establishing a new computing platform before competitors.

However, the proposal fails the "everyday use" test for the general public. The risks identified are not merely hurdles but fundamental blockers for mass adoption at this price point. A $1,500 entry cost restricts the device to enthusiasts, yet the value proposition relies on "everyday" utility which currently does not exist for that demographic. Privacy objections regarding a always-on camera in public spaces create immediate social friction and potential regulatory backlash, rendering the device socially toxic rather than aspirational. The "invitation-only" strategy may generate hype, but it does not solve the core utility deficit; without a killer app beyond novelty, the device becomes an expensive liability.

The "do nothing" alternative (waiting for battery tech to improve, privacy norms to settle, and use cases to emerge organically on smartphones) carries less reputational risk than launching a product likely to be banned from venues and mocked socially. The cost of delay is low compared to the cost of a failed public launch that cements the form factor as intrusive. There is no evidence provided that the "early adopter" segment is large enough to sustain the ecosystem required to make the product useful for the general public later. The kill criterion—social acceptance and clear daily utility—is not met by the current specs.

This is a one-way door for brand reputation; once labeled "creepy" or "useless," repositioning is nearly impossible. The proposal assumes hype substitutes for utility, a fatal flaw for hardware at this price.

DECISION: NO-GO
