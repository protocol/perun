The claim is that a full rewrite with altered voting mechanics and a forced migration will reverse slowing traffic. The steelman is that only a radical break from legacy constraints can modernize content surfacing enough to outpace a growing rival.

However, the attack reveals fatal flaws in the current plan. First, the core community values existing voting mechanics; changing these while forcing a migration risks alienating the loyal user base that drives the site's value, potentially accelerating decline rather than reversing it. Second, a "big bang" launch with no rollback capability creates a single point of failure; if the new algorithm fails to engage users, the site cannot revert, handing the rival an insurmountable advantage. Third, the engineering risk of a from-scratch rewrite diverts resources from iterative improvements that could address traffic slumps without existential risk.

The proposal fails the "do nothing" and "iterative change" alternatives. A better approach would be A/B testing the new surfacing logic on a subset of users or introducing publisher-favored content within the existing architecture to validate engagement before committing to a rewrite. The cost of delay in testing is lower than the cost of a catastrophic, irreversible launch.

The pre-mortem is clear: the launch occurs, the community rebels against the changed voting rules, traffic plummets, and the inability to rollback forces a prolonged outage or a fractured user base. The evidence provided highlights specific risks (community values, engineering size, no rollback) that the proposal ignores rather than mitigates. There are no kill criteria or phased gates to manage the transition.

This plan bets the company on an unproven hypothesis with no safety net. It violates the principle of reversibility for high-stakes changes and ignores the specific sensitivities of the existing user base. The rivalry demands agility, not a monolithic gamble.

DECISION: NO-GO
