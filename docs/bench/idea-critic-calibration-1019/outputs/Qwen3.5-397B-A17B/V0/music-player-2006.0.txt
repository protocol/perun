**Steelman:** Leverage existing ecosystem loyalty and marketing muscle to disrupt a complacent leader by offering superior hardware (screen, wireless sharing) and a unified store/subscription, betting that user experience trumps first-mover advantage.

**Attack:** The proposal ignores critical structural barriers. First, dropping the industry-standard copy-protection alienates existing label partners who licensed content based on that specific DRM; without their catalog, the store fails immediately. Second, "wireless sharing" directly conflicts with label restrictions mentioned in the risks; if labels block this feature, the primary hardware differentiator vanishes. Third, entering a saturated market late with no price advantage requires convincing users to switch ecosystems entirely, a friction point marketing budgets rarely overcome without a unique, legally viable content hook. The "large marketing budget" cannot fix a broken supply chain or illegal feature set.

**Better Way:** Instead of a full frontal assault with a new ecosystem, license the existing dominant player's software or store infrastructure to bundle with the new hardware, focusing solely on hardware differentiation. Alternatively, delay the launch until DRM-free agreements are secured industry-wide, rather than unilaterally dropping standards and risking a content blackout.

**Kill Criteria:** If major labels refuse to license content without the proposed DRM standard within 90 days, or if legal counsel confirms "wireless sharing" violates current licensing terms, the project must stop. The leading metric is the percentage of the top 100 labels signed with terms allowing the proposed sharing features.

**Verdict:** The proposal relies on features that likely violate existing contracts and assumes marketing can override content scarcity. The risks are not just competitive but existential regarding partner relationships. The plan lacks a verified path to secure content rights under the new, relaxed DRM model.

DECISION: NO-GO
