The proposal describes a "blitzscaling" strategy reliant on perpetual capital availability and negative unit economics to purchase market share. The steelman is that rapid dominance will create a monopoly allowing future price hikes to recoup losses. However, the attack reveals fatal flaws in the current context (early 2000).

First, the plan requires selling products below cost while incurring high shipping expenses on heavy items, creating a deeply negative gross margin. This burns cash at an accelerating rate with every sale. Second, the strategy depends on an IPO and open capital markets to fund this burn. Given the era's volatility and the explicit risk that markets could close, a single sentiment shift would cut off funding immediately. Third, acquiring a competitor while both parties bleed cash on TV ads and below-cost goods consolidates losses rather than creating value.

The "do nothing" or "slow growth" alternative preserves capital and tests unit economics before scaling. The proposed path has no kill criteria; it assumes success is inevitable if spending continues. A pre-mortem shows the company runs out of cash 18 months post-IPO when markets tighten, leaving massive debt from warehouse construction and no path to profitability because the customer base was bought, not earned.

The strongest attack survived is the fear of losing first-mover advantage. However, being the first to go bankrupt at scale offers no strategic benefit. Without a verified path to positive unit economics or a guaranteed multi-year capital commitment, this plan is a bet on market irrationality lasting longer than the company's cash runway. The risks of negative margins, heavy shipping costs, and capital market closure outweigh the speculative benefit of rapid expansion. The proposal fails to demonstrate how the company survives if the IPO underperforms or if the ad campaign fails to convert to retention.

DECISION: NO-GO
