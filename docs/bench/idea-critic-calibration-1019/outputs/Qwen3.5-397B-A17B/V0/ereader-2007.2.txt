The claim is that a non-hardware bookseller should launch a subsidized e-reader with free cellular data to dominate the emerging digital market. The steelman: owning the device creates a frictionless, closed loop for content sales, locking out competitors and overcoming piracy fears via convenience rather than DRM alone.

However, the skeptic identifies fatal gaps in the provided facts. First, the company has zero hardware experience, yet the proposal requires designing a custom device and negotiating global cellular data contracts—two massive operational leaps with no stated mitigation or partners. Second, the economics are undefined: if the device is subsidized and data is free, the unit economics rely entirely on future book margins. With publishers already fearing margin squeeze, their willingness to support the low-price model required to recoup hardware losses is unverified. Third, "cannibalization" is treated as a risk, but without a model showing that digital margins exceed print margins net of hardware subsidy, this could destroy overall profitability.

The better-way analysis suggests a cheaper alternative: license existing hardware or launch a software-only store first to validate demand before committing to capital-intensive hardware and telecom deals. The proposal ignores the "do nothing" or "partner" options which avoid the highest risks.

The pre-mortem reveals a likely failure mode: the device launches late and over-budget due to inexperience, publishers refuse the low-price tier seeing no volume guarantee, and the company bleeds cash on unsold inventory and unused data plans. The "one-way door" nature of hardware manufacturing makes this irreversible compared to software tests.

Crucially, the proposal lacks a specific kill criterion or a capped first step. It asks for a full launch commitment without a pilot, a specific target for device adoption, or a defined break-even point for the subsidy. Entering a capital-intensive hardware market with unproven unit economics and no stated path to publisher alignment is reckless without a phased approach. The risks of execution failure and negative unit economics are too high to approve as proposed.

DECISION: NO-GO
