The claim is that a mandatory online client will solve update/cheating issues and create a new store revenue stream. The steelman: centralizing distribution creates a defensible moat against cheaters, ensures 100% patch compliance, and captures long-tail revenue via a third-party marketplace, outweighing short-term retail friction.

However, the proposal fails the "fit" and "pre-mortem" checks for the 2003 context.
1. **Infrastructure Mismatch:** In 2003, universal broadband is an assumption, not a fact. A mandatory large download for boxed goods alienates the dial-up majority, directly conflicting with the goal of selling "very popular" games through existing retail channels.
2. **Channel Suicide:** Forcing a digital gate on physical goods invites immediate retaliation from retail partners who drive current revenue. Dropping these games is a high-probability outcome, not a mere risk.
3. **Adoption Barrier:** Players dislike mandatory accounts; combining this with connectivity hurdles creates a "double friction" event that encourages piracy (the very problem anti-cheat aims to stop) rather than preventing it.
4. **Scope Creep:** Building a store for *other* developers is a distinct, capital-intensive business model that distracts from the core engineering crisis (updates/cheating).

The "do nothing" alternative (optional patches, serial-code verification) preserves retail relationships while solving 80% of the cheating issue without the infrastructure bet. The "cheaper path" is an optional launcher for digital bonuses, not a mandatory gate for physical media.

The strongest attack survived is the fear of competitors copying the idea; however, being first to fail in a low-bandwidth environment is worse than being second to succeed. The proposal conflates a technical fix (anti-cheat) with a platform pivot (store) during a period of insufficient market readiness. The cost of delay (waiting for broadband saturation) is lower than the cost of immediate channel collapse.

This is a one-way door with irreversible retail damage. The kill criterion for the original goal (fight cheating) is met by less invasive means; the expansion goal (store) lacks a viable user base in 2003.

DECISION: NO-GO
