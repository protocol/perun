The claim is that a computer and music player maker can successfully enter the phone market by launching a premium, keyboard-less touchscreen device with exclusive carrier distribution, despite lacking industry experience and third-party apps.

Steelman: By controlling the entire stack (hardware, software, services) and securing revenue sharing, the company can deliver a superior user experience that justifies a premium price, disrupting the carrier-dominated model where handsets are commoditized.

However, critical failures loom. First, the "no keyboard" assumption ignores the 2007 enterprise and prosumer dominance of physical keyboards (BlackBerry, Treo); touch typing without haptic feedback or proven accuracy is a massive adoption barrier. Second, the "single carrier exclusivity" strategy severely limits market reach during the critical launch window, relying entirely on one partner's marketing efficacy against entrenched competitors. Third, the lack of third-party applications at launch creates a "feature phone" perception despite the smart pricing, risking developer abandonment before the platform proves viability. Finally, the premium pricing strategy assumes consumers value the integrated music/web experience over established business utility and keyboard reliability, an unproven hypothesis in the mobile sector.

Better ways exist: license the OS to multiple carriers to maximize reach, include a physical keyboard hybrid to mitigate input risk, or delay launch until a developer ecosystem is secured. The "do nothing" option preserves high-margin music player sales without risking brand equity on an unproven form factor.

Kill criteria: If pre-orders do not exceed 500,000 units in the first quarter, or if carrier churn exceeds 5% due to device incompatibility, the project must pivot to a multi-carrier, lower-price strategy immediately. The leading metric is "active daily users" vs. "returns due to input difficulty."

The strongest attack survived is the carrier control model; while risky, it prevents the fragmentation that plagues competitors, but only if the user experience is flawless. Yet, the absence of a keyboard and apps makes "flawless" unlikely at launch. The risk of brand damage from a high-profile failure in an unfamiliar market outweighs the potential erosion of music player sales.

DECISION: NO-GO
