The claim is that offering 1,000x more storage than incumbents, funded by content-matched ads, will disrupt the mature email market despite high infrastructure costs and privacy risks.

Steelman: If storage scarcity is the primary user pain point and privacy concerns are outweighed by utility, a massive capacity jump creates a viral wedge that ad revenue can sustain.

However, the proposal fails the skepticism and kill-criteria tests on the facts provided. First, the economic model relies on text ads matching message content, yet the proposal explicitly cites "privacy objections to scanning mail" as a known risk without offering a mitigation strategy. In 2004, trust is the currency of email; scanning private correspondence for profit without an opt-in or clear legal shield invites regulatory backlash and user abandonment before network effects can form. The "invitation-only" launch limits initial revenue while storage costs accrue immediately, creating a negative cash flow gap with no defined bridge.

Second, the "better-way" analysis reveals a fatal flaw in the cost assumption. Providing a gigabyte per user when competitors offer megabytes increases storage liability by three orders of magnitude. If users do not delete messages (the very behavior this service encourages), the marginal cost per user remains permanently high. Ad revenue per user in 2004 is unproven at this scale. If the take-rate on ads is even slightly lower than projected, the service becomes an insolvent sinkhole. The proposal treats "fast search" as a differentiator, but search is useless if the service shuts down due to unsustainable burn rates.

Finally, the kill criteria are absent. There is no threshold for cost-per-user or adoption rate that would trigger a stop. Launching without a proven unit-economic model where costs are fixed (storage) and revenue is variable (ads) is a gamble, not a strategy. The risk of privacy scandals combined with uncapped infrastructure liability outweighs the potential first-mover advantage in a market where users are already accustomed to deleting mail to manage limits. The proposal assumes behavior change (hoarding email) without validating the economic engine to support it.

DECISION: NO-GO
