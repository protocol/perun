The claim is that committing to a massive, unproven wide-body jet will secure market dominance and meet a specific customer's need for lower seat costs. The steelman: this move preempts competitors, locks in the largest customer, and creates a dual-use asset (passenger/freight) that survives the supersonic transition.

However, the risks are existential. The development outlay exceeds all prior history, creating a single-point failure where any delay or technical flaw bankrupts the firm. Relying on an unproven engine from a supplier introduces a critical external dependency with no backup. The market assumption—that subsonic travel will grow despite the expected supersonic revolution—is a direct contradiction of current industry consensus. If supersonic jets become the standard, this massive subsonic investment becomes a stranded asset immediately. Furthermore, relying on a single airline order for such a colossal gamble violates basic diversification principles; if that carrier falters or changes its mind, the project collapses with no other buyers to absorb the capacity.

The "better way" is to incrementally stretch existing models or wait for the supersonic market to clarify before betting the company. A "do nothing" stance preserves capital for the inevitable sonic boom era. The cost of delay is low compared to the cost of failure, which is total insolvency.

The pre-mortem is clear: the engine fails certification, the factory burns cash without volume, and the supersonic shift renders the design obsolete before it pays back. The door is one-way; once this money is spent, there is no retreat. The evidence provided supports only one airline's desire, not a market reality. The assumption that subsonic demand will outstrip supersonic adoption is unverified and highly contentious. Without multiple launch customers, a guaranteed engine contract, and a definitive study disproving the supersonic threat, the variance of outcomes is too skewed toward zero.

DECISION: NO-GO
