The claim is that an annual membership will reduce friction and increase loyalty despite short-term margin hits. The steelman: locking in repeat customers via upfront fees creates predictable revenue and increases order frequency enough to offset shipping losses through volume efficiencies and reduced price sensitivity.

However, critical failures loom. First, the "heavy buyer" risk is existential: if the top 20% of customers double their order frequency, variable shipping costs could vastly exceed the $79 fixed revenue per user, creating a negative marginal contribution on every additional order. Second, the proposal admits economics are "unproven"; launching without a pilot or capped cohort turns the entire customer base into a live test for an unverified unit model. Third, reversibility is low; once customers pay upfront, revoking the benefit triggers immediate churn and reputational damage, effectively trapping the retailer in a losing contract.

Better ways exist. A tiered threshold (e.g., free shipping over $50) tests price elasticity without capping unlimited liability. A monthly subscription allows faster iteration and easier exit than an annual commitment. A limited-time pilot for invited-only high-value customers would validate the "frequency vs. cost" hypothesis before exposing the whole base.

The pre-mortem reveals a cash-flow crisis: heavy adoption in Q1 leads to massive shipping outflows in Q2 before renewal revenue arrives, potentially breaking working capital. The cost of delay is low; waiting one quarter to run a controlled pilot prevents a structural margin error.

Without verified data proving that increased frequency yields net-positive contribution after shipping, and without a mechanism to cap liability or test reversibility, this is a gamble, not a strategy. The lack of a specific kill criterion (e.g., "stop if average orders per member < X") makes failure detection too late.

DECISION: NO-GO
