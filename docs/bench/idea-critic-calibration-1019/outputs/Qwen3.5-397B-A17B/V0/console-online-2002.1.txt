The claim is that a mandatory, paid, broadband-only online service will succeed despite market norms of free, publisher-run multiplayer and low broadband adoption in 2002.

Steelman: A unified, high-quality service creates a premium ecosystem that differentiates the console, justifying the subscription through superior reliability, identity, and social features that fragmented free alternatives cannot match.

However, the attack reveals critical failures. First, the "broadband required" constraint ignores the verified state of 2002: dial-up dominates. Mandating broadband excludes the vast majority of the installed base, artificially capping the addressable market before launch. Second, the "paid subscription" model contradicts the established consumer expectation that online play is a value-add, not a recurring toll. Competitors offering free services create a powerful alternative; unless the quality gap is insurmountable (unproven here), users will reject the toll. Third, the company lacks verified operational capacity for hosting and support. Building this from scratch while launching hardware is a massive execution risk that could degrade the core console experience.

Better ways exist: Offer a hybrid model (free basic matchmaking, paid premium features) or support dial-up to maximize reach. Do nothing and let publishers handle it, avoiding operational debt.

Pre-mortem: The service launches with low adoption due to the broadband barrier. Gamers revolt against the double cost (console + subscription). The company burns cash on underutilized servers, failing to achieve economies of scale, and the brand becomes synonymous with "expensive" and "exclusive" in a negative sense.

Evidence is missing on the actual percentage of broadband homes willing to pay extra. The risks of excluding the majority of users and entering an unproven operational domain without a fallback are too high. The proposal fails the "do no harm" test by actively shrinking the potential user base via the broadband mandate.

DECISION: NO-GO
