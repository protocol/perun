The claim is that a computer and music player maker can disrupt the mobile market by launching a premium, keyboard-less touchscreen phone with exclusive carrier control, despite having no phone industry experience.

Steelman: The convergence of computing, music, and communication into a single, software-controlled device justifies a premium price and carrier revenue sharing, rendering physical keyboards obsolete and shifting power from carriers to the manufacturer.

However, critical failures loom. First, the "no keyboard" assumption contradicts the dominant business market preference for physical input in 2007; if touch typing accuracy fails to exceed 90% of physical keyboard speed, adoption will stall. Second, exclusivity with a single carrier severely caps initial market reach; if the partner carrier's network coverage or brand reputation is weak in key demographics, sales will miss targets regardless of product quality. Third, the lack of third-party applications at launch creates a barren ecosystem; if competitors release app stores within six months, this device becomes a static tool rather than a platform, accelerating obsolescence.

Better ways exist: licensing the software to multiple handset makers to gain immediate scale without hardware risk, or launching with a hybrid input method to ease the transition for business users. Doing nothing preserves current music player dominance while observing how the keyboard-less experiment fails or succeeds elsewhere.

The pre-mortem reveals failure via carrier friction: the partner demands bloatware or branding changes, breaking the "control hardware and software" condition. This is a one-way door; once manufactured, the hardware cannot be undone. The cost of delay is high, as music player erosion is already occurring, but the cost of a failed exclusive launch is catastrophic brand damage.

Evidence is purely hypothetical as no data exists yet. The assumption that users will accept a virtual keyboard is unproven. The assumption that one carrier will accept revenue sharing is unprecedented.

Strongest attack survived: The fear of no keyboard is mitigated only if the multi-touch interface offers a qualitatively new experience (pinch-to-zoom, fluid scrolling) that makes physical keys feel archaic, not just different.

DECISION: NO-GO
