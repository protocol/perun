The claim is that a mandatory, paid, broadband-only online service will succeed in 2002 despite low broadband adoption, a market expectation of free online play, and the company's lack of operational experience in running such a service.

The steelman: A unified, high-quality paid service creates a premium ecosystem that differentiates the console, funds robust infrastructure, and locks in users via a shared identity and friends list, outweighing the friction of cost and connectivity.

However, the failure hypothesis is strong: The combination of low broadband penetration in 2002 and resistance to paying for online play (when rivals offer it free or publisher-managed) will severely restrict the addressable market. If the player base remains too small due to these barriers, matchmaking fails, rendering the core value proposition void. Furthermore, the company has no proven capability to operate the required hosting and support infrastructure; building this from scratch while simultaneously trying to convert a skeptical user base introduces massive execution risk.

Alternatives include a hybrid model (free basic, paid premium), publisher-managed servers with optional central identity, or delaying the mandatory subscription until broadband adoption reaches a critical threshold. The proposal beats "do nothing" only if the premium experience drives hardware sales enough to offset software friction, which is unproven.

The pre-mortem: The service launches, subscription uptake is 5% of the installed base, server costs per user skyrocket due to low density, and the company is forced to sunset the service or make it free, damaging the brand. This is a one-way door for reputation and capital allocation.

The evidence provided highlights fundamental mismatches between the proposal's requirements (broadband, payment) and the 2002 market reality (dial-up dominance, free expectation). Without a verified plan to bridge this gap or a pilot proving willingness to pay, the risk of catastrophic failure outweighs the potential upside. The lack of operational history compounds this.

DECISION: NO-GO
