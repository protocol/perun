The claim is that committing to a massive, unproven wide-body jet will secure market dominance despite existential financial risk and shifting technological tides. The steelman: this specific design (raised cockpit, high-bypass engines) creates a dual-use asset (passenger/freight) that locks in the largest customer and preempts competitors, betting that subsonic demand growth will outpace the supersonic threat.

However, the failure hypothesis is catastrophic insolvency. The development and factory outlay exceeds all prior company history, relying on a single customer order. If that airline cancels, or if the unproven engine fails certification, the company faces bankruptcy. The "supersonic replacement" risk is not merely theoretical; if regulators or markets pivot to speed over capacity within the development window, this asset becomes obsolete before entry. Furthermore, "designed for later conversion" adds immediate complexity and cost for a future revenue stream that is not guaranteed.

The "do nothing" alternative preserves capital to wait for engine maturity or supersonic clarity. A "better way" would be stretching the existing airframe incrementally or partnering to share the massive tooling risk, rather than bearing it alone. The proposal lacks a kill criterion: there is no defined metric for engine performance or secondary orders that would trigger a stop before total commitment. The cost of delay is low compared to the cost of a wrong bet; waiting one year for engine data could save the firm.

The strongest attack is the concentration of risk: one customer, one unproven engine, and a bet against the industry's expected shift to supersonic travel. The evidence provided (only one order, unproven tech, massive outlay) suggests the probability of ruin outweighs the potential reward at this specific moment. The proposal asks for a binary "all-in" commitment without intermediate gates or shared risk mechanisms. Without a verified second customer or a proven engine test bed, the rational decision is to withhold approval until these de-risking milestones are met.

DECISION: NO-GO
