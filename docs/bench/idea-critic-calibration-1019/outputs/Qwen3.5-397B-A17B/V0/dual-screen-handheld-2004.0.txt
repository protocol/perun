The proposal to launch a dual-screen, touch-sensitive handheld at a lower price point represents a strategic pivot from competing on raw graphical power to competing on novel interaction and accessibility. This is a classic "blue ocean" maneuver: rather than engaging in a costly arms race against a well-funded rival with superior media specs, the company leverages unique form factors to broaden the audience beyond core gamers.

The risks identified—developer confusion, unproven touch mechanics, and the "gimmick" label—are valid but manageable through strong first-party software support that demonstrates the hardware's unique value. The decision to keep the older handheld on sale is critical; it maintains revenue from the core demographic while the new device cultivates a broader market, effectively creating a two-tier ecosystem that maximizes market coverage.

Matching the rival's hardware would likely result in a me-too product with higher costs and no distinct advantage. Conversely, this asymmetric approach forces the rival to compete on a battlefield where they lack dominance: intuitive, novel gameplay rather than polygon counts. The lower price point reduces the barrier to entry for the target "broad audience," mitigating the risk of the hardware being perceived as an expensive novelty.

While the dual-screen design adds manufacturing complexity, the strategic benefit of differentiation outweighs the cost, provided the software library immediately showcases the utility of the second screen and touch input. The potential to unlock non-traditional gamers justifies the deviation from standard hardware conventions. The "gimmick" concern is neutralized only if the launch titles prove the mechanic is essential, not incidental. Given the dominance in handhelds and the need to evolve past a pure specs war, this is the necessary evolutionary step.
