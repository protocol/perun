The claim is that a computer and music player maker can successfully launch a premium, keyboard-less touchscreen phone via an exclusive carrier partnership that cedes design control and shares revenue, despite lacking phone industry experience.

Steelman: By integrating a superior music player and full web browser into a revolutionary touchscreen interface, the company can create a new market category that makes existing keyboard phones obsolete, justifying a premium price and exclusive carrier deal to fund the risk.

However, critical failures loom. First, the "no physical keyboard" assumption ignores the 2007 enterprise standard where Blackberry dominates; business users may reject a glass screen for heavy typing, destroying the premium value proposition. Second, the "exclusive carrier" strategy creates a single point of failure; if that carrier's network quality is poor or marketing weak, the product dies with no alternative distribution. Third, "no third-party applications" at launch leaves the device vulnerable to competitors who offer immediate ecosystem utility, potentially rendering the hardware a closed toy rather than a platform.

Better ways exist: License the OS to multiple carriers to maximize reach, or include a physical keyboard to hedge against touchscreen adoption risks. Doing nothing preserves current music player dominance without risking capital on an unproven handset form factor.

The pre-mortem reveals the most likely failure: The device launches, tech enthusiasts praise the screen, but mainstream business users and carriers reject the high price and lack of keyboard/apps, causing sales to stall before the network effect kicks in. The cost of delay is high as music players are already eroding, but the cost of a failed flagship launch is existential.

The strongest attack survived is the carrier control demand. While carriers usually dictate terms, the unique value of the integrated music player and web browser might force a concession. Yet, without apps or a keyboard, this leverage is unproven. The proposal assumes the market will instantly value "experience" over "features," a dangerous bet without a proven mobile track record.

Given the lack of third-party apps, the alien form factor for business users, and the extreme risk of single-carrier exclusivity for a first-generation product, the failure scenarios outweigh the potential rewards at this stage.

DECISION: NO-GO
