The proposal deliberately sacrifices hardware superiority to win on portability, battery life, and price. The "weak" monochrome screen and modest processor are not oversights but strategic constraints enabling 10+ hour battery life and a sub-$90 price point, directly addressing the primary failure modes of previous single-game devices and high-cost rivals.

**Alternatives Considered:**
1.  **Match Rival Specs:** Would require a color screen and stronger CPU, likely doubling the cost to ~$180+, reducing battery life to <5 hours, and targeting a niche enthusiast market rather than mass adoption.
2.  **Do Nothing:** Cedes the portable market entirely to the rival, risking long-term brand erosion as gamers adopt a competing ecosystem.
3.  **Mid-Tier Compromise:** A slightly better screen at $130 fails to differentiate on price or battery, getting crushed between the premium rival and budget toys.

**Pre-Mortem & Kill Criteria:**
Assume failure: The device launches and sits unsold. Why? Developers refuse to build games because the screen is "too old," or parents reject it as a "black and white toy" despite the price.
*   **Door Type:** Two-way. If sales miss targets by 50% in Q1, production can be halted with limited sunk cost compared to a full console launch.
*   **Cost of Delay:** High. Every month delayed allows the rival to lock in exclusive third-party titles and define the category standard.
*   **Kill Criteria:** If pre-orders do not cover 40% of the initial manufacturing run by launch month, pause secondary marketing spend. If attach rate (games per unit) is below 1.5 after six months, re-evaluate software strategy.

**Strongest Attack Survived:**
*Objection:* "A monochrome screen is a non-starter in 1989; consumers demand color."
*Rebuttal:* History shows mass markets prioritize price and utility (battery life) over fidelity when the core loop is engaging. The bundled puzzle game demonstrates immediate value, turning the hardware limitation into a focused gameplay strength. The $20M marketing budget must explicitly frame the screen as a feature for clarity and battery, not a defect.

The risk of thin margins is mitigated by the razor-and-blades model: low hardware margin drives high-volume software sales, where the real profit lies. The proposal correctly identifies that winning the platform war requires ubiquity, not spec-sheet dominance.
