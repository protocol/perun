The claim is that acquiring a small, unprofitable software firm with a modern OS is the viable path to save a declining computer maker, despite high costs, migration risks, and a contentious founder history.

**Steelman:** Internal replacement has failed twice; the target's object-oriented architecture is the only proven modern foundation available; the founder's return is essential for technical vision; licensing is a strategic dead end.

**Attack & Alternatives:**
1.  **Do Nothing:** Continued market share erosion leads to insolvency. Status quo is fatal.
2.  **License Competitor:** Surrenders control of the core differentiator, reduces margins, and delays differentiation. It treats the symptom, not the disease.
3.  **Build Internally (Again):** Two prior failures suggest structural inability to execute this specific complexity without external catalyst.

**Pre-mortem:** The acquisition fails if the technical integration stalls due to cultural friction with the founder, or if the developer ecosystem rejects the migration path. However, the "few hundred million" price, while high relative to current revenue, is likely below the cost of a third failed internal attempt plus the opportunity cost of further market loss. The target's low sales are irrelevant; the asset is the IP and engineering talent, not the commercial track record.

**Fit:** This moves the North Star from "managing decline" to "regaining technical leadership." The risk of the founder's difficult history is mitigated by limiting their role to "advisor" rather than immediate operational control.

**Kill Criteria:** If the technical due diligence reveals the OS cannot scale to the maker's hardware requirements, or if the founder demands operational control contrary to the advisor mandate, the deal stops. The leading metric is the successful beta release of the new OS within 18 months.

**Verdict:** The alternatives are worse. Licensing creates dependency; internal builds have a 100% failure rate in this context. The acquisition buys a working engine and the specific human capital required to finish it. The financial premium is the cost of survival.

DECISION: GO
