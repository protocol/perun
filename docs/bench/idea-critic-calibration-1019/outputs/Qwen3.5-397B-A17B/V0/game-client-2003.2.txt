The claim is that a mandatory online client will solve update delivery and cheating while creating a new revenue stream via a third-party store. The steelman: centralizing authentication and delivery creates a defensible moat against cheaters, ensures 100% patch compliance, and captures platform margins long-term, outweighing short-term retail friction.

However, the proposal fails the "fit" and "pre-mortem" tests against the 2003 context. Home broadband was not universal; mandating online access alienates a massive portion of the PC gaming market that relies on dial-up or LAN play. The risk of retail partners dropping boxed games is immediate and existential, as digital distribution infrastructure is immature and cannot yet replace physical shelf space. The "do nothing" alternative (patches via website, manual anti-cheat) preserves retail revenue while the technology matures.

The pre-mortem reveals a likely failure mode: early client bugs combined with mandatory always-online requirements cause a player revolt, driving users to pirated cracked versions that bypass the client entirely, while retailers delist the games in retaliation. The cost of delay (waiting 1-2 years for broadband penetration) is low compared to the cost of premature execution (brand destruction and channel collapse). The proposal attempts to build a platform (store for other developers) before securing the core utility (reliable updates for own games), violating the principle of focusing on the primary constraint first.

While the strategic direction toward digital is correct, the timing and the "mandatory" nature for *new* games in 2003 constitute a fatal mismatch with market reality. The risks of channel conflict and user exclusion are not mitigated by the proposed plan; they are exacerbated by the lack of a fallback offline mode. The idea should be held until broadband adoption crosses a critical threshold or implemented as an optional beta for a single title, not a mandate for all new releases.

DECISION: NO-GO
