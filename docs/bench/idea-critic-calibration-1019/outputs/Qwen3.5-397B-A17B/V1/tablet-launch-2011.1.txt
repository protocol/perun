The claim is that a computer manufacturer should launch a tablet in 2011 using a recently acquired mobile operating system, priced competitively despite a tiny app catalog and negligible OS market share, relying on heavy marketing to overcome these deficits.

**Steelman:** The company can leverage its brand loyalty and marketing budget to force a foothold in the tablet market before the ecosystem gap becomes insurmountable, betting that hardware quality and aggressive developer incentives will rapidly close the app gap.

**Skeptic Attack:** The proposal ignores the critical "chicken-and-egg" failure mode of platform businesses. In 2011, tablet buyers prioritize app availability over hardware specs or price parity. A small catalog is not a minor risk; it is a fatal flaw for a premium-priced device. Heavy advertising for a product with missing core utility (apps) burns cash while accelerating negative word-of-mouth. The "market leader" dominance creates a network effect barrier that marketing alone cannot breach without a differentiated value proposition beyond "we exist."

**Better Way:** The "do nothing" or "delay" option is stronger. Wait until the OS has organic traction or the app catalog reaches a viability threshold. Alternatively, price significantly below the leader to compensate for the lack of apps, targeting a budget segment rather than competing directly on prestige with a hollow product. Launching at parity price with a fraction of the utility is economically irrational.

**Kill Criteria:** If the app catalog does not include the top 50 essential tablet applications by launch, the product will fail to retain users regardless of ad spend. The risk of brand damage from a high-profile flop during leadership transition outweighs the potential first-mover advantage in this specific configuration.

**Verdict:** The proposal asks to spend heavily to sell an incomplete product at a premium price in a winner-take-most market. The lack of apps is a structural blocker that advertising cannot fix. The plan fails the "fit" test against market realities and the "pre-mortem" reveals immediate obsolescence upon review.

DECISION: NO-GO
