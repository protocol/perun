Steelman: Acquiring the distressed department chain provides immediate scale and real estate to pivot the discount chain's format faster than organic growth allows, betting that combined purchasing power and cross-selling can reverse declining sales before cash runs out.

However, this claim fails the "fit" and "pre-mortem" tests. Both entities suffer from declining sales and underinvested stores; combining two weakening balance sheets creates a larger, slower-moving entity rather than a revitalized one. The proposal admits cost savings are uncertain and customer bases differ, undermining the core synergy thesis. In a market where agile big-box rivals are growing, merging two legacy formats diverts critical management attention and capital into complex integration (converting stores, merging functions) instead of fixing the fundamental value proposition.

The "do nothing" or "smaller pilot" alternative is superior. Organic conversion of a few discount stores to test the combined format carries a fraction of the risk and preserves cash. Acquiring a larger, older chain introduces massive integration debt and cultural friction without a proven path to revenue growth. The pre-mortem is clear: the merged entity burns cash on restructuring while sales continue to slide due to format irrelevance, leading to a second insolvency event faster than either would face alone. The lack of verified cost savings and the mismatch in customer demographics are fatal flaws in a turnaround scenario where execution margin for error is zero.

This is a one-way door with high capital exposure and no leading metric to validate the synergy hypothesis before full commitment. The proposal relies on hope that "combined" equals "better," ignoring evidence that both models are currently failing. Without a capped, time-boxed pilot to prove the cross-selling concept, the full acquisition is an unjustifiable gamble.

DECISION: NO-GO
