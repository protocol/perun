The claim is that a full rewrite with altered voting mechanics and a forced migration will reverse slowing traffic. The steelman is that only a radical overhaul can modernize the platform and appease publishers enough to spark growth.

However, the attack reveals fatal flaws in this approach. First, the core community values the existing voting mechanics; changing them risks alienating the loyal user base that generates the content, effectively killing the network effect while trying to save it. Second, a "big bang" launch to all users eliminates the ability to roll back if the new algorithm fails, creating a single point of catastrophic failure. Third, rewriting from scratch introduces massive engineering risk and delay, allowing the growing rival to capture more market share during the development void.

The "do nothing" or "iterative change" alternative is superior. A/B testing the new surfacing logic on a subset of traffic would validate the publisher-focused approach without endangering the whole community. A phased migration allows for rollback, mitigating the technical risk. The proposal fails to address why the specific pain point (slowing traffic) requires a total rewrite rather than targeted optimization.

The pre-mortem is clear: the launch occurs, the community revolts against the changed voting rules, engagement plummets, and the site cannot revert because the old codebase is deprecated. The rival site absorbs the disgruntled users. The cost of delay for a rewrite is high, but the cost of a failed launch is existential.

This proposal lacks a safety valve. It bets the entire company on an unproven algorithm and a monolithic deployment strategy. The risks of community alienation and technical inability to rollback constitute a concrete failure scenario that the proposal's "all at once" strategy explicitly invites.

DECISION: NO-GO
