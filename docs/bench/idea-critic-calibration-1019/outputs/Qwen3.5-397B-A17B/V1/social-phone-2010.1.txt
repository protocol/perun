The claim is that launching two social-networking phones on a new OS, via a single carrier with a mandatory expensive data plan and no app store, will successfully capture the teenage market in 2010.

Steelman: Leveraging an acquired messaging brand allows a focused, carrier-subsidized entry into the teen segment, bypassing the complexity of a full app ecosystem initially.

However, the attack reveals fatal flaws. First, the target demographic (teenagers on social networks) in 2010 increasingly demands third-party applications (games, emerging social platforms) which rivals already provide; offering "no application store" creates an immediate functional deficit that a messaging feature cannot offset. Second, the pricing strategy is misaligned: a mandatory data plan costing as much as a full smartphone plan removes the primary advantage for price-sensitive teens, especially when competitors offer tiered or family plans. Third, building a new in-house operating system instead of using the company's existing mobile platform fragments engineering resources and delays critical updates, increasing the risk of obsolescence before scale is reached. The "sunk cost" of two years development is a trap; continuing to invest in a product with high price, low utility (no apps), and fragmented software strategy throws good money after bad.

The "do nothing" or "revise" alternative dominates: port the messaging features to the existing platform, negotiate youth-specific data tiers, and launch with at least a basic curated store. The current proposal fails the "fit" test against market reality (app necessity) and economic constraints (teen budget). The pre-mortem is clear: the phones launch, teens reject the high cost and lack of apps, the carrier relationship sours due to poor activation, and the brand equity of the acquired maker is diluted by a failed hardware push.

There is no path to profitability or adoption with the current constraints. The lack of an app store alone is a disqualifier for a "social network" phone in this era.

DECISION: NO-GO
