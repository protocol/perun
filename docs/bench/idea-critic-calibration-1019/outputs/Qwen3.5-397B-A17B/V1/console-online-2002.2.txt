The claim asserts that a mandatory, paid, console-wide online service will succeed despite low broadband adoption, a market expectation of free play, and the company's lack of operational experience in running such a service. The steelman is that a unified, high-quality experience justifies a premium price and creates a defensible moat against fragmented, free competitors.

However, the "better-way" analysis reveals a fatal flaw in the current proposal's rigidity. The alternative of "do nothing" or a voluntary, free tier dominates the proposed mandatory paid model on the dimension of user acquisition. With few homes having broadband, adding a paywall creates a double barrier (hardware + subscription) that likely shrinks the addressable market to near zero before network effects can begin. Competitors offering free or publisher-managed services lower the friction for early adopters, which is critical when the infrastructure (broadband) is still nascent.

The pre-mortem indicates the most probable failure mode is not technical incompetence, but market rejection due to pricing and mandatory access. If the service launches as mandatory and paid, third-party publishers may refuse to support it to avoid alienating their own user bases, leaving the platform with no games to justify the subscription. The company's lack of hosting experience compounds this, suggesting high initial costs that the low subscription volume cannot cover.

The proposal fails the "fit" test against the current market reality of 2002. While the vision of a unified service is sound for the future, the execution strategy of making it mandatory and paid immediately ignores the "Chesterton's fence" of why competitors are keeping it free or decentralized: the market isn't ready to pay for infrastructure that is itself not yet ubiquitous. A staged approach (free beta, optional premium) would mitigate the risk of total adoption failure. As proposed, the plan bets the company's reputation on an unproven willingness to pay in a hardware-constrained environment.

The strongest attack survived is the potential for high-margin recurring revenue; however, without a user base, there is no margin. The risk of launching a "ghost town" service that charges an entry fee is catastrophic.

DECISION: NO-GO
