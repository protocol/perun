The claim is that a B2B component manufacturer can successfully drive demand by branding directly to end consumers, forcing retailers and OEMs to comply. The steelman: if end-users demand the component by name, computer makers lose pricing power and must adopt the brand to sell units, creating a defensible moat against clones.

Alternatives include maintaining the status quo (competing on specs alone), deepening OEM partnerships without consumer spend, or a lower-cost trade-marketing program targeting buyers rather than the general public. However, "do nothing" cedes ground to clones using similar part numbers. The cost of delay is high: once clones establish equivalence in the public mind, differentiation becomes impossible.

The skeptic notes the unprecedented nature of consumer advertising for an invisible part and the risk of channel conflict. Yet, the lack of precedent is the opportunity; if it were common, the moat would already exist. The risk of OEM resentment is real but mitigated by the subsidy: they pay less for ads that convert better. The "invisible" nature of the chip is exactly why the campaign is needed; if buyers knew what they had, the clones would already be losing.

Pre-mortem: The campaign fails if the logo remains meaningless to consumers or if OEMs collectively boycott. The kill criterion is clear: if, after a defined pilot period, consumer awareness does not reach a specific threshold or OEM adoption drops below a critical mass, the program stops. The leading metric is "brand pull-through"—the percentage of retail inquiries specifically requesting the branded chip.

The strongest attack is that this burns cash on unproven consumer psychology. This fails because the alternative—losing the market to indistinguishable clones—is a slow death. The proposed mechanism (subsidizing OEM ads) aligns incentives rather than fighting them. The risk is contained by making the subsidy contingent on logo placement, ensuring every dollar spent builds brand equity. The potential reward (owning the category in the consumer mind) vastly outweighs the budget risk.

DECISION: GO
