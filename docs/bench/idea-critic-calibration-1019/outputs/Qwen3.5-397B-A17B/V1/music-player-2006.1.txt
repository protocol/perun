The claim is that a late entrant can displace a dominant music ecosystem by abandoning copy-protection and leveraging hardware differentiation. The steelman: removing DRM and enabling wireless sharing creates a superior user experience that outweighs first-mover advantage, provided labels consent and marketing cuts through the noise.

However, the proposal contains a fatal internal contradiction. It explicitly states the company is "late to market" against a rival with an "enormous share and a loyal ecosystem," yet simultaneously proposes to "drop the copy-protection standard" that the company previously promoted to partners. This alienates the very record labels required to stock the new store. If the company championed DRM to secure content deals, abruptly reversing stance without a guaranteed alternative distribution channel suggests a strategic disconnect that will result in an empty store. A music player without major label content is a hardware shell, regardless of screen size or wireless features.

The "better way" analysis fails to identify a path where labels accept this reversal given the stated risks. The "do nothing" option preserves existing partner relationships. The proposed path risks total inventory starvation. The pre-mortem is clear: the device launches, marketing spends the budget, but users find no music to buy because labels, feeling betrayed by the DRM pivot and fearing unchecked sharing, withhold catalogs. The cost of delay is irrelevant if the product cannot function due to lack of content.

The strongest attack is the label relationship breach. The proposal admits labels "may restrict sharing," yet the core feature relies on it. Without verified, signed agreements from major rights holders *before* launch, the business model collapses. The proposal offers no evidence these agreements exist; it only lists the risk. Proceeding without resolved content licensing is not a risk; it is a guarantee of failure.

DECISION: NO-GO
