**Steelman:** Leverage existing compact mechanics to rapidly deliver a stylish, customizable coupe/convertible at a low entry price, targeting young buyers with aggressive marketing to create a new segment.

**Skeptic:** The primary risk is brand dilution; sharing "cheap" parts may repel buyers seeking status. However, the "long hood, short deck" styling directly counters the "economy" aesthetic, signaling performance over thrift. The unproven demand is mitigated by the low base price, which limits consumer downside, and the extensive options list, which allows high-margin upselling if volume materializes.

**Better-Way:** Alternatives include launching a wholly new platform (too slow/costly for 1964 constraints) or marketing the existing compact as sporty (insufficient differentiation). Doing nothing cedes the emerging youth demographic to competitors. The proposed path offers the optimal balance of speed, cost, and market responsiveness.

**Fit & Pre-mortem:** This aligns with capturing the postwar boom generation. If it fails, it will likely be due to execution errors in styling perception or ad spend inefficiency, not the core concept. The shared mechanics provide a safety net: if sporty sales lag, the vehicle remains a functional compact car, limiting inventory stranded risk. The heavy ad spend is a one-way door cost but necessary to define the category.

**Kill Criteria:** The proposal lacks specific numeric thresholds for success. To proceed safely, we must define a "stop-loss" metric: if initial sales do not reach 50% of the economy model's volume within six months, marketing spend must be capped immediately. The leading metric is "options take-rate," which determines profitability.

**Verdict:** The risks are real but manageable through design cues and financial guardrails. The opportunity to define a new market segment outweighs the danger of cannibalization, provided the styling successfully masks the economy underpinnings. The low entry price creates a high floor for adoption.

DECISION: GO
