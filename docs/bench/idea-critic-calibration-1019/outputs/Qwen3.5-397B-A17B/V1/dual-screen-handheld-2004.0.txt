The claim is that a dual-screen, touch-enabled handheld at a lower price point will successfully broaden the audience and neutralize a rival's graphical superiority. The steelman is that unique interaction models create new markets where raw power is irrelevant, provided the price advantage is significant and the legacy library remains accessible.

However, critical flaws exist in the current evidence. First, the "lower price" assertion contradicts the admission that "two screens add cost." Without a verified bill of materials showing how the lower price is achieved despite added hardware complexity, the financial premise is unsupported. Second, the risk that "unusual hardware may confuse developers" is treated as a manageable risk rather than a potential fatal blocker; if the installed base fractures and developers refuse to support the new form factor, the software ecosystem collapses regardless of price. Third, the proposal assumes "touch-screen games are unproven" is a risk to be overcome, but offers no concrete plan or existing titles to prove the value proposition to consumers immediately.

The "do nothing" alternative (competing on core gaming strength) is safer than betting the brand on an unproven "gimmick" that increases manufacturing complexity while lowering margins. The proposal fails to demonstrate how the added cost of two screens is offset to achieve the required lower price, nor does it provide a kill criterion for when developer adoption stalls. A strategy relying on creating a new market segment requires proof of consumer demand for touch interfaces, which is explicitly admitted as "unproven."

Because the financial math (higher cost vs. lower price) is unexplained and the primary risk (developer confusion) lacks a mitigation strategy beyond hope, the claim cannot survive scrutiny. The proposal asks for approval based on assumptions that directly contradict the stated constraints.

DECISION: NO-GO
