The proposal to launch a head-worn display in 2013 as a mass-market consumer product at $1,500 is fundamentally flawed due to premature market timing and unresolved value propositions.

The identified risks are not merely hurdles; they are existential blockers for the proposed strategy. First, the price point of $1,500 places the device in luxury territory, yet the proposal admits there is "no clear everyday use" for the majority of people. High-end early adopters pay premiums for distinct utility or status, but a device lacking a defined daily function fails both criteria. Selling a solution in search of a problem is a proven path to inventory obsolescence.

Second, the privacy objections and social acceptance issues are critical. A wearable camera introduced without a established social contract or robust privacy safeguards invites immediate public backlash and potential regulatory scrutiny. In 2013, the cultural conversation around surveillance was already intensifying; launching a ubiquitous camera without a mature trust framework is reckless. Social friction—being banned from venues or stared at in public—will severely limit usage, undermining the "everyday product" positioning.

Third, the technical limitations, specifically short battery life, directly contradict the promise of an all-day wearable. A device that cannot last a standard day is a novelty, not a utility.

The strategy of moving from an invitation-only program to the general public assumes the initial phase will resolve these core deficits. However, if the fundamental use case is unclear and the form factor is socially contentious, early adoption will likely validate these fears rather than overcome them. The proposal attempts to skip the necessary phase of finding a specific, high-value niche (e.g., industrial, medical, or specialized enterprise) where the price and limitations are acceptable trade-offs for unique capabilities.

Positioning this as an everyday consumer product in 2013 ignores the severity of the identified risks. The market is not ready, the technology is not polished, and the value proposition is not defined. Proceeding as proposed would likely result in significant financial loss and reputational damage. The project requires a pivot to a vertical-specific strategy with a clearer problem-solution fit before any consumer launch.

DECISION: NO-GO
