This proposal represents a classic "late entrant" paradox: entering a mature market dominated by a loyal incumbent requires not just parity, but a fundamental shift in value perception. The proposal attempts this through hardware differentiation (larger screen, wireless sharing) and ecosystem integration (store + subscription). However, the strategic contradictions are fatal in the current form.

First, abandoning the copy-protection standard you previously championed alienates existing partners (labels and device makers) without a guaranteed consumer payoff. In 2006, label trust is the primary bottleneck for digital music; burning that bridge to launch a store is suicidal unless the new model offers undeniable, superior security or revenue share, which is not detailed here.

Second, "wireless song sharing" is a regulatory landmine. If labels restrict this (as noted in the risks), the feature becomes a marketing liability rather than an asset. If they allow it, it cannibalizes store sales. The proposal treats this as a technical feature rather than a licensing negotiation outcome that hasn't happened yet.

Third, being late to market with no price advantage means you are asking users to switch ecosystems solely for a "larger screen" and wireless capabilities. In 2006, storage capacity and battery life are the true currencies of portable music, not screen real estate. The dominant player already owns the "it just works" narrative; a complex new client and unproven sharing model introduce friction, not ease.

The marketing budget cannot fix a value proposition that relies on features the content owners may block and hardware specs that don't address the core user pain points (storage, battery, price). The proposal asks for a full-stack launch (hardware, software, store, subscription) while simultaneously dismantling the diplomatic capital needed to stock the store.

To proceed, the company must secure ironclad label agreements on sharing *before* announcing the feature, pivot the hardware narrative to storage/battery dominance, and offer a disruptive pricing model or exclusive content to justify the switch. As it stands, this is a high-cost entry into a low-margin war with alienated partners.

DECISION: NO-GO
