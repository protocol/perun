This proposal fails on three critical dimensions: community trust, engineering safety, and competitive timing.

First, the community is the asset. Their loyalty stems from specific voting mechanics they value. Changing how stories surface to favor publishers directly antagonizes the core user base that drives traffic. In 2010, social news communities are fragile; alienating power users invites immediate migration to the growing rival. You cannot rebuild a loyal community from scratch; you must nurture the existing one.

Second, the "big bang" rewrite is an unacceptable engineering risk. Rewriting from scratch discards years of battle-tested logic, bug fixes, and edge-case handling. Moving all users at once eliminates the ability to test changes in production with real traffic. If the new system fails or the community rejects it, there is no rollback path. The business becomes binary: total success or total collapse. This is gambling, not strategy.

Third, the competitive landscape demands speed and precision, not a monolithic bet. While resources are tied up in a high-risk rewrite, the rival gains ground. A better approach would be iterative: test new surfacing algorithms on a subset of traffic, gather data, and refine. Favoring publishers should be validated as a hypothesis, not baked into a full rewrite before launch.

The proposal solves the wrong problem. Slowing traffic is likely a symptom of stagnation or external shifts, not necessarily the UI. Favoring publishers might boost short-term content volume but could destroy the user engagement loop that makes the site viable.

To approve this would be to ignore the primary risk: that the new version launches to a hollowed-out community and a broken backend, with no way back. The company needs evolution, not revolution. It needs data, not guesses. It needs a safety net, not a tightrope walk without one.

DECISION: NO-GO
