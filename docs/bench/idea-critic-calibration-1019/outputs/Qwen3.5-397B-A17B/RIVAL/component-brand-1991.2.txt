This proposal represents a fundamental shift from a pure B2B model to a B2B2C "pull" strategy. The core insight is correct: in a market where components are invisible and commoditized by clone makers, the only defense is brand equity at the consumer level. By making the end-user demand the component by name, the chip maker forces computer manufacturers to prioritize their product over cheaper alternatives.

The risks are substantial but manageable. Consumer advertising for a component is indeed unprecedented, which is precisely why it offers a first-mover advantage. If successful, it creates a moat that competitors cannot easily cross without massive expenditure. The concern regarding computer maker resentment is valid; however, the subsidy model mitigates this by offering free marketing dollars. Manufacturers are unlikely to reject financial support that also enhances their own product's perceived value. The key is framing the program as a partnership that drives sales for both parties, not as an attempt to control their branding.

The budget allocation risk is the most critical. Diverting a large share of the marketing budget is a high-stakes gamble. However, the cost of inaction—allowing clone makers to erode market share through price competition while remaining invisible to the end user—is likely higher in the long term. Without brand differentiation, the product becomes a mere commodity, subject to relentless price wars.

The proposal addresses the root cause of the vulnerability: obscurity. By attaching a recognizable logo to the consumer experience, the chip maker transforms an invisible specification into a desirable feature. This aligns the incentives of the computer makers (who get subsidized ads and a selling point) with the chip maker (who gets brand recognition and pricing power).

While execution will require careful negotiation with partners to ensure the logo is displayed prominently and the messaging is consistent, the strategic logic is sound. The potential to own the consumer mindshare in a nascent market outweighs the risks of trying a novel approach. The unprecedented nature of the campaign is its greatest asset, not its liability.

DECISION: GO
