The proposal to launch a clear cola nationwide in 1992 carries significant strategic risk despite positive test market data. The core assumption—that "purity" signaled by clarity translates to sustained cola preference—is unproven at scale. Cola consumption is deeply tied to sensory expectations, where color acts as a flavor anchor; removing it risks cognitive dissonance that marketing alone cannot override.

Test markets often inflate demand through novelty effects. Early adopters intrigued by the visual gimmick may not convert to repeat buyers once the curiosity fades. Without evidence of long-term retention beyond the initial trial, a national rollout invites costly failure. The soft-drink category's slowing growth amplifies this danger: missteps are less forgiving when the overall pie isn't expanding.

Cannibalization poses another critical threat. If the clear variant draws sales from the flagship brand without expanding total volume, the net effect is revenue erosion disguised as innovation. The proposal lacks safeguards to measure or mitigate this shift. Additionally, rivals can quickly replicate the concept, turning a first-mover advantage into a commodity race where differentiation evaporates.

The marketing campaign's scale compounds these risks. A "large" spend implies high fixed costs that demand immediate, sustained uptake to justify. If repeat rates lag, the brand faces margin pressure and potential reputational damage from a high-profile flop. Historical precedents show that clear beverages often struggle to maintain relevance after the initial buzz.

Before approving, the executive team needs: (1) longitudinal data on repeat purchase rates in test markets, (2) a cannibalization model quantifying flagship impact, and (3) a phased rollout plan with clear kill criteria. Launching nationwide without these validates speculation over evidence. The opportunity cost of failure—diverting resources from core brand defense during a growth slowdown—is too high to ignore.

DECISION: NO-GO
