The proposal addresses critical operational failures: the inability to deliver updates and combat cheating in popular PC titles. Relying on physical boxes in 2003 creates a static product vulnerable to exploitation and obsolescence. A mandatory online client directly solves these issues by enabling real-time patching and server-side verification.

While risks are significant, they are manageable or temporary. Player friction regarding accounts and downloads is a short-term hurdle that diminishes as value (seamless updates, fair play) becomes evident. Retail partner backlash is a valid concern, yet the company's leverage lies in its "very popular" games; retailers need these titles more than the developer needs specific shelf space. If necessary, a hybrid model can transition over time.

Early bugs are inevitable in software launches but are preferable to the current state of unchecked cheating and stagnant versions. The lack of universal broadband in 2003 is a genuine constraint, but adoption is rising rapidly. The client can be designed with low-bandwidth modes or optional offline components for single-player segments while enforcing online checks for multiplayer. Competitor copying is actually a validation of the strategy's potential; being first allows the company to define the standard and capture the user base before others react.

The long-term vision of opening the client as a store for other developers transforms a cost center into a massive revenue stream, creating an ecosystem lock-in similar to emerging digital platforms. Waiting for perfect conditions or universal connectivity means ceding ground to pirates and cheaters now. The strategic advantage of controlling the distribution channel and user experience outweighs the transitional friction. The company must lead this shift rather than react to it later when competitors inevitably attempt the same.
