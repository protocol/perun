The proposal addresses a critical vulnerability: the end-user's inability to distinguish the product from generic clones, despite the chip being the computer's core value driver. In 1991, the market dynamics favor shifting power from the computer manufacturer to the component supplier. By making the invisible visible, the company transforms a commodity into a preferred brand.

The primary risk is the unprecedented nature of advertising a component directly to consumers. However, "unprecedented" often signals a first-mover advantage rather than a fatal flaw. If successful, this strategy creates a pull effect where consumers demand the specific chip, forcing computer makers to comply or lose sales. This neutralizes the threat of clones using similar part numbers, as brand recognition supersedes alphanumeric confusion.

Regarding the financial concern, dedicating a large share of the marketing budget is aggressive but necessary for a paradigm shift. The co-advertising model is strategically sound; it leverages the existing media spend of computer makers rather than building a standalone media empire from scratch. While computer makers may initially resent the influence over their advertising, the promise of increased sales volume through consumer demand will likely outweigh their discomfort. Those who refuse the program risk being perceived as selling inferior, unbranded goods.

The fear that benefits may not show in sales is valid for any major campaign, but the status quo guarantees continued erosion by clones. Doing nothing ensures the product remains a hidden feature, easily swapped for cheaper alternatives. This campaign creates an emotional connection and a quality guarantee in the buyer's mind, creating a moat that technical specifications alone cannot build. The potential to own the consumer mindset in the burgeoning personal computer era justifies the financial exposure and operational friction. The timing is ideal before the market becomes entirely saturated with indistinguishable options.

DECISION: GO
