The proposal introduces a fundamental shift from transactional shipping fees to a recurring revenue model, directly addressing the primary friction point for repeat customers. While the identified risks are substantial, they represent the necessary cost of disrupting market expectations rather than reasons to halt. The concern that heavy buyers will increase order frequency is not a flaw but the intended mechanism for driving volume and customer lifetime value. If shipping costs exceed fee revenue for these users, the loss is offset by the sheer increase in merchandise sales and the locking out of competitors.

The short-term margin hit is an explicit investment in market share and behavioral change. In 2005, e-commerce was still establishing trust and convenience as primary drivers; absorbing this cost signals a commitment to customer experience that competitors matching per-order fees cannot easily replicate. The difficulty in reversing the offer once memberships are sold is a feature, not a bug. It creates a psychological and financial commitment from the customer, ensuring high retention rates and creating a formidable barrier to exit.

Regarding unproven economics, waiting for perfect data in a nascent market guarantees falling behind. The $79 price point creates an immediate cash influx and filters for the most valuable demographic. The potential for exponential growth in order frequency outweighs the linear risk of shipping overages. Hesitation based on unproven models allows rivals to define the standard. The strategic advantage lies in being the first to remove the shipping friction entirely, thereby owning the customer relationship. The risks are manageable through operational scaling and dynamic eligibility adjustments post-launch, but the window to lead is narrow. Approval must be immediate to capitalize on first-mover advantage.

DECISION: GO
