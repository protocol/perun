**Steelman claim**  
Launch a new digital music player, accompanying music store and subscription service in 2006, featuring wireless song‑sharing, a larger screen, a new desktop client, and a heavy marketing push.

**Skeptic attack**  
1. **Ecosystem lock‑in** – The dominant player already controls hardware, software, and DRM (FairPlay). Switching to a different copy‑protection breaks existing label agreements and will likely be refused.  
2. **Late entry** – By 2006 the market is already saturated; iPod sales exceed 100 M units and iTunes holds >70 % of digital sales. Gaining traction now demands >$200 M marketing with no proven ROI.  
3. **Label constraints** – Labels historically condition DRM on the dominant platform; they may block the proposed “open” sharing, nullifying the key differentiator.  
4. **No price advantage** – Without a cost lead, consumers have little incentive to switch from a mature, trusted ecosystem.

**Better‑way alternatives**  
- License the dominant DRM and integrate the service into the existing player, reducing development and marketing costs.  
- Target a niche (e.g., high‑resolution audio) where the incumbent has weak offering, using a lean “software‑only” client rather than new hardware.  
- Acquire a smaller existing player to piggy‑back on its user base and distribution deals.

**Kill‑criteria (must be met to proceed)**  
- Secure DRM licensing and label sharing rights within 6 months.  
- Marketing spend capped at $150 M with a projected break‑even within 2 years.  
- Achieve ≥5 % market share in the first 24 months; otherwise abort.

**Pre‑mortem** – If DRM licensing fails, the product cannot launch; if labels block sharing, the core feature disappears; if marketing fails to shift consumer habits, the spend is sunk.

Given the high probability that the kill‑criteria will not be satisfied and the existence of lower‑cost, lower‑risk alternatives, the proposal should not be approved as written.

DECISION: NO-GO
