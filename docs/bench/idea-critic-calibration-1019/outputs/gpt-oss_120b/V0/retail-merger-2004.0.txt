**Steelman claim:** Acquiring the larger, older department‑store chain and merging corporate functions will create a combined retail platform that leverages the discount‑store’s cost discipline, revitalizes under‑invested locations, and generates brand‑mix revenue, offsetting the decline of both businesses.

**Skeptic attacks**  
1. **Dual decline:** Both entities are losing sales; combining them does not guarantee a reversal and may compound the downward trend.  
2. **Store condition:** The target’s stores are old and under‑maintained, requiring capital infusion that erodes any cost‑saving envelope.  
3. **Customer mismatch:** Discount shoppers and department‑store shoppers have distinct price‑sensitivity and product expectations; cross‑selling brands may dilute the discount value proposition.  
4. **Synergy uncertainty:** Merging corporate functions often yields 2‑5 % cost reductions after 2 years, yet integration costs (systems, layoffs, re‑branding) can exceed that amount.  

**Better‑way alternatives**  
- **Organic expansion:** Invest a modest capex in remodeling a subset of discount stores to test the mixed‑format concept before a full acquisition.  
- **Strategic partnership:** Negotiate a joint‑venture for brand‑sharing without taking on the legacy real‑estate and debt burden.  

**Kill‑criteria (pre‑mortem)**  
- If combined same‑store sales do not improve ≥5 % within 12 months, abort further integration.  
- If realized cost synergies fall below 3 % of combined revenue after 18 months, unwind the merger.  

**Remaining risk:** Execution complexity and cultural clash could delay cost savings, while big‑box rivals may capture market share during the transition.

Given the high uncertainty of turning around two declining businesses, the modest upside versus the sizable integration and refurbishment costs, the proposal does not meet a clear, quantifiable upside threshold.

DECISION: NO-GO
