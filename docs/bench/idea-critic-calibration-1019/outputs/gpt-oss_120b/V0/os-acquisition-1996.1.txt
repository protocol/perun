**Steelman**  
Buy the small software firm that created a modern, object‑oriented OS, paying a few hundred million dollars, then use that OS as the foundation for a new system to replace the company’s dated platform. The founder would return as an advisor to guide integration and future development.

**Assumptions**  
1. The modern OS can be ported to the existing hardware line with acceptable engineering effort.  
2. The acquisition price will be recouped through regained market share and higher‑margin products.  
3. The founder’s advisory role will add strategic value without reigniting past conflicts.  
4. Existing third‑party applications can be migrated or re‑engineered economically.

**Better‑Way Alternatives**  
- **License an established OS** (e.g., from a major vendor) – lower upfront cost, proven ecosystem, faster time‑to‑market.  
- **Incremental in‑house modernization** – leverage current code base, retain control, spread R&D spend over several years.  
- **Strategic partnership** – co‑develop a platform while sharing risk and cost.

**Kill‑Criteria (Pre‑mortem)**  
- If projected migration cost exceeds 30 % of the acquisition price, abort.  
- If the founder’s involvement triggers internal turnover or delays > 6 months, abort.  
- If the acquired OS cannot support at least 80 % of the existing software portfolio within 12 months, abort.  
- If licensing an external OS would achieve comparable market‑share gain at ≤ 50 % of the acquisition cost, abort.

**Risk Assessment**  
The target has sold few units, indicating limited market validation. Migration of legacy applications is historically costly and can erode customer confidence. The founder’s “difficult history” raises a non‑technical risk that could destabilize the integration team. Paying a “few hundred million” for a company with minimal revenue creates a poor price‑to‑revenue ratio, especially when a licensing route offers a ready‑made ecosystem and lower capital outlay.

**Recommendation**  
Given the high financial exposure, uncertain technical payoff, and viable lower‑risk alternatives, the proposal does not meet the kill‑criteria thresholds for a prudent investment.

DECISION: NO-GO
