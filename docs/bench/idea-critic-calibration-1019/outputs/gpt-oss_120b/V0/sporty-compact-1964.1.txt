**Steelman** – The plan is to turn a reliable, low‑cost compact car into a sporty two‑door coupe/convertible, keeping the mechanical platform, pricing it low, offering many options, and supporting the launch with a massive advertising push. The aim is to capture the emerging post‑war baby‑boom youth market that wants style without a premium price.

**Skeptic attack**  
1. **Unproven demand** – In 1964 the U.S. market has shown strong growth for full‑size and mid‑size cars, but volume sales of small sporty cars are still nascent (e.g., the Chevrolet Corvair and Ford Falcon had modest sporty variants). Betting on high volume without market data risks large unsold inventory.  
2. **Perceived cheapness** – Sharing the drivetrain and interior of a known economy model may make the new coupe look like a dressed‑up cheap car, eroding the “sporty” image and possibly harming the parent brand’s reputation for quality.  
3. **Financial exposure** – A “very large” launch campaign consumes capital before any revenue. If the model underperforms, the company faces a double hit: sunk advertising spend and excess production capacity.

**Better‑way alternatives**  
- **Limited‑run pilot**: Produce a small batch (e.g., 5 % of projected volume) and test market response before scaling advertising.  
- **Facelift of existing model**: Add sporty trim, a longer hood and shorter deck to the current compact without a new nameplate, reducing tooling costs and preserving brand equity.  
- **Partner with an established sport‑car division**: Co‑brand the vehicle, leveraging existing sporty credibility while sharing development risk.

**Kill‑criteria (pre‑mortem)**  
- If pre‑launch dealer orders < 30 % of the 12‑month sales target, halt full‑scale production.  
- If advertising spend exceeds 8 % of projected first‑year revenue, pause the campaign and re‑budget.  
- If brand perception surveys (post‑launch, 3 months) show a ≥ 10 % drop in “quality” scores among core customers, discontinue the model.

**Conclusion** – The proposal’s upside is attractive, but the combination of market uncertainty, brand‑image risk, and heavy upfront spend makes it premature to green‑light at full scale. A phased approach or a less costly variant should be pursued first.

DECISION: NO-GO
