**Steelman:** The developer will launch a mandatory online client for all new PC titles, handling updates, anti‑cheat, purchases, and later serving as a storefront for third‑party games, aiming to streamline post‑sale support and open a new revenue channel.

**Attack:**  
- **User friction:** In 2003, broadband penetration was ≈30 % in many markets; mandatory large downloads and required accounts would alienate the majority of players, driving them to competitors or piracy.  
- **Retail fallout:** Retailers selling boxed copies would see their product’s value proposition eroded, likely demanding removal of titles that force online activation, cutting a critical sales channel.  
- **Technical risk:** A brand‑new client rolled out at scale would inevitably ship bugs; fixing them would require a support infrastructure the studio likely lacks, increasing operational cost and damaging reputation.  
- **Competitive exposure:** By exposing the client publicly, rivals could replicate the anti‑cheat/update model without the developer’s brand lock‑in, nullifying the intended moat.

**Better‑ways / Alternatives:**  
1. Keep boxed distribution and add an **optional** client for updates and anti‑cheat, encouraging adoption through incentives.  
2. Pilot the client with a single new title, gathering telemetry before mandating it.  
3. Partner with an emerging digital platform (e.g., early Steam) to offload distribution risk.

**Fit & Kill‑Criteria:** The proposal misaligns with the current market reality (low broadband, strong retail dependence). A clear go‑no‑go trigger should be a ≤‑10 % sales decline or ≥‑15 % retailer cancellations within six months; none are guaranteed.

**Conclusion:** The risks outweigh the strategic benefits at this time; the plan should be revised to an optional, phased rollout.

DECISION: NO-GO
