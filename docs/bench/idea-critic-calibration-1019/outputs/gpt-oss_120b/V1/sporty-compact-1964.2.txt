**Steelman claim**  
Launch a low‑price, sport‑styled two‑door coupe/convertible built on the existing compact‑car platform, stocked with a long options list and supported by a large advertising push, to capture the post‑war baby‑boom youth market and generate profitable volume.

**Assumptions challenged**  
1. *Market demand*: The segment is unproven at volume; success of the 1964 Mustang shows appetite but it used a dedicated platform, not an economy under‑carriage.  
2. *Brand perception*: Sharing cheap‑car mechanicals may make the new model appear inexpensive rather than sporty.  
3. *Financial risk*: A “very large” ad spend must be recouped before sales data exist.

**Better‑way alternatives**  
- Introduce a sport‑trim of the existing compact with distinctive body panels, avoiding a full new model cost.  
- Run a limited‑run pilot in a few regions to validate demand before committing national advertising spend.  
- Partner with an external coachbuilder for the convertible body to lower tooling risk.

**Kill‑criteria (trip‑wires)**  
- If pre‑orders in the first 30 days are < 10 % of the 1‑year sales target, cancel the program.  
- If per‑unit cost exceeds the projected margin by > 15 %, halt production.  
- If brand‑perception surveys show a ≥ 5 point drop in “sportiness” rating after launch, suspend advertising.

**Fit with strategy**  
Capturing the emerging youth segment aligns with the company’s growth north‑star. The plan leverages existing parts to keep unit cost low, satisfying the cost‑control pillar, while the design brief (long hood, short deck) directly addresses the styling desire.

**Pre‑mortem**  
Failure would likely stem from cheap‑car image bleed‑through or overspending on ads. The proposed kill‑criteria and pilot test mitigate these risks.

**Recommendation**  
Approve the launch, but condition it on meeting the pre‑order threshold and maintaining the cost‑margin guardrails.  

DECISION: GO
