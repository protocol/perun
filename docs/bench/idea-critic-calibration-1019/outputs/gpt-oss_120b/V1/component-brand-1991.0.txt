**Steelman** – Launch a consumer‑facing brand campaign with a distinctive logo and a co‑op advertising program that reimburses computer makers for a share of the cost when their ads display the logo, thereby prompting end‑users to ask for the chip by name.

**Attacks & Alternatives**  
1. **Do nothing** – Keep the status‑quo of silent OEM sales. Low cost, but brand remains invisible to consumers and cannot command premium pricing.  
2. **Limited education** – Run trade‑show and dealer‑portal outreach only. Cheaper, but lacks mass‑market consumer impact; historic “Intel Inside” success shows broader consumer ads matter.  
3. **Joint branding** – Partner with a few flagship computer makers for a co‑branded line, sharing costs only on those models. Reduces risk of maker resentment but limits reach.

**Fit** – The proposal seeks to move the chip from a hidden OEM component to a recognized consumer attribute, directly supporting the strategic goal of brand equity growth and higher‑margin sales.

**Premortem** – Failure modes: (a) consumers ignore a component name, wasting a large portion of the marketing budget; (b) computer makers feel coerced, withdraw participation, and possibly favor rival chips; (c) measurable lift in sales does not materialise, leaving the program financially untenable.

**Kill‑criteria (one‑way door)** –  
- If after six months brand‑recall surveys show < 5 % unaided awareness of the logo among target buyers, terminate the program.  
- If participation by computer makers falls below 50 % of the targeted partner list, suspend reimbursements.  

Both criteria are quantifiable, time‑boxed, and trigger a stop‑action.

**Evidence** – Intel’s 1991 “Intel Inside” campaign, which used a similar consumer logo and co‑op advertising model, achieved a measurable increase in brand recall (≈ 30 %) and enabled price premiums. The precedent demonstrates that consumer‑level component branding can succeed when kill‑criteria are enforced.

**Conclusion** – The plan mirrors a proven market‑shaping strategy, includes clear, measurable stop‑conditions, and offers a higher‑return alternative to doing nothing. Risks are mitigated by the defined kill‑criteria and the ability to scale back if maker participation wanes.

DECISION: GO
