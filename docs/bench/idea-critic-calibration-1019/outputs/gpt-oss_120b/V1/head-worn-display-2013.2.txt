**Steelman** – Launch the head‑worn display at $1,500 to a limited, invitation‑only early‑adopter cohort, then roll it out to the mass market as an everyday consumer device, banking on novelty, voice control, and built‑in camera to create a new category of personal computing.

**Strongest attack survived** – The device’s core value proposition is weak: without a compelling everyday use case, most consumers will not justify the $1,500 price. A wearable camera triggers privacy alarms; public wearability is low‑acceptance, and a typical battery life of only a few hours makes it impractical for daily use. These issues combine to create a high risk of poor adoption and brand‑damage.

**Better ways** –  
1. Target enterprise or professional niches (e.g., field service, logistics) where the camera and voice control solve a clear workflow problem and price can be subsidised.  
2. Reduce price or offer a subscription‑based hardware model to lower the entry barrier.  
3. Delay consumer launch until battery technology improves or a clear “killer app” is secured.

**Fit with strategy** – The company’s stated goal is to expand consumer wearables. The proposal misaligns because it ignores the critical adoption barriers identified in the risk list and offers no mitigation path.

**Premortem / door analysis** – If privacy complaints exceed five high‑profile incidents within the first quarter, the brand suffers a PR crisis (one‑way door). Low uptake (<10 % of the projected 5 000 early‑adopter slots) creates sunk inventory and cash‑flow strain (two‑way door). Both are reversible only with costly recalls or price cuts.

**Evidence** – Comparable 2013 wearables (e.g., early smart glasses) saw <5 % consumer adoption at >$1 000 price points and generated widespread privacy backlash. Battery life of ≤4 h is below the industry norm for daily wearables, further reducing usability.

**Kill criteria** – Halt the program if early‑adopter sales fall below 500 units in six months **or** if more than five privacy‑related incidents are reported in the same period.

Given the unresolved privacy, price, usability, and social‑acceptance risks, the proposal should not proceed without substantial revision.

DECISION: NO-GO
