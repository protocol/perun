The firm’s legacy OS is a clear liability: it is outdated, internal replacement attempts have failed twice, and market share is eroding. A decisive move is needed, but the proposed acquisition carries several material risks that outweigh its potential upside.

1. **Limited market traction** – The target’s OS has sold only a handful of units, providing little proof of scalability or ecosystem robustness. Scaling it to the firm’s global user base would require extensive validation and likely extensive engineering effort.

2. **Migration complexity** – Moving existing applications and development teams onto a brand‑new core entails rewriting, re‑testing, and retraining. Historical experience shows such transitions can consume years and inflate costs far beyond the purchase price.

3. **Founder‑related risk** – Bringing back a founder with a “difficult history” introduces cultural and governance uncertainty that could destabilize the integration effort and distract senior leadership.

4. **Financial mismatch** – Paying a few hundred million dollars for a company whose revenue is modest creates an unfavorable price‑to‑sales ratio, increasing pressure on the combined entity to deliver rapid returns.

5. **Viable alternative** – Licensing an established third‑party OS would provide a proven platform, lower integration risk, and preserve cash for complementary investments (e.g., UI, services, marketing).

Given the high execution risk, cultural concerns, and the availability of a lower‑risk licensing path, the proposal should not be approved in its current form. A deeper cost‑benefit analysis, including a licensing comparison and a mitigation plan for migration challenges, is required before committing capital.

DECISION: NO-GO
