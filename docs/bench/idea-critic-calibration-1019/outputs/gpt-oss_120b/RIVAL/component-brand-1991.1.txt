The proposal tries to convert a B2B‑only microprocessor vendor into a consumer‑facing brand by subsidizing computer‑maker advertising that features a new logo. The goal is to make end‑users ask for the chip by name, thereby creating demand pull.

**Strengths**
* Directly addresses the core problem: buyers cannot identify the chip, so they cannot request it.
* A co‑marketing program could embed the brand in existing ad spend, reducing the need for a separate consumer‑media budget.

**Weaknesses / Risks**
* In 1991, consumer advertising for a component that is invisible to the end user is unprecedented; there is no proven demand‑generation model.
* The program would consume a large share of the chip maker’s marketing budget, potentially crowding out other critical activities (e.g., channel development, technical support).
* Computer manufacturers may view the requirement to display the logo as intrusive, damaging partner relationships and possibly prompting them to favor competing suppliers who do not impose branding constraints.
* Even if the logo appears in ads, there is no guarantee that end‑users will translate brand awareness into purchase decisions for a hidden component, making ROI highly uncertain.

Given the high financial exposure, the lack of precedent, and the risk of alienating key channel partners, the proposal should be revisited. A smaller‑scale pilot or alternative demand‑creation tactics (e.g., developer outreach, OEM‑level incentives) would provide data before committing to a full‑budget co‑marketing program.

**Recommendation:** Hold the current plan, gather market insights, and redesign the program to mitigate partner‑relationship risk and budget impact.

DECISION: NO-GO
