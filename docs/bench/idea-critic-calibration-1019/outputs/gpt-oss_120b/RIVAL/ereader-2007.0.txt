The proposal tries to leap from a pure‑online bookseller into hardware, a domain where the company has no track record. The only evidence for demand comes from an earlier generation of e‑readers that “sold poorly,” indicating that the market was already skeptical of dedicated devices. Adding a paper‑like screen and a free cellular connection would raise bill‑of‑materials costs dramatically, while the plan calls for “low price per title” e‑books that will compress margins and likely provoke publisher resistance—already a known concern.

Even if the hardware succeeded, the built‑in cellular link creates a recurring cost the company must subsidise, further eroding profitability. The strategy also risks cannibalising the core print business the company already dominates, without a clear plan to offset lost revenue. No mitigation for these risks is described: no partnership with an experienced hardware OEM, no phased rollout, no pricing model that protects margins, and no publisher‑friendly licensing terms.

Given the lack of internal hardware expertise, the historical market failure of similar devices, and the financial pressures from low‑priced e‑books and free connectivity, the proposal is premature. The company would be better served by first testing a software‑only e‑reading app on existing devices, negotiating publisher terms, and gathering real‑world usage data before committing to costly hardware development.

**Recommendation:** Do not approve the hardware program as currently scoped. Re‑evaluate after a low‑risk pilot that validates demand, publisher agreement, and margin impact.
