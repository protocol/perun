The proposal tries to shift a B2B component into a B2C brand, a move that has never been attempted for a microprocessor. Buyers currently do not know which chip powers their machines, and the market is already confused by clone part numbers. Introducing a consumer‑facing logo and subsidising computer‑maker advertising would require a sizeable portion of the chip maker’s marketing budget with no proven return.

Key concerns:

1. **Unproven consumer demand** – There is no evidence that end‑users will seek out a specific chip when they cannot see it inside the computer. Without a visible differentiator, the logo may have little impact on purchase decisions.

2. **Budget impact** – Allocating a large share of the marketing spend to reimburse computer‑maker ads reduces resources for other proven channels (e.g., developer outreach, OEM partnerships). The opportunity cost is high.

3. **Partner resistance** – Computer manufacturers may view the subsidy as interference in their own branding strategy, potentially straining relationships that are critical for volume sales.

4. **Measurement difficulty** – Even if the campaign raises brand awareness, isolating its effect on unit sales will be challenging, making ROI unclear.

5. **Risk of brand dilution** – A consumer logo for a component that is invisible to end users could be ignored or even mocked, harming the chip maker’s reputation.

Given these substantial uncertainties and the potential for negative fallout with key partners, the plan should be refined before any spend is committed. A smaller pilot, clearer metrics, and stronger evidence of consumer interest are needed to justify the large budget allocation.

**Recommendation:** Hold the full launch until the above gaps are addressed.

DECISION: NO-GO
