The proposal targets a market already dominated by a entrenched player whose ecosystem, brand loyalty, and massive user base create a high barrier to entry. Entering late (2006) removes the advantage of first‑mover momentum, while the dominant player’s control over distribution channels means the new store will struggle to acquire catalog and negotiate favorable terms with labels, especially if those labels fear loss of revenue from unrestricted wireless sharing.

Switching away from the copy‑protection standard the company previously championed introduces a critical technical and partnership risk. Existing device makers and partner stores have built their DRM pipelines around that standard; abandoning it could alienate them, erode trust, and increase the chance of legal challenges from rights holders. Labels, already wary of sharing, may impose stricter licensing or refuse to supply content, limiting the catalog and undermining the value proposition.

The product differentiation—larger screen, new desktop client, and wireless sharing—does not address the core competitive advantage of the incumbent: a seamless, well‑curated ecosystem with deep integration across devices. Without a clear price advantage or unique content, the offering risks being perceived as a marginal upgrade rather than a compelling alternative.

Given the combination of market timing, ecosystem lock‑in, DRM reversal, and uncertain label cooperation, the proposal lacks sufficient risk mitigation and a defensible value proposition to justify the large marketing spend at this stage.

**Decision:** The plan should be revised to include concrete strategies for DRM transition, label partnership incentives, and a differentiated pricing or content model before proceeding.

DECISION: NO-GO
