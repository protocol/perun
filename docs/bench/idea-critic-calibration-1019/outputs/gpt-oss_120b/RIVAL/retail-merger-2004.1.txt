The two businesses are both in decline, and the merger does not address the root causes of that decline. Closing the deal would lock in the under‑invested, aging store base of the older chain, adding significant capital‑expenditure burdens for refurbishment or write‑offs. Expected cost savings from merging corporate functions are speculative; the two organizations have different operational histories, IT systems, and supplier contracts, making integration costly and time‑consuming.

Customer overlap is low: the discount‑store chain serves price‑sensitive shoppers, while the department‑store’s clientele expects a broader assortment and higher‑service experience. Re‑branding the older chain’s brands into discount stores risks alienating both groups, diluting brand equity, and confusing the market. Moreover, the big‑box rivals that are gaining share have already demonstrated the ability to offer deeper selections and lower prices at scale—advantages the combined entity will still lack without a clear plan to modernize stores, improve inventory turnover, or invest in e‑commerce.

Potential upside—adding floor space and geographic reach—does not outweigh the integration risk, the need for substantial capital upgrades, and the likelihood that combined sales will continue to fall. A more prudent approach would be to stabilize the discount chain first, invest in store refreshes and omnichannel capabilities, and evaluate targeted acquisitions that complement its core demographic and supply chain.

Given the current information, proceeding with the acquisition is likely to exacerbate financial stress rather than create value.

DECISION: NO-GO
