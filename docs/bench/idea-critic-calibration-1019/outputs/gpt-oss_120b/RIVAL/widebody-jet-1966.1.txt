The proposal promises a dramatic capacity increase that could lower the airline’s cost per seat, but it rests on three high‑impact risks that outweigh the potential upside.

1. **Capital exposure** – Building a new factory and a four‑engine wide‑body platform will require an outlay far larger than any previous program. With no precedent, the company faces a steep cost‑recovery curve and a long cash‑flow burden before any revenue materializes.

2. **Engine uncertainty** – The high‑bypass engine is unproven. Reliance on a single supplier for a novel powerplant introduces technical risk (performance, reliability, certification) and supply‑chain risk (delays, cost overruns). Failure to mature the engine would jeopardize the entire program.

3. **Market dynamics** – The aviation outlook in 1966 anticipates supersonic airliners supplanting subsonic jets. Investing heavily in a larger subsonic platform could leave the product obsolete as airlines shift toward faster, higher‑margin aircraft. Moreover, only one airline has placed an order, concentrating commercial risk in a single customer and providing no market validation.

While the raised cockpit for future freighter conversion adds flexibility, it does not mitigate the core financial and technical exposures. A prudent path would be to defer the large‑scale development, gather additional airline commitments, and either pursue incremental capacity growth on the existing platform or wait for clearer market signals on engine technology and supersonic competition.

Given the scale of investment, the untested engine, and the uncertain market, the proposal should not be approved in its current form.

DECISION: NO-GO
