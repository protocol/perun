The company’s core strength is its massive catalog and retail platform; it has no track record in consumer hardware. Designing an e‑reader that combines a paper‑like display with built‑in cellular connectivity would require substantial investment in industrial design, supply‑chain management, firmware, certification, and post‑sale support—areas where the firm currently has no expertise. The financial outlay and time to market therefore carry a high probability of overruns.

From a market standpoint, 2007 already saw the launch of a competing device that achieved rapid adoption despite modest pricing, indicating that consumers will buy a well‑executed product even if the hardware brand is new. However, early dedicated e‑readers suffered from limited buyer awareness and a perception of niche appeal. Without a strong hardware pedigree, the proposed device risks being dismissed as another “gimmick,” limiting sales and exacerbating the capital risk.

Pricing e‑books at a low, single‑price point could accelerate adoption but would compress margins and may alienate publishers who already fear piracy. The company would need to negotiate new royalty structures, which could increase its cost per title or force it to accept a higher wholesale price, eroding the low‑price advantage. Moreover, an easy‑to‑use, always‑connected device could accelerate the shift from print to digital, threatening the existing high‑margin print business the company has built.

A more prudent path would be to test the market through partnerships with established hardware manufacturers or by offering a software‑only solution (e‑reader apps) that leverages the existing catalog without the hardware risk. This approach would validate demand, allow the company to refine its pricing model, and preserve capital while building credibility with publishers.

Given the high execution risk, unclear margin impact, and potential cannibalization of profitable print sales, the proposal should not be approved in its current form.

DECISION: NO-GO
