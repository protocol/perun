The airline’s request targets a dramatic seat‑capacity increase that would lower cost per passenger if the aircraft reaches the projected demand growth. However, the proposal hinges on three interlocking uncertainties that together outweigh the potential upside.

1. **Capital exposure** – The development program and new factory represent an investment far beyond any previous project. Financing such a program would strain cash flow and likely require debt or equity issuance, diluting shareholder value and increasing financial risk at a time when the company’s balance sheet is optimized for incremental upgrades rather than a megaproject.

2. **Engine maturity** – The high‑bypass turbofan is untested in service. Certification timelines for a new engine can easily double, and any reliability shortfall would cascade into delayed deliveries, warranty costs, and reputational damage. The airline’s cost‑per‑seat advantage depends on the engine’s fuel efficiency; a setback would erode the very benefit that drives the order.

3. **Market timing** – Forecasts in 1966 anticipate supersonic transport (e.g., Concorde) supplanting subsonic jets for premium routes. While the mass‑market segment will still need efficient subsonic aircraft, a single‑airline order provides no hedge against a possible shift in airline fleet strategies toward faster, higher‑value aircraft. Without a broader order book, the program cannot achieve economies of scale, and the risk of a stranded asset rises sharply.

Given these factors, the prudent course is to defer commitment until additional customers are secured, the engine program demonstrates reliable performance, and a more detailed financial risk assessment is completed. Proceeding now would expose the company to unacceptable financial, technical, and market risks.

DECISION: NO-GO
