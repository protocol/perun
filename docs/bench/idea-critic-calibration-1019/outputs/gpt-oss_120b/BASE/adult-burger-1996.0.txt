The chain’s child‑focused identity is a core strength, but it also creates a strong perception barrier for adult diners seeking a “premium” experience. Introducing a higher‑priced burger will clash with the current value‑oriented positioning and with the expectations of the primary customer base—families that visit for kids’ meals and low‑cost options.  

Key concerns:

1. **Brand mismatch** – Adults already associate the brand with quick, inexpensive meals. A premium product requires a shift in perception that typically takes years and sustained, consistent messaging, not a single advertising splash.

2. **Price elasticity** – The proposed price point sits above the chain’s average ticket. Existing patrons, especially price‑sensitive families, may reduce overall spend or switch to competitors rather than upgrade.

3. **Advertising spend risk** – Allocating the industry’s largest budget before any market validation creates a high fixed‑cost exposure. If demand does not materialize, the financial hit will be substantial and could erode margins on the core menu.

4. **Operational complexity** – A premium burger often demands different preparation standards, higher‑quality ingredients, and staff training. This adds operational risk and could affect service speed, undermining the chain’s fast‑food promise.

Mitigation steps that would improve the odds of success—limited‑region pilots, co‑branding with a known premium partner, tiered pricing, or a “grown‑up” sub‑brand—are not part of the current proposal. Without testing demand first, the company risks damaging both its core business and its brand equity.

Given the magnitude of the spend, the uncertain demand, and the brand‑perception challenge, the plan should be re‑examined and a phased, data‑driven approach adopted before committing to full‑scale rollout.

DECISION: NO-GO
