The airline’s request reflects a genuine market signal: passenger growth is accelerating and airlines are seeking lower cost‑per‑seat aircraft. A very large, high‑capacity jet could meet that need and, if successful, would lock in a long‑term customer relationship and generate a platform for future freighter conversions.

However, the proposal carries several decisive liabilities that outweigh the upside in its current form:

* **Capital exposure** – The development programme and the new production line are described as “far larger than any the company has made.” Such a jump in outlay will strain cash flow and likely require financing on terms that could jeopardise the firm’s balance sheet, especially if subsequent orders do not materialise quickly.

* **Engine risk** – The high‑bypass engine is unproven. A four‑engine aircraft depends critically on engine reliability and performance; any delay or shortfall in the engine programme would cascade into the airframe schedule and cost structure.

* **Market uncertainty** – In 1966 the industry is abuzz about supersonic transport (e.g., Concorde, Boeing 2707). While subsonic jets have historically dominated, the perception that supersonic will soon replace them could dampen airline appetite for a massive subsonic platform, making the single‑airline order especially vulnerable.

* **Customer concentration** – Only one airline has placed an order. Without a broader launch customer base, the programme lacks the commercial cushion needed to absorb technical setbacks or cost overruns.

Given these factors, the prudent course is to defer a full commitment until the risk profile can be reduced. Actions that would make the proposal acceptable include securing additional launch customers, sharing development cost with the engine supplier, or phasing the factory build to match confirmed order flow.

**Recommendation:** Do not approve the proposal as it stands. Require risk mitigation steps before proceeding.

DECISION: NO-GO
