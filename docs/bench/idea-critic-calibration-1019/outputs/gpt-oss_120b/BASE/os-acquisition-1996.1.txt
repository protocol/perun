The PC maker’s OS is a strategic choke point: a dated system, two failed internal rebuilds, and a slipping market share indicate that the status‑quo cannot continue. An external source of technology is therefore essential.

**Strengths of the acquisition**
* Provides a ready‑made, modern, object‑oriented kernel that the maker has not been able to develop in‑house.
* Brings a focused development team and a founder who knows the code‑base intimately, potentially accelerating the transition.
* Offers a proprietary platform that could differentiate the maker’s hardware from competitors that rely on off‑the‑shelf OSes.

**Key weaknesses and risks**
* The target’s commercial track record is minimal – only a few units sold – so the product’s market acceptance and robustness are unproven at scale.
* Migration of existing applications and developer ecosystems to a new kernel is technically complex; legacy software may require substantial re‑engineering, risking loss of third‑party support and customer confidence.
* The founder’s “difficult history” raises governance concerns; reliance on a single advisor could create bottlenecks or cultural friction during integration.
* The purchase price (“a few hundred million dollars”) far exceeds the target’s current revenue, creating an immediate financial burden without a clear near‑term ROI.
* Licensing an established OS (e.g., Windows 95) would avoid many technical and integration risks, though it would sacrifice differentiation and involve recurring royalty costs.

**Strategic alternatives**
1. **License an existing OS** – lower upfront cost, proven ecosystem, faster time‑to‑market, but limited control and ongoing fees.
2. **Joint development partnership** – share risk and cost with the target while retaining more ownership of the IP.
3. **Incremental acquisition** – acquire only the core technology and key engineers, leaving the commercial business out, reducing price and integration scope.

**Recommendation**
Given the urgent need for a modern OS, the acquisition’s potential upside is attractive, yet the financial exposure, unproven product, and integration hazards outweigh the benefits at this stage. The board should pause the deal, conduct deeper due diligence on the technology’s scalability, negotiate a price linked to performance milestones, and explore licensing or partnership alternatives as fallback options.

DECISION: NO-GO
