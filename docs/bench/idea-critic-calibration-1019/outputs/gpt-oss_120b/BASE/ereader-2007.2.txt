The company’s core strength is its massive book catalog and retail platform, not hardware design or manufacturing. Entering the e‑reader market would require building a new supply chain, mastering industrial design, certification, and after‑sales support—capabilities it does not currently possess. The proposal’s hardware ambition (a “paper‑like” screen with free cellular connectivity) further amplifies cost and technical risk; free data service creates an ongoing expense that must be subsidised by book sales.

Pricing the e‑books at a “single low price per title” threatens margins on a product that already has thin profit contribution. It also raises the likelihood of publisher push‑back, since publishers already fear piracy and have shown reluctance to lower list prices. Their cooperation is essential for a healthy e‑book catalogue; antagonising them could limit content availability.

Historical market signals are unfavorable: earlier dedicated e‑readers “sold poorly,” indicating limited consumer appetite for standalone devices at that time. The proposed device could also cannibalize the company’s higher‑margin print business, eroding a proven revenue stream without a clear substitute profit model.

While the idea of a seamless, on‑the‑go buying experience is attractive, the combination of unfamiliar hardware, uncertain demand, margin pressure, and potential publisher resistance creates a risk profile that outweighs the strategic upside at this stage. A more prudent path would be to explore partnerships with established hardware makers or to focus on software‑only solutions (apps) that leverage existing devices, thereby testing the market with far lower capital exposure.

**Conclusion:** The proposal, as presented, poses too many operational and financial risks for approval.

DECISION: NO-GO
