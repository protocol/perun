**Assessment of the proposal**

**Market context (2004)** – Free webmail is already dominated by Hotmail, Yahoo! Mail and the newly launched Gmail, all offering 2‑10 MB of storage and proven ad‑supported models. Users expect basic reliability; a 1 GB offering would be a dramatic differentiation but also a massive cost driver.

**Cost & scalability** – At 2004 hardware prices, a single gigabyte of reliable storage costs roughly $10‑$15. Even a modest user base of 100 000 would require >1 TB of storage, translating to $10‑$15 k in hardware alone, plus redundancy, power, cooling and bandwidth. Ongoing operational expenditures (backup, replication, indexing for fast search) are likely to exceed the projected ad revenue in the launch phase.

**Privacy & legal risk** – Scanning message content to serve text ads creates a strong privacy objection. In 2004, user awareness of email surveillance is rising, and any perception of “spying” could generate backlash, legal challenges, and damage to brand reputation. Implementing content‑based ad targeting would also require robust security controls to prevent data leakage.

**Competitive response** – Entrenched players could quickly match the storage offering (Google later expanded to GBs) and have far larger ad ecosystems. Without a clear moat, the service risks being a costly “feature race” with limited differentiation beyond storage size.

**Revenue timing** – The model assumes ad revenue will offset costs only after a critical mass of users is reached. With no immediate cash flow, the venture would need substantial upfront financing and a clear break‑even horizon, which is not presented.

**Recommendation** – The concept is innovative but the risk profile is too high for approval in its current form. The proposal should be revised to:

1. Reduce initial storage (e.g., 100 MB) and scale up as usage data justifies.
2. Use non‑content‑based contextual ads to avoid privacy issues.
3. Pilot with a very limited invitation pool to validate cost, performance, and user acceptance.
4. Develop a detailed financial model showing break‑even timelines and required capital.

Given these concerns, the executive team should not approve the plan as written.

DECISION: NO-GO
