The proposal targets a nascent online‑gaming market in 2002. At launch, broadband penetration in most households is still limited; many players still rely on dial‑up or no connection at all. Introducing a console‑wide, paid online service that mandates broadband and an annual fee therefore creates three immediate barriers to adoption:

1. **Technical access** – A sizable portion of the installed base will be unable to use the service at all, shrinking the effective market from day one.
2. **Price expectation** – Competitors either offer free online play or leave it to publishers. A mandatory subscription runs counter to prevailing consumer expectations and risks alienating early adopters.
3. **Operational readiness** – The company has no experience running large‑scale hosting, customer support, or abuse‑moderation. Building this capability from scratch adds significant upfront cost and execution risk.

Potential upside—recurring revenue, a unified identity, and a richer social layer—are compelling, but they depend on achieving a critical mass of users. That mass will be hard to reach without either (a) a free‑to‑play entry point that lowers the barrier and builds a user base, or (b) a phased rollout that first targets markets with higher broadband adoption and partners with ISPs to subsidize connectivity.

A more prudent approach would be to pilot a limited‑feature, free tier (e.g., friends list and basic matchmaking) while the company builds the necessary backend and support infrastructure. After establishing a solid user base and operational competence, a premium tier could be introduced with voice chat, advanced matchmaking, and other value‑added services.

Given the current risk profile—low broadband penetration, misaligned pricing expectations, and untested operational requirements—the proposal as written is too risky to launch immediately.

DECISION: NO-GO
