The proposal describes a classic "blitzscaling" strategy reliant on perpetual capital availability and the assumption that market share can be bought cheaper than it can be built organically. The steelman is that dominating the market quickly creates a monopoly moat, allowing future price hikes to recoup losses.

However, the attack reveals fatal structural flaws. First, the unit economics are inverted: selling below cost while incurring high shipping expenses on heavy items means every additional customer deepens the loss hole. This is not a temporary subsidy; it is a mathematical guarantee of insolvency unless margins reverse immediately, which is unlikely given competitors are also spending heavily. Second, the timing is precarious. Going public in early 2000 places the company at the peak of the dot-com bubble. The plan assumes capital markets will remain open indefinitely to fund negative gross margins. If the bubble bursts (a high-probability event given the era), the company cannot raise the cash needed to sustain the TV campaign or the acquisition, leading to immediate collapse.

The "better way" analysis suggests that buying a competitor while both parties are burning cash simply consolidates losses without fixing the underlying margin problem. Building more warehouses increases fixed costs right before a potential demand shock. The strategy lacks a kill criterion; there is no defined point where customer acquisition costs are deemed unsustainable, nor a pivot plan if the IPO window closes.

The pre-mortem is clear: six months post-IPO, the market turns, the stock price collapses, and the company cannot fund its operating losses or debt from the acquisition. With negative margins on core goods and no path to profitability independent of external funding, the risk is not just failure, but total wipeout. The proposal mistakes velocity for viability. It fails the "do nothing" test; preserving cash and fixing unit economics is safer than accelerating toward a cliff.

DECISION: NO-GO
