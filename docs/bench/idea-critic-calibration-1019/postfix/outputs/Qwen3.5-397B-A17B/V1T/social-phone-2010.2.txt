The claim is that launching two social-networking phones on a new, app-less OS via a single carrier will succeed with teenagers despite high data costs and established rivals. The steelman posits that teens prioritize specific messaging features over general apps, and the sunk cost of two years justifies completing this unique path rather than pivoting to the company's existing platform.

However, the attack reveals fatal flaws. First, the "no application store" constraint contradicts the 2010 market reality where rivals offer large catalogs; teens, living on social networks, will reject a device that cannot access expanding third-party social tools. Second, pricing a data plan equivalent to a full smartphone plan targets a demographic with low disposable income, creating an immediate adoption barrier. Third, building a new in-house OS instead of leveraging the company's own mobile platform fragments resources and delays time-to-market without a compensating unique value proposition. The sunk cost of two years is a fallacy; continuing to spend on a misaligned product compounds the loss.

The "better way" is to port the popular messaging features to the company's existing mobile platform, partner with multiple carriers to offer competitive youth pricing, and ensure immediate access to an app ecosystem. The current proposal fails the "do nothing" test because the likelihood of market rejection is higher than the risk of delaying launch to integrate properly. The pre-mortem is clear: the phones launch, teens realize they cannot run preferred apps and cannot afford the data, and the hardware sits unsold while the new OS becomes a graveyard for developer interest.

There is no observable metric or condition where the current constraints (no apps, high cost, single carrier) lead to success against competitors with open ecosystems. The proposal requires fundamental structural changes, not minor tweaks.

DECISION: NO-GO
