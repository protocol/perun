The claim is that a mandatory, paid, broadband-only online service will succeed despite market norms favoring free, publisher-led models and low broadband penetration. The steelman: a unified, high-quality network creates a defensible moat and superior user experience that justifies a premium, forcing the market to upgrade rather than fragmenting it.

However, the "better-way" analysis reveals fatal friction. In 2002, broadband adoption is critically low; mandating it excludes the vast majority of the installed base, artificially capping the network effect before it starts. Competitors offering free or optional services can capture the mass market while this proposal restricts access to a tiny elite. The "do nothing" or "optional tier" alternative dominates on adoption risk.

The pre-mortem is clear: the service launches to a tiny audience because few users have the required infrastructure and are unwilling to pay a recurring fee for a feature competitors offer free. The company, lacking operations experience, bleeds cash on underutilized servers. The "one-way door" of a mandatory paywall prevents later pivoting to a free model without alienating early adopters who paid upfront.

The strongest attack is the infrastructure mismatch. The proposal assumes the market is ready for broadband-only gaming in 2002. Evidence suggests otherwise; dial-up is still dominant. Forcing broadband creates a barrier to entry that no amount of feature polish can overcome at this specific moment in time. The risk of limiting the player base is not just a concern; it is a certainty that kills the network effects required for matchmaking and social features to function.

While the vision of a unified identity is sound, the execution strategy of making it mandatory, paid, and broadband-exclusive in 2002 is premature and misaligned with market reality. A staged approach (optional, free tier, dial-up support) would be safer, but the proposal as written demands an all-or-nothing bet on unproven infrastructure adoption. The cost of delay (waiting for broadband to mature) is lower than the cost of a failed launch that alienates the current user base.

DECISION: NO-GO
