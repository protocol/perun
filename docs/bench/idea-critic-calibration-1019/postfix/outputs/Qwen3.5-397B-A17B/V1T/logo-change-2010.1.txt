The claim that an untested, unannounced, big-bang logo replacement will successfully modernize a mature brand without alienating its core base is fragile. The steelman is that a decisive, clean break signals immediate contemporaneity, avoiding the "half-measure" confusion of a phased rollout. However, this relies on the assumption that the brand's equity resides solely in its name, not its visual heritage, and that silence prevents backlash rather than fueling speculation.

Alternatives exist: a phased rollout allowing for feedback loops, a soft launch to test market reaction, or retaining the square as a secondary mark to preserve recognition while updating the primary wordmark. The proposed "all-at-once" approach eliminates the ability to pivot if the initial reaction is negative. The risk of instant social media backlash is high given the attachment loyal customers have to the old square; removing their primary recognition anchor without warning treats them as obstacles rather than stakeholders.

The pre-mortem is clear: the launch triggers a wave of negative sentiment accusing the brand of abandoning its heritage, leading to boycotts or a rushed, expensive reversion. The cost of delay (waiting to test or phase) is negligible compared to the reputational damage of a failed universal launch. There is no evidence provided that the new design resonates with the target demographic or that the loss of the "blue square" identifier won't degrade brand recall. The proposal lacks any kill criteria or rollback plan, treating the change as a one-way door despite the high probability of failure.

Without customer testing, a communication strategy, or a phased approach to mitigate risk, this proposal gambles the brand's existing equity on an unverified aesthetic preference. The absence of a pilot or feedback mechanism makes the "no advance announcement" strategy reckless rather than bold.

DECISION: NO-GO
