The claim is that a computer manufacturer should launch a tablet in 2011 using a recently acquired, low-share mobile operating system, priced equally to the market leader, despite a tiny app catalog and leadership transition.

The steelman: Leveraging existing hardware expertise and an owned OS creates long-term ecosystem control; heavy marketing can bridge the initial app gap if the device quality is superior.

However, the attack reveals fatal flaws. First, pricing parity with the dominant market leader while offering a fraction of the applications violates basic consumer value logic; shoppers will not pay a premium for an unproven, app-poor ecosystem. Second, the "small app catalog" is a critical failure point in a platform business; developer outreach takes years to mature, leaving early adopters with a broken experience. Third, launching during leadership transition invites strategic drift and internal confusion when execution must be flawless.

Better ways exist: Price significantly lower to penetrate the market, delay launch until the app ecosystem matures, or license a proven OS instead of forcing an acquired one. The proposal ignores the network effect barrier; without apps, users won't come, and without users, developers won't build.

Pre-mortem: The device launches, reviews praise the hardware but pan the lack of software, sales stall due to high price-to-value ratio, and the company abandons the OS within two years, writing off the acquisition.

The evidence provided (dominant competitor, tiny catalog, leadership flux, unproven brand in this category) overwhelmingly suggests this specific configuration (high price, low apps) will fail. The risks are not merely "high"; they are structural to the proposal's core pricing and timing assumptions. No kill criteria or pivot strategy is offered to mitigate the near-certain market rejection.

DECISION: NO-GO
