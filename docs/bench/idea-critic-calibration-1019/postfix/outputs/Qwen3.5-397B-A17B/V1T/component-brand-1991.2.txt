The claim is that a B2B component manufacturer can successfully drive demand by branding directly to end-consumers, forcing retail pull-through despite having no direct sales channel to them. The steelman: if buyers cannot distinguish chips and clones mimic part numbers, the only defensible moat is emotional brand equity that makes the component a purchase prerequisite, compelling computer makers to comply to avoid losing sales.

Alternatives include maintaining the status quo (competing solely on price/specs against clones), pursuing legal action against clone makers (slow, costly, uncertain), or offering deeper discounts to computer makers (erodes margin, fails to solve the identification problem). Doing nothing guarantees commoditization.

Assumptions tested: (1) Consumers care enough about an invisible component to ask for it by name. (2) Computer makers will prioritize the chip maker's brand over their own system branding. (3) The advertising subsidy is sufficient to change behavior without bankrupting the marketing budget.

Pre-mortem: The campaign launches, consumers remain indifferent to the logo, computer makers resent the intrusion and quietly drop participation once subsidies tighten, and the budget is wasted with no sales lift. The "unprecedented" nature of the risk is valid; component branding has no historical precedent because it violates standard B2B logic. However, the specific context (indistinguishable products, clone confusion) creates a unique necessity where standard logic fails.

The strongest attack is that computer makers will resent the loss of control over their advertising. This fails only if the chip maker proves that the logo *increases* total system sales enough to offset the annoyance. The proposal includes a financial lever (paying part of ad costs) to align incentives immediately. Without this, the chip becomes a commodity; with it, the chip becomes a premium feature. The risk of budget drain is real but is the cost of survival against clones. The "unprecedented" risk is outweighed by the certainty of margin collapse if clones win the confusion game.

No fatal flaw exists provided the subsidy is tied strictly to verified logo placement and sales lift is measured early. The potential reward (owning the category mindshare) dominates the cost.

DECISION: GO
