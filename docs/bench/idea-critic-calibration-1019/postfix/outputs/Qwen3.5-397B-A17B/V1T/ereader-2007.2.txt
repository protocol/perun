The claim is that a non-hardware bookseller should launch a proprietary e-reader with free cellular connectivity to dominate the emerging digital market. The steelman: owning the device and distribution pipeline creates an insurmountable moat, locking customers into the ecosystem despite high upfront costs and operational inexperience.

However, critical flaws exist. First, the company lacks hardware DNA; entering this domain risks catastrophic capital burn on manufacturing, supply chain, and support failures. Second, the "free cellular" model assumes telecom carriers will accept negligible revenue for massive data usage, a dangerous assumption without pre-negotiated contracts. Third, aggressive low pricing to drive adoption may trigger publisher revolts, starving the device of content—the very asset that justifies its existence. Finally, cannibalization of high-margin print sales could destroy core profitability before digital volume compensates.

Better ways exist: license existing hardware partners to test demand without balance sheet risk, or launch a software-only app for existing devices to build the digital catalog first. Doing nothing preserves cash while competitors validate the market.

The pre-mortem reveals failure via hardware delays, unsustainable carrier costs, or content strikes. This is a one-way door with irreversible capital commitment. The cost of delay is losing first-mover advantage, but the cost of failure is existential.

Kill criteria are missing. There is no defined metric for carrier partnership viability, no cap on hardware loss-leader spending, and no pivot trigger if print cannibalization exceeds 15%. Without these guardrails, the proposal is a gamble, not a strategy. The risks of inexperience, unverified carrier economics, and publisher backlash are fatal in the current form.

DECISION: NO-GO
