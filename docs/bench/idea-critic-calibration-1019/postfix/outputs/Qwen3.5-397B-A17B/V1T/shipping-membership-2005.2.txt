**Steelman:** An upfront $79 fee secures customer loyalty and increases order frequency, offsetting per-order shipping friction and generating predictable recurring revenue despite short-term margin compression.

**Attack & Alternatives:**
The primary failure mode is adverse selection: if heavy buyers exceed the shipping cost break-even point before the year ends, the retailer loses money on every loyal customer. The "hard to reverse" risk is real; cancelling an active membership damages trust irreparably. However, the "do nothing" alternative guarantees continued friction for repeat buyers, capping growth. Cheaper alternatives like free shipping thresholds ($50+) exist but fail to lock in annual loyalty or upfront cash flow.

**Pre-mortem & Evidence:**
Assume failure: The average member costs $90 to ship but pays only $79. This happens if the retailer underestimates the elasticity of demand (users ordering *more* because it's "free"). The proposal admits economics are "unproven," which is a fatal flaw for a board-level commitment without a pilot. Yet, the structural logic holds: shipping is a variable cost that can be optimized via volume negotiations with carriers once membership scales. The one-way door (reversal difficulty) is mitigated by the fact that memberships are annual; the program can simply stop selling new ones if unit economics fail, letting the book run off naturally.

**Calibration:**
Is the lack of proven economics a stopper? No, provided the $79 price point is derived from data showing the average customer currently spends significantly less than $79 on annual shipping. If the average customer currently pays $40/year in shipping, they won't buy. If they pay $100, they will. The proposal assumes the latter. Without data, this is a gamble, but the potential to fundamentally alter the retail model outweighs the short-term margin hit. The risk is contained to the marketing budget if rolled out carefully. The "heavy buyer" risk is actually the goal: we want them to order more, increasing total gross merchandise value (GMV), even if shipping margins dip.

**Verdict:**
The strategic upside of locking in loyalty and changing consumer behavior justifies the short-term margin risk. The inability to reverse is manageable by halting sales rather than refunding.

DECISION: GO
