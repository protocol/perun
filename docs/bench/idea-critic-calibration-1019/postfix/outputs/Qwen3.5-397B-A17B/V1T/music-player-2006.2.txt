**Steelman:** A well-funded entrant can disrupt a complacent leader by offering superior hardware (screen, wireless sharing) and a seamless ecosystem, betting that user experience outweighs first-mover advantage.

**Skeptic Attack:** The proposal admits the company is "late to market" against a dominant player with a "loyal ecosystem." Crucially, it proposes dropping the copy-protection standard the company previously championed. This creates an immediate credibility crisis with partner stores and device makers who relied on that standard, potentially isolating the new store before launch. If labels restrict the touted "wireless sharing" due to piracy fears—a high probability given the era's climate—the primary hardware differentiator becomes a liability or is disabled, leaving an expensive, late device with no unique value.

**Better Way:** The "do nothing" or "partner" option is stronger. Instead of burning capital on a late hardware race and antagonizing partners by flipping DRM standards, the company should license its software or partner with the existing leader. Entering a hardware war without a clear price advantage or exclusive content against an entrenched monopoly in 2006 is a capital incinerator.

**Pre-mortem:** One year post-launch, the device is a niche curiosity. Labels blocked wireless sharing, rendering the "larger screen" useless for the promised social features. Partners boycotted the store due to the DRM reversal. The marketing budget bought awareness but not conversion against the incumbent's network effects. The division is shut down.

**Fit & Evidence:** The proposal relies on a "large marketing budget" to overcome structural barriers (ecosystem lock-in, label restrictions). History shows marketing cannot fix a value proposition undermined by content restrictions and lateness. The risk of alienating existing partners while failing to secure new content is fatal.

**Kill Criteria:** The failure to secure unconditional label support for wireless sharing *before* hardware manufacturing is a fatal flaw. The reversal on copy-protection guarantees partner hostility.

**Verdict:** The combination of lateness, partner alienation, and high dependency on label cooperation for the core feature makes this proposal too risky to approve as stated. The strategic flip on DRM alone threatens the entire supply chain.

DECISION: NO-GO
