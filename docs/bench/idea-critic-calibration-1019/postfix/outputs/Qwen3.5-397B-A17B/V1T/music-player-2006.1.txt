The proposal attempts to displace a dominant incumbent by entering late with a fragmented ecosystem strategy. The core claim relies on wireless sharing and a larger screen to overcome the leader's entrenched network effects and loyal user base. However, the proposal explicitly admits to dropping the industry-standard copy-protection mechanism that previously secured partner and label support. This is not a minor deviation; it is a structural barrier to content acquisition. Without label cooperation, the store lacks the catalog necessary to compete, rendering the device a hollow shell regardless of screen size or marketing spend.

Comparing alternatives, "doing nothing" avoids burning capital on a likely failure. A "cheaper path" would involve licensing the existing dominant ecosystem rather than rebuilding it from scratch with inferior compatibility. The proposal fails the "fit" test: entering a mature market late requires a decisive advantage in content or price, neither of which is present. The risk of labels restricting sharing due to the abandoned DRM standard is a near-certain failure mode, not a manageable risk.

The pre-mortem is clear: the device launches with a limited library because rights holders refuse to support a non-standard, open-sharing environment. The marketing budget accelerates awareness of a product that cannot deliver the promised experience. The cost of delay is low because the market is already captured; rushing a flawed product only cements the competitor's victory. There is no viable kill criterion that saves this specific approach because the fatal flaw is foundational (content access), not operational. The proposal asks to fight a war on two fronts (hardware and content) while voluntarily disarming on the content front.

DECISION: NO-GO
