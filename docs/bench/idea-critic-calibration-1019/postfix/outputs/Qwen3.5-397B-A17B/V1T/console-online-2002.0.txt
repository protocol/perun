The proposal asserts that a mandatory, paid, console-wide online service will succeed despite low broadband adoption and a market expectation of free play. The steelman is that a unified, high-quality network with voice chat and matchmaking creates a defensible premium ecosystem that rivals cannot match with fragmented, free alternatives.

However, the "better-way" analysis reveals a fatal mismatch with current reality. In 2002, broadband penetration is critically low; mandating it excludes the vast majority of the installed base. Competitors offering free or publisher-hosted options remove the barrier to entry entirely. The proposal requires the company to build massive hosting and support operations from scratch, introducing extreme operational risk and fixed costs before proving demand.

The pre-mortem is clear: the service launches, the addressable market (broadband owners willing to pay extra) proves too small to cover the new infrastructure costs, and the higher total cost of ownership drives gamers to rival consoles with free online capabilities. The "do nothing" or "optional freemium" alternatives preserve the player base while allowing organic adoption of online features as broadband spreads.

The claim that users will pay an annual subscription on top of the console price for a service they currently expect to be free is an unsupported assumption in this specific historical context. The risk of limiting the player base is not just a downside; it is an existential threat to the platform's network effects. The operational inexperience compounds this, making the cost of failure catastrophic. There is no kill criterion mentioned that would prevent a massive loss of capital on an unproven model in an unready market. The proposal fails to beat the "do nothing" or "optional" alternatives on risk and reversibility.

DECISION: NO-GO
