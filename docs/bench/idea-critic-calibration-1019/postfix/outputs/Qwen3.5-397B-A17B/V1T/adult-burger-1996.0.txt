The claim is that a premium burger, backed by record-breaking ad spend, will pivot a child-centric brand to an adult demographic. The steelman: a massive, visible signal is required to break the entrenched "kids' meal" perception, and only a dominant media blitz can force adults to reconsider the brand's value proposition quickly.

However, the attack reveals fatal flaws in fit and evidence. First, the strategy contradicts the brand's core identity; adults seeking premium food actively avoid venues defined by children's entertainment, regardless of ad spend. Second, the proposal ignores the "value" anchor; raising prices without a proven shift in perceived quality risks alienating the existing base without securing the new one. Third, and most critically, the plan relies on the largest advertising budget in industry history *before* validating demand. This is a one-way door with catastrophic downside: if the product-market fit is wrong (as the raised risks suggest), the sunk cost is unrecoverable.

The "do nothing" or "staged pilot" alternatives dominate this approach. A limited regional test with moderate spend would verify if adults actually buy the premium item at the higher price point. If they do not, the loss is contained. If they do, the ad budget can be scaled. Launching nationally with maximum spend assumes the answer to the most dangerous question ("Will adults pay more for our food?") is "yes" without data. The risks raised—mismatched perception, price sensitivity, and unproven demand—are not mitigated by spending more money; they are exacerbated by it.

The proposal fails the pre-mortem: it assumes brand perception is malleable via volume alone, ignoring deep-seated consumer psychology. There is no kill criteria or staged rollout to limit exposure. The evidence provided (risks raised) directly contradicts the plan's core assumption that awareness equals adoption. Without a pilot to prove the premium angle works, the massive spend is a gamble, not a strategy.

DECISION: NO-GO
