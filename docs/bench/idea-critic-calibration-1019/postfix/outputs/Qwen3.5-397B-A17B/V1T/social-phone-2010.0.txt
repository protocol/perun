The proposal fails the "do nothing" and "cheaper alternative" tests. Launching a new, unproven operating system in 2010 ignores the critical market reality: teenagers on social networks demand immediate access to existing, popular third-party applications. A device with no app store at launch is functionally broken for this demographic, regardless of its messaging heritage.

The "sunk cost" of two years of development is a trap; continuing to spend on a doomed platform compounds the loss. The decision to bypass the company's own established mobile platform for a new, in-house OS introduces massive, unnecessary technical risk and delays time-to-market. Furthermore, the mandatory high-cost data plan creates a prohibitive barrier for price-sensitive teenagers, especially when rival smartphones offer comparable or superior data plans with full app ecosystems.

The strongest attack on this claim is the absence of an application ecosystem. Without apps, the phone is merely a limited messaging device, unable to compete with rivals offering comprehensive smart experiences. The proposal offers no viable path to acquire essential apps quickly. A better way would be to leverage the acquired messaging brand on an existing, popular platform (like Android or iOS) or the company's own mature mobile OS, ensuring immediate app availability and lower development costs.

The pre-mortem is clear: the phones launch to poor reviews citing the lack of apps, sales falter due to the high data cost, and the new OS fails to gain traction, resulting in a total write-off of the sunk costs plus significant new losses. The one-way door here is the commitment to a proprietary OS and single carrier deal, which locks the company into a failing strategy.

There are no kill criteria that salvage this specific proposal; the fundamental value proposition is flawed. The risks are fatal because they address core user needs (apps) and affordability (data plan), which the proposal explicitly neglects.

DECISION: NO-GO
