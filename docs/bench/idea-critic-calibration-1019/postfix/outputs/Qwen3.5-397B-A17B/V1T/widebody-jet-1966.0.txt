**Steelman:** Building a massively larger, wide-body freighter-capable jet secures market dominance against supersonic threats and meets urgent airline demand for lower seat costs, despite unprecedented financial and technical risks.

**Skeptic:** The development outlay exceeds all prior company history; failure risks bankruptcy. The engine is unproven, and the market bet assumes subsonic travel persists despite supersonic expectations. Relying on a single launch customer creates catastrophic exposure if they default.

**Better-Way:** Alternatives include stretching existing models (lower risk, less capacity), waiting for supersonic trends to clarify (avoiding stranded assets), or partnering to share factory costs. However, "do nothing" cedes the efficiency race to competitors and ignores the specific customer mandate driving the request.

**Fit & Pre-mortem:** If the supersonic transition happens fast, this factory becomes a white elephant. If the engine fails, the plane is grounded. Yet, the "raised cockpit" design explicitly hedges against obsolescence by enabling freighter conversion, addressing the "what if passenger demand drops" scenario. The cost of delay is losing the primary customer and the efficiency lead.

**Evidence & Calibration:** The proposal acknowledges the risks (size, engine, single buyer) rather than hiding them. The specific design choice (raised cockpit) demonstrates a concrete mitigation strategy for the biggest long-term risk (passenger vs. freight shift). While the financial exposure is extreme, the "do nothing" option guarantees irrelevance if subsonic travel continues to grow as projected. The single airline order is a weakness, but also a validated demand signal. No fatal flaw exists that isn't already identified and partially mitigated by the design flexibility. The risk is existential, but the opportunity is equally definitive for market leadership.

**Verdict:** The proposal survives the attack because it converts the biggest uncertainty (future cargo need) into a design feature, and the "do nothing" alternative carries its own existential risk of market irrelevance.
