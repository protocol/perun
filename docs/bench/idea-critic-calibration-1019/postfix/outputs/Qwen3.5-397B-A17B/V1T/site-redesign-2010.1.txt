The claim is that a full rewrite with altered mechanics and an immediate, irreversible cutover will reverse slowing traffic. The steelman: only a radical break from the current architecture can enable the necessary shift toward publisher content to compete with a growing rival.

However, the attack reveals fatal flaws in this specific approach. First, the proposal explicitly alienates the core asset: the loyal community that values existing voting mechanics. Changing these mechanics while simultaneously forcing a migration removes the very users required to generate the content that attracts publishers. Second, the "all-at-once" launch eliminates the ability to test the new surfacing logic or rollback if engagement collapses, turning a large engineering risk into an existential threat. Third, while a rival grows, the cost of delay in fixing the current platform is likely lower than the cost of a failed rewrite that drives users away permanently.

The "do nothing" alternative (optimizing the current stack) preserves community trust while allowing incremental testing of publisher features. A staged rollout or A/B test would validate the new mechanics without betting the entire user base on an unproven hypothesis. The proposal fails the pre-mortem: if this fails, it is because the core community revolted against changed rules and the team had no way to revert the change quickly enough to save the site.

The lack of a pilot, the one-way door nature of the cutover, and the direct conflict with stated community values constitute a concrete failure scenario. The proposal assumes the community will accept new rules without evidence, and that a rewrite is the only path to publisher favor. These are unsupported claims presented as fact. The risk of losing the loyal base to chase a hypothetical publisher influx, with no safety net, is unacceptable.

DECISION: NO-GO
