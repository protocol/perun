**Steelman:** Acquiring a modern, object-oriented OS core from a small vendor is the only viable path to rapidly replace a failing internal platform, halt market share erosion, and leverage external innovation where internal attempts have twice failed.

**Attack & Alternatives:**
The primary objection is financial: paying a premium for low-revenue assets. However, the cost of *inaction* is total platform irrelevance. The "licensing alternative" fails because it cedes control of the core differentiator to a competitor, perpetuating dependency rather than solving the strategic deficit. The "migration risk" is real but inferior to the certainty of continuing with a dated OS that cannot support modern applications.

Regarding the founder's "difficult history": past friction often stems from the very structural constraints this acquisition removes. Bringing them back as an advisor aligns incentives without granting immediate operational control, mitigating personnel risk while securing critical institutional knowledge. The low unit sales of the target are irrelevant; the value lies in the architecture's suitability as a foundation, not its current commercial traction.

**Pre-mortem & Kill Criteria:**
Failure scenario: The integration stalls due to cultural clash, delaying the launch by over 18 months, allowing competitors to cement dominance.
Mitigation: The acquisition must be structured as a technology buy-in with a strict, time-boxed integration milestone (e.g., developer beta within 12 months). If the core cannot run key legacy applications via emulation or compatibility layers within 6 months, the project pivots to a pure licensing strategy immediately.

**Decision:**
The proposal addresses an existential threat where internal R&D has proven incapable. The risks of acquisition are manageable compared to the fatal risk of status quo. The "high price" is a rational premium for survival and speed. The alternative (licensing) solves the technical gap but creates a strategic dead end. The founder's return is a calculated risk necessary for technical fidelity.

DECISION: GO
