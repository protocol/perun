The claim is that a mandatory online client will solve update/cheating issues and create a new store revenue stream. Steelman: Centralizing distribution via a proprietary client is the only viable path to control software integrity, monetize DLC, and build a defensible ecosystem against piracy in the current PC market.

However, the proposal fails the "do nothing" and "cheaper alternative" tests relative to 2003 constraints. First, the "home broadband is not universal" risk is fatal to a *mandatory* client. In 2003, dial-up was still dominant for a significant portion of the core PC gaming demographic; forcing a large download and persistent connection excludes a massive revenue segment immediately. Second, the risk of retail partners dropping boxed games is not merely a friction point but an existential threat to cash flow for a publisher reliant on physical shelf space. Alienating the primary distribution channel before the digital alternative is proven creates a liquidity crisis.

The "better way" is a hybrid approach: retain boxed sales with optional, incentive-driven online registration for updates and anti-cheat, rather than a hard gate. This captures the security benefits for willing users without severing the retail lifeline or blocking low-bandwidth users. The proposal's plan to later open the store is premature; the company must first prove it can operate a stable client for its own titles without crashing under load or sparking a consumer revolt.

The pre-mortem is clear: The client launches with server instability (common for 2003 infrastructure), dial-up users cannot patch, retailers delist the games in retaliation, and the installed base shrinks rather than grows. The cost of delay (waiting for higher broadband penetration) is low compared to the cost of a botched mandatory rollout that destroys retail relationships. The proposal ignores the infrastructure ceiling and channel conflict. It attempts a platform play before securing the utility play.

DECISION: NO-GO
