Steelman: Transform an invisible component into a consumer demand driver by subsidizing partner ads featuring a unified logo, forcing buyers to specify the chip by name.

**Skeptic:** The core assumption is that end-users care about or understand internal components. Historically, B2B ingredient branding fails when the host product's brand dominates the decision. If computer makers resent the creative constraints or view the subsidy as insufficient leverage, adoption will stall. The "unprecedented" nature of this move suggests a high probability of channel conflict rather than cooperation. Without verified buy-in from major manufacturers, the budget spend yields zero reach.

**Better-way:** The "do nothing" option preserves capital for R&D, maintaining the technical lead which ultimately drives OEM selection regardless of consumer noise. A cheaper alternative is a trade-focused program rewarding manufacturers for volume milestones, avoiding the complexity and cost of consumer media buying. Another path is co-branding only with a single flagship partner to prove the concept before a full rollout, reducing the "large share of marketing budget" risk.

**Kill-criteria:** This is a one-way door; once the brand promise is made to consumers, retreating signals weakness. The pre-mortem reveals failure if computer makers refuse to place the logo prominently or if consumer surveys show no recognition lift within six months. The cost of delay is low; waiting for a competitor to attempt this first provides valuable market data without burning cash. The proposal lacks a specific metric for success (e.g., "premium price retention") or a stop-loss condition if OEM participation drops below a critical threshold.

**Verdict:** The proposal bets the marketing budget on an unproven behavioral shift in a B2B2C chain where the middleman (computer maker) holds the power. The risks of channel resentment and budget dilution outweigh the speculative benefit of consumer pull, especially without a pilot program or guaranteed OEM compliance mechanisms. The lack of a defined kill criterion for low adoption makes this too dangerous to approve as stated.

DECISION: NO-GO
