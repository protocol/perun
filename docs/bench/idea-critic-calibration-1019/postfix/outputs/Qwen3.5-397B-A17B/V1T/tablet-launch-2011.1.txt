The claim is that a computer manufacturer should launch a tablet in 2011 using a recently acquired mobile operating system, priced at parity with the market leader, despite a tiny app catalog and negligible OS market share.

Steelman: The company can leverage its brand loyalty and vertical integration to overcome late-entry disadvantages, using heavy marketing to jumpstart developer interest before the market fully consolidates.

However, the attack reveals fatal flaws in the "priced in line" and "small app catalog" combination. In a two-sided marketplace, users choose the platform with the most apps, and developers choose the platform with the most users. Entering with a fraction of the leader's apps while charging a premium price removes the only viable entry strategy: undercutting on cost or exceeding on niche utility. The "heavy investment" in outreach cannot instantly manufacture the network effect required to retain developers if early adopters churn due to app scarcity.

The "do nothing" or "wait" alternative is stronger. Entering during leadership transition adds execution risk to an already fragile value proposition. History shows that hardware alone cannot save an orphaned software ecosystem unless the price is aggressively disruptive (loss-leading) or the device offers不可替代 functionality. This proposal does neither; it asks customers to pay full price for an inferior experience based on future promises.

The pre-mortem is clear: The device launches, early reviewers praise the hardware but pan the app gap, sales miss targets due to the high price, developers deprioritize the platform, and the company writes off the acquisition. The cost of delay is low compared to the reputational damage and capital burn of a failed flagship launch.

There is no kill criterion that saves this specific configuration. To proceed, the price must be significantly below the leader to compensate for the app deficit, or the launch must be delayed until the catalog matures. As proposed—premium price, immature ecosystem—the unit economics and adoption curves do not support success. The risk of cannibalizing the brand's premium perception outweighs the potential gain of a marginal market share grab.

DECISION: NO-GO
