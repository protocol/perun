The claim is that a full rewrite with altered voting mechanics and a forced migration will reverse slowing traffic. The steelman is that only a radical departure can outpace a rival and re-engage publishers.

However, the attack reveals fatal flaws in the "all-at-once" strategy and the modification of core mechanics. The community's loyalty is explicitly tied to existing voting mechanics; changing these alienates the primary asset while the rival grows. A full rewrite introduces massive engineering risk without validated demand for the new publisher-favoring algorithm. Most critically, moving all users simultaneously eliminates the ability to rollback, turning any unforeseen bug or community revolt into a catastrophic, unrecoverable event.

Better ways exist: A/B test the new surfacing logic on a traffic segment while keeping the core voting intact. Iterate on the rewrite module-by-module to reduce engineering risk. Engage the community before altering the rules they value.

The pre-mortem is clear: Launch day arrives, the community rebels against the changed voting rules, traffic plummets further due to confusion and anger, a critical bug is found in the new codebase, and because there is no rollback path, the site remains broken or empty while the rival captures the entire user base. The cost of delay is lower than the cost of a failed big-bang launch; waiting allows for staged testing.

This proposal fails the kill criteria: it lacks a safe rollback path (one-way door with high downside), ignores the specific risk of alienating the core community, and bets the company on an unproven rewrite. The "fatal flaw" is the irreversible, all-at-once migration combined with changing the core mechanic that defines the community.

DECISION: NO-GO
