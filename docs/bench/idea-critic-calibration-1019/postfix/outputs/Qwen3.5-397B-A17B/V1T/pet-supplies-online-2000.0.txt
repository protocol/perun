The proposal describes a classic "blitzscale" burn strategy reliant on perpetual capital availability and negative unit economics to purchase market share. The steelman is that speed creates an unassailable monopoly before competitors can react, allowing future price hikes to recoup losses. However, this claim collapses under basic arithmetic and the specific context of early 2000.

The plan requires selling products below cost while simultaneously increasing fixed costs (warehouses) and variable costs (national TV ads, heavy shipping). This creates a negative feedback loop: every additional customer increases the cash bleed. The strategy assumes capital markets will remain open indefinitely to fund these losses. In early 2000, the dot-com bubble is peaking; investor sentiment is shifting rapidly from "growth at all costs" to profitability. A single quarter of missed expectations or a market correction would cut off funding immediately.

There is no "do nothing" or "smaller scale" alternative considered. A better way would be to secure profitability in existing regions before expanding nationally, or to focus on high-margin niche products rather than heavy, low-margin goods that exacerbate shipping losses. Buying a competitor while insolvent adds integration costs to an already burning balance sheet.

The pre-mortem is clear: the company goes public, the market turns within six months, and the stock crashes because the path to profitability is nonexistent. The company runs out of cash while locked into long-term leases and ad contracts, leading to bankruptcy. The "kill criterion" here is immediate: any plan requiring negative gross margins to function is fatal when capital access is uncertain. The risks raised (heavy shipping, negative margins, capital closure) are not just risks; they are certainties that invalidate the business model.

This is not a strategic gamble; it is a mathematical impossibility under the stated constraints. The proposal ignores the fundamental requirement that revenue must eventually exceed the cost of goods sold plus acquisition and fulfillment costs.

DECISION: NO-GO
