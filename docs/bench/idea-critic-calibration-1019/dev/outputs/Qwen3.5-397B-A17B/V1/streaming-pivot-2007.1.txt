The claim is that adding a free, inferior streaming tier in 2007 protects a profitable DVD monopoly against future tech giants. The steelman: this is a low-cost defensive experiment to retain subscribers and gather data before the market shifts, leveraging existing cash flow to fund a transition that otherwise leaves the company obsolete.

However, the skeptic identifies fatal flaws in the specific proposal parameters. Offering streaming at **no extra charge** while the catalogue is small, quality is poor, and licensing costs are high creates immediate negative unit economics without a clear path to profitability. It invites cannibalization of the high-margin DVD business by substituting a premium physical product with an inferior digital one, eroding revenue per user while increasing content costs. The "defensive" argument fails because a free, poor-quality product does not lock in users; it merely trains them to expect digital delivery while burning cash on licenses they cannot monetize.

The better-way analysis suggests a paid add-on or a separate, priced digital tier would test demand without subsidizing the switch or destroying margin. A "do nothing" stance preserves current profits until technology and licensing terms improve, which history shows happens rapidly. The proposal's specific combination of "free," "poor quality," and "high cost" guarantees financial leakage.

Pre-mortem: The initiative launches, DVD cancellations rise due to confusion or perceived value shift, licensing fees drain quarterly earnings, and the company lacks the capital to compete when true high-quality entrants arrive. The one-way door here is the precedent of free digital access and the long-term licensing commitments made at peak prices.

The kill criteria are missing: there is no cap on losses, no metric to halt expansion if cannibalization exceeds a specific threshold, and no pivot plan if adoption remains low due to poor quality. Without a paid model or strict loss limits, this is not a strategic pivot; it is uncontrolled self-disruption. The risks of competing with larger entrants are real, but this specific approach ensures defeat by weakening the current cash cow before the new engine works.

DECISION: NO-GO
