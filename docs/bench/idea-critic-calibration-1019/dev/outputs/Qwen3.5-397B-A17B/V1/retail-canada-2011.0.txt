The claim is that rapid, large-scale international expansion via lease acquisition is the optimal entry strategy. The steelman: securing 130–150 sites immediately creates a dominant footprint before the entrenched competitor can react, leveraging the retailer's domestic loyalty to capture price-sensitive customers in a high-cost market.

However, the "better-way" analysis reveals fatal flaws. The proposal ignores the "do nothing" or "pilot" alternative. Entering a new country with unproven cross-border logistics, untested inventory systems, and unfamiliar bilingual regulations simultaneously violates the principle of staged risk. The cost of failure here is not a single store closing, but the collapse of the entire supply chain and brand reputation in a new region. A cheaper, safer path exists: open 5–10 pilot stores to validate the supply chain, pricing elasticity, and regulatory compliance before committing capital to 150 leases.

The pre-mortem is clear: if the new inventory system fails (a high probability given it is unproven in this context), 150 stores will face stockouts or overstock situations immediately, burning cash on rent without revenue. The "one-way door" nature of long-term leases in a foreign legal environment makes reversal prohibitively expensive. The proposal assumes the domestic model translates directly, ignoring the specific friction of cross-border logistics and local competition.

The strongest attack survived is the fear of losing sites to competitors. Yet, speed without viability is suicide. The retailer's loyal domestic following does not guarantee immediate cross-border adoption, especially against an entrenched local player. The price perception risk is compounded by the operational complexity of bilingual labeling and new regulations, which could delay openings and increase costs, negating the "discount" advantage.

There is no kill criterion or staged approach to mitigate the massive upfront exposure. The proposal demands a binary bet on unproven systems and markets. A sound strategy requires verifying the supply chain and inventory system in a controlled environment first. Without a pilot phase, the risk of total failure is unacceptably high.

DECISION: NO-GO
