The claim is that a grocery startup with no operating history should immediately commit $1B to build automated warehouses in a dozen cities before validating unit economics. The steelman argues that speed and infrastructure dominance are necessary to secure market share before competitors, leveraging abundant venture capital to bypass the slow learning curve of organic growth.

However, this proposal fails the fundamental test of business viability. Groceries are notoriously low-margin, requiring extreme operational efficiency and high customer density to succeed. The proposal explicitly admits there is no track record and unit economics are unknown. Committing $1B to fixed, highly specialized assets (automated warehouses) before proving the core model works in a single city creates catastrophic irreversible risk. If the first city fails or requires a different operational model, the company is left with a dozen useless white elephants and no cash.

The "better way" is clear: validate the model in the first metropolitan area. Prove that customers will pay for the service at a profit, determine the optimal warehouse size and automation level, and establish real customer density metrics. Only then should capital be deployed to expand. The cost of delay here is negligible compared to the cost of building the wrong infrastructure at scale. A one-way door decision of this magnitude requires evidence that simply does not exist yet.

The pre-mortem is obvious: the company runs out of cash because the automated warehouses in new cities are underutilized, the technology proves mismatched to actual grocery workflows, and the low margins cannot support the debt service on $1B of premature construction. The leading metric for any expansion must be proven, repeatable unit economics in the initial market, not just order volume.

This is not a calculated bet; it is a gamble with near-certain failure modes due to the inversion of the learning sequence. You cannot scale what you have not yet validated. The risks of capital intensity and thin margins are exacerbated, not mitigated, by this approach.

DECISION: NO-GO
