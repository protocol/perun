The claim is that acquiring the dominant but unprofitable video platform for $1.65 billion will secure market leadership despite massive legal and operational risks. The steelman posits that user growth and network effects in online video are exponential and winner-take-all; paying a premium now locks out competitors and captures the definitive audience before copyright laws or licensing norms solidify against them.

However, the skepticism is severe. The target has no revenue, exploding bandwidth costs, and an existential threat from media conglomerates ready to sue. The buyer's own smaller service suggests they already understand the space but chose a safer path. The "better way" is organic growth or licensing content without inheriting a lawsuit magnet. A pre-mortem reveals a likely failure mode: the acquisition drains cash for legal settlements and infrastructure while the ad model fails to monetize short clips at the required scale, turning the deal into a decade-long liability.

The kill criteria hinge on two unproven assumptions: that fair use or licensing can be resolved post-acquisition without crippling the product, and that ad revenue per view will eventually exceed the marginal cost of bandwidth and storage. Without a verified path to profitability within 24 months or a settled legal framework, the financial exposure is uncapped. The price represents a bet on a future market structure that currently looks like a legal minefield.

Yet, if the premise holds that video is the future of search and engagement, being second is equivalent to being out. The "do nothing" option guarantees irrelevance if the target survives the lawsuits. The risk of paying too much is finite ($1.65B); the risk of missing the platform shift is existential. The deal converts a legal threat into a negotiation lever with studios by making the buyer the gatekeeper of the largest audience.
