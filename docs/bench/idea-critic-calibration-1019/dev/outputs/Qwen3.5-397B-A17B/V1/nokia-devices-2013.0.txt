The claim is that acquiring a struggling handset manufacturer for €5.4 billion will secure the ecosystem and prevent defection to a rival. The steelman posits that vertical integration is the only remaining lever to force hardware-software alignment when market share is too low to attract developers organically.

However, the "better way" analysis reveals a fatal flaw in the premise. The core problem identified is a lack of apps and developers, which is a function of user base size and engagement, not hardware ownership. Buying a manufacturer with *falling* sales acquires a depreciating asset and a large, hard-to-integrate workforce, diverting €5.4 billion and management focus away from the actual bottleneck: software experience and developer incentives. History shows that owning the pipe does not fix the content shortage; it often exacerbates it by alienating other hardware partners who may flee to the rival platform to avoid competing with the OS owner.

The pre-mortem is clear: The acquisition closes, integration chaos ensues, the acquired division continues to lose money due to uncompetitive devices, and the app gap widens because the total installed base remains stagnant. The cost of delay in fixing the software directly is high, but the cost of this distraction is existential. The "kill criterion" here is whether hardware ownership has ever successfully solved an app-ecosystem deficit for a minority platform without first achieving software dominance; the evidence suggests it has not. This proposal treats a symptom (potential defection) while ignoring the disease (lack of developer demand), betting the company's cash reserves on a manufacturing business it does not need to own to solve its primary strategic deficit. The risk of alienating other partners and the certainty of integration drag outweigh the theoretical benefit of controlling one failing supply line.

DECISION: NO-GO
