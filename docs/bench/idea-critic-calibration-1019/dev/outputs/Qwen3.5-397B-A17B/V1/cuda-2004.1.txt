The claim is that a graphics-chip maker should bet its mass-market architecture on general-purpose scientific computing between 2004 and 2006, despite zero current revenue from this segment.

Steelman: Embedding programmability now creates an unassailable moat by the time scientific demand matures, turning commodity hardware into a platform lock-in before rivals react.

However, the attack reveals fatal flaws in the specific proposal scope. First, the plan mandates adding chip area and raising unit costs on **every** consumer chip immediately. In a hyper-competitive gaming market where performance-per-dollar dictates market share, voluntarily increasing costs without a paying customer base risks ceding core gaming volume to rivals. Second, building a full compiler and toolchain for a non-existent market diverts critical engineering resources from the primary existential threat: the rival's next gaming architecture. Third, the "niche" academic users are currently hacking existing APIs; forcing a hardware tax on millions of gamers to serve thousands of academics is a misalignment of cost bearers and value receivers.

The "do nothing" alternative is superior: wait for software demand to prove willingness to pay, then introduce specialized SKUs or leverage existing shader models without bloating the mass-market silicon. The proposal fails the "fit" test; it sacrifices the core revenue engine (gaming) for a speculative future with no bridge to profitability. The pre-mortem is clear: the company loses the gaming war due to higher costs and distraction, goes bankrupt, and never reaches the computing boom it sought to enable.

This is a one-way door with irreversible capital allocation. The kill criterion is immediate: if there is no committed customer willing to subsidize the unit cost increase today, the broad rollout must not proceed. The proposal lacks a staged approach, such as a limited high-end SKU or a software-only beta, making the current "all-in" strategy reckless.

DECISION: NO-GO
