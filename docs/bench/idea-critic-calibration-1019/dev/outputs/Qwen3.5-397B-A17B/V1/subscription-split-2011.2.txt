The claim is that splitting the brand, billing, and data silos will liberate streaming while isolating the declining DVD business. The steelman: distinct P&Ls and brand identities allow aggressive streaming growth without being dragged down by legacy logistics costs or consumer associations with physical media.

However, the attack reveals a fatal flaw in timing and execution. The proposal follows immediately after a 60% price hike for combined services. Executing a full split now—forcing separate accounts, queues, and bills—compounds the friction. Customers who just absorbed a massive price increase now face increased complexity (two logins) and reduced value (lost cross-recommendations). This looks less like strategic clarity and more like a "double penalty" designed to push DVD users off the platform entirely.

The "better way" analysis shows that separating management internally does not require fracturing the user experience immediately. A unified login with distinct billing line items could achieve financial separation without the churn risk of forcing users to re-register and rebuild queues on a new domain. The loss of shared ratings destroys network effects that improve recommendations for both services, degrading the product for remaining users.

The pre-mortem is clear: announcing this split two months after a 60% hike triggers a perception of bad faith. Users will not see "strategic focus"; they will see a company breaking its service apart to hide declining metrics or force cancellations. The risk of mass churn outweighs the benefit of separate branding at this specific moment. The proposal fails the "fit" test because it ignores the immediate context of customer anger over pricing. A split might be valid eventually, but doing it now, in this manner, is self-sabotage. The lack of shared data is a permanent degradation of product quality that cannot be easily reversed if the split fails.

DECISION: NO-GO
