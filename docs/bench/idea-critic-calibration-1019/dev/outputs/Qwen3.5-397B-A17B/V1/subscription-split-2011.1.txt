The proposal attempts to solve a pricing backlash and strategic misalignment by forcibly separating two services customers expect to be unified. The core failure is treating a value perception problem (a 60% price hike) as a branding problem. Splitting the brand, accounts, and billing creates immediate, tangible friction (two logins, two bills) while offering zero compensatory value to the user. This validates the customer's fear that the company is abandoning them rather than serving them.

The "declining DVD" rationale is an internal operational preference, not a customer benefit. Severing data sharing destroys the cross-service recommendation engine, degrading the product experience for both segments. Historically, forcing users into fragmented ecosystems during a price increase accelerates churn rather than containing it. The proposal assumes customers will tolerate higher friction because the services are "different," ignoring that the combined convenience was the primary value proposition.

A better approach would maintain a single account and brand while offering tiered pricing or grandfathering existing users, addressing the revenue need without fracturing the user experience. The current plan guarantees confusion and perceived hostility. The risk of mass churn and reputational damage from a botched separation outweighs the potential operational agility gained by splitting the brands. The lack of a unified view prevents the company from managing the transition holistically.

The strongest attack—that this move signals abandonment and increases friction without adding value—survives because the proposal offers no mitigation for the lost convenience or the dual-billing headache. The "separate strategy" goal can be achieved internally without imposing external fragmentation on the customer. Proceeding as proposed invites a catastrophic loss of trust.

DECISION: NO-GO
