**Steelman:** Acquiring the startup secures a free, open mobile OS to commoditize handset software, forcing widespread adoption of mobile web browsing and locking in search advertising dominance before competitors establish closed ecosystems.

**Attack & Alternatives:**
The primary failure mode is carrier and handset maker resistance; they control distribution and currently profit from proprietary OS licensing or services. Without a guaranteed revenue share or hardware subsidy, they have zero incentive to pre-install an unproven, ad-centric OS. The $50M price tag for a six-person team with no revenue is speculative based entirely on the "if we build it, they will come" hypothesis.

*Alternative 1 (Do Nothing):* Wait for the market to mature. Risk: A competitor (e.g., Microsoft, Nokia) locks down the mobile search interface first.
*Alternative 2 (Partnership):* License the tech without full acquisition. Risk: The startup sells to a rival; lack of control over the roadmap.
*Alternative 3 (Internal Build):* Build an OS internally. Risk: Too slow, lacks the startup's secretive agility and open-source credibility.

**Pre-mortem:** In five years, the OS exists but is installed on <1% of devices because carriers blocked it to protect their own portals. The $50M is sunk, and the company remains dependent on WAP-based search which is dying.

**Kill Criteria & Evidence:**
There is no evidence of handset maker interest. The assumption that "open source" alone breaks carrier control is unproven in 2005 mobile markets. However, the cost of being locked out of the mobile future is existential. The $50M is a capped option price on the entire mobile internet. If the OS fails to gain traction within 3 years despite aggressive open-source promotion, the project must be pivoted to a pure application layer or abandoned.

**Verdict:**
The risk of inaction (missing the mobile shift) outweighs the financial loss of the acquisition. The open-source model is the only viable wedge against entrenched carriers. Proceed, but treat the OS as a loss-leader strictly measured by search query volume growth, not OS licensing revenue.

DECISION: GO
