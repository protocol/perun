The claim is that committing now to a 1997 hybrid launch creates a decisive first-mover advantage despite unproven technology and weak market signals. The steelman: seizing this window locks in regulatory goodwill and brand leadership before competitors react, turning today's technical risk into tomorrow's moat.

However, the attack reveals fatal flaws in the "bold public schedule" combined with "unproven" core components. Nickel-metal-hydride durability is unknown; a public deadline forces shipping immature batteries, guaranteeing massive warranty exposure and reputational damage if early units fail. Cheap fuel removes the economic incentive for consumers, meaning the company bears all cost premiums without a corresponding demand signal. No competitor has entered because the unit economics and technical readiness likely do not support volume production yet.

The "do nothing" alternative—continuing R&D without a fixed public launch date or mass-production commitment—offers a safer path. It allows the company to validate battery life cycles and wait for fuel prices or regulations to shift demand, avoiding the catastrophe of a recalled flagship product. A "go-if" approach tied to specific durability milestones would be prudent, but the proposal demands an unconditional commitment to a calendar date regardless of technical validation.

The pre-mortem is clear: by 1998, the cars are in the field, batteries degrade rapidly, warranty costs bankrupt the division, and the brand becomes synonymous with unreliable technology. The one-way door nature of a public corporate program makes retreat impossible once announced. The cost of delay is negligible compared to the cost of failure; waiting a quarter to prove battery life does not lose the market, but shipping a defective product loses the future.

This proposal fails the calibration test. The lack of a verified durability metric, the absence of a demand signal, and the rigidity of the public schedule create a concrete failure scenario that the plan does not mitigate. It bets the corporation on a hope rather than a verified engineering state.

DECISION: NO-GO
