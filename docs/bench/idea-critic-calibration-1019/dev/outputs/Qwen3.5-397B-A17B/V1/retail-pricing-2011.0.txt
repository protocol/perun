The claim is that replacing a coupon-driven model with everyday low pricing (EDLP) and branded shops will reverse falling sales for a mid-market department store whose customers are historically trained to wait for discounts.

**Skeptic:** The core assumption is that the existing customer base values "fair" everyday prices over the psychological reward of perceived savings via coupons. For a demographic described as "older" and "coupon-driven," this behavior is likely deeply entrenched, not a reaction to complexity. Removing the coupon mechanism removes the primary trigger for their purchases. The proposal treats the customer base as interchangeable with that of a successful electronics chain, ignoring that electronics buyers often seek convenience and fixed margins, whereas this demographic seeks the "hunt" for a deal.

**Better-way:** The "do nothing" baseline involves optimizing the current coupon model rather than discarding it. A superior alternative is a hybrid approach: maintain the coupon framework but simplify the rules, or run a controlled pilot in 5-10 stores to measure elasticity before a chain-wide rollout. The cost of a full chain rebuild without data is catastrophic if the hypothesis fails.

**Kill-criteria:** The pre-mortem is clear: if customers stop buying because they no longer feel they are "saving" money, revenue will collapse immediately. There is no reversibility once the brand identity shifts and physical stores are rebuilt; the "one-way door" has been kicked open without checking if anyone is walking through it. The lack of a pilot is a fatal flaw. The proposal ignores the specific constraint that most sales historically come from discounted items. To proceed without validating whether these specific customers will accept EDLP is negligence.

The strongest attack is the behavioral mismatch: the plan solves for a customer who wants simplicity, but the revenue comes from customers who want discounts. Without a pilot to prove these customers will adapt, the risk of total revenue collapse is unmitigated. The evidence provided (historical reliance on discounts) directly contradicts the strategy's core premise.

DECISION: NO-GO
