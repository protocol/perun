The claim is that adding a low-quality, limited streaming tier in 2007 protects a dominant DVD business against future disruption. The steelman: this is a low-cost defensive experiment to retain subscribers and gather data before incumbents dominate, treating streaming as a feature rather than a replacement.

However, the "no extra charge" model creates immediate negative unit economics. Licensing costs are high while the catalog is poor; adding this cost to seven million subscribers without incremental revenue destroys margin on every user who activates it. The "cannibalization" risk is not theoretical; if the streaming experience is genuinely poor (low quality, old titles), it does not retain customers—it actively degrades the brand value of the core profitable service. Users paying for premium DVD service may churn if they perceive the company pivoting to a inferior product, even if free.

The "better way" is a separate, paid tier or a strictly opt-in beta with a capped user base to validate technology and licensing models without exposing the entire profit engine to immediate cost inflation. Doing nothing preserves the cash cow while competitors burn capital testing the market; entering now with a subpar product signals weakness, not innovation. The cost of delay is minimal because broadband penetration and content libraries are not yet ready for mass adoption; being first with a bad product is worse than being second with a good one.

The fatal flaw is the economic structure: unlimited free access to high-cost licensed content with no revenue offset creates an uncapped liability on a loss-leading feature. This fails the "do no harm" test for the core business. The proposal lacks a kill criterion for activation rates or cost-per-stream, and the "free" model prevents learning true price elasticity.

DECISION: NO-GO
