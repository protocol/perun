The claim is that acquiring a struggling handset manufacturer for €5.4 billion will secure the ecosystem and prevent defection to a rival. The steelman: vertical integration is the only remaining lever to force hardware-software alignment and signal commitment to developers when organic growth has stalled.

However, the attack reveals fatal flaws. First, the premise that hardware ownership fixes an app-ecosystem deficit is unproven; owning a shrinking device base does not automatically attract developers who follow the market leader's volume. Second, the target's sales are already falling; buying a depreciating asset at a premium creates a value trap rather than a turnaround platform. Third, the risk of alienating other handset makers is extreme: if they perceive the OS owner as a direct competitor, they may accelerate their own exit to the rival platform, collapsing the very ecosystem this deal aims to save.

The "do nothing" alternative preserves cash and avoids integrating a failing workforce, though it risks the manufacturer eventually switching. A better way might be a tightly scoped exclusive partnership or subsidy without the burden of full ownership and legacy staff.

The pre-mortem is clear: one year post-acquisition, the company is burdened with massive write-downs, other partners have fled to the rival, and the app gap remains unchanged because total market share did not increase. This is a one-way door with high irreversibility; once the cash is spent and competitors alienated, retreat is catastrophic. The cost of delay is low compared to the cost of a failed integration that accelerates ecosystem collapse.

The evidence provided—falling sales, dominant rival ecosystem, and specific integration risks—suggests the acquisition addresses a symptom (potential defection) while ignoring the disease (lack of developer demand). Without a concrete mechanism to reverse the sales decline or guarantee partner retention, the €5.4 billion expenditure is unjustified.

DECISION: NO-GO
