**Steelman:** Committing now secures a decade-long lead in electrification, forcing competitors to chase while establishing the brand as the definitive eco-technology leader before regulations mandate it.

**Attack:** The proposal fails the "do nothing" test. With cheap fuel and zero regulatory pressure, the market signal is absent. The core technology (NiMH durability, novel transmission) is unproven at volume, creating massive warranty liability without a price premium customers are willing to pay. A bold public schedule locks the company into a fixed deadline for a high-risk R&D phase, inviting catastrophic reputation damage if delays occur (which they will). The cost of delay is negligible; the cost of failure is existential.

**Better Way:** Run a limited fleet pilot (taxis/government) to validate durability and gather real-world data without a public mass-production commitment. Delay the mass-market announcement until battery cycle life is proven and fuel prices or regulations shift the economic calculus.

**Fit:** This moves the north star of "innovation" but ignores the north star of "profitability" and "brand reliability." It bets the farm on a future market state that is not guaranteed.

**Pre-mortem:** By 1998, the car is delayed, costs are double, early units suffer battery failures, and the public narrative shifts from "pioneer" to "reckless." The stock tanks, and the program is canceled, having burned billions.

**Kill Criteria:** Without a binding regulatory mandate or a fuel price spike, the business case relies entirely on speculation. The lack of a near-term demand signal is a fatal flaw for a mass-production commitment. The risk of warranty exposure on unproven batteries is uninsurable.

**Verdict:** This is a one-way door with no safety net. The proposal demands a binary go/no-go on unproven tech for a non-existent market. The prudent path is staged validation, not a corporate-level mass-production pledge.

DECISION: NO-GO
