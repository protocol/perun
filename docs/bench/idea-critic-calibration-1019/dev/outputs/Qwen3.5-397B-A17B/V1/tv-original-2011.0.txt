**Steelman:** Leveraging proprietary viewing data to bypass the traditional pilot model and commission a high-profile original series directly secures exclusive content, differentiates the brand from licensed-content competitors, and validates a new "all-at-once" distribution model that aligns with emerging on-demand behaviors.

**Attack & Alternatives:**
The primary failure mode is financial catastrophe: spending a massive portion of the content budget on an unproven format without a pilot safety net. If the show fails, the service lacks the capital to recover, potentially threatening solvency. The "do nothing" alternative (continuing to license content) avoids this specific risk but accepts rising costs and lack of differentiation. A safer alternative would be a single-season order or a traditional pilot, but these dilute the strategic signal and may not attract top-tier talent willing to commit only to a bold, full-series vision.

**Fit & Pre-mortem:**
This move shifts the company from a distributor to a studio, a necessary evolution given rising licensing fees. However, assuming viewing data translates perfectly to long-form narrative engagement is a dangerous leap. Data shows what people watch, not necessarily what they will pay for exclusively in a new format. The "all-at-once" release risks burning through content quickly without sustaining monthly subscription retention compared to weekly serialization.

**Evidence & Calibration:**
The proposal relies entirely on internal viewing data as proof of concept. There is no external market validation for straight-to-series prestige drama or binge-releasing. The risk is one-way door: once the money is spent, it cannot be recovered. The cost concentration is extreme; a single failure could cripple future production capabilities.

**Verdict:**
While the strategic logic is sound, the execution plan lacks sufficient guardrails. Committing two full seasons (26 episodes) without a pilot or a phased single-season approach concentrates too much risk on a single unproven hypothesis. The "no pilot" decision removes the industry-standard circuit breaker. The scale (two seasons) exceeds what is necessary to test the hypothesis. This is a fatal flaw in risk management; the ambition outpaces the de-risking mechanism. The proposal must be revised to a single-season order to maintain a viable kill criterion before committing half the budget.

DECISION: NO-GO
