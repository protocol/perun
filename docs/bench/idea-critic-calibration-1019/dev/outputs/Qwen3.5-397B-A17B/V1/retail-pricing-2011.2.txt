The claim is that replacing a coupon-driven model with everyday low pricing and branded shops will reverse sales declines for a mid-market department store chain reliant on discounts.

**Steelman:** Eliminating promotional complexity reduces operational costs and attracts a broader demographic, while branded shops modernize the experience, replicating a successful electronics retail strategy.

**Attack:** The proposal ignores the specific behavioral conditioning of the existing customer base, who historically purchase *only* when discounts are applied. Removing coupons effectively raises prices for 100% of the core revenue stream immediately. Furthermore, the strategy copies a playbook from branded electronics (high differentiation, tech-focused) to general merchandise (high substitutability, price-sensitive), a category mismatch. The decision to roll out chain-wide without a pilot removes the ability to measure elasticity or adjust before catastrophic revenue loss. The "do nothing" or "staged pilot" alternatives dominate this approach on risk and reversibility; a full rollout is a one-way door with immediate cash flow implications.

**Pre-mortem:** Six months post-implementation, sales have collapsed by 30%. Loyal customers stopped buying because the perceived value vanished without coupons, and new customers did not arrive fast enough to replace them because the brand lacks the unique pull of the electronics competitor. The capital spent on rebuilding stores cannot be recovered, and the chain faces liquidity issues.

**Fit & Evidence:** The proposal assumes the electronics CEO's past success is transferable without verifying if the customer psychographics align. There is no evidence provided that the department store's inventory supports a "branded shop" model or that the supply chain can sustain everyday low margins without the volume spikes driven by promotions.

**Kill Criteria:** This plan lacks a defined metric to stop the bleed. A fatal flaw exists: launching a price-increase strategy (via coupon removal) on a price-sensitive demographic without a test violates basic retail experimentation principles. The risk of immediate insolvency outweighs the potential long-term brand benefits. The correct path is a limited market pilot to validate price elasticity before committing the entire chain.

DECISION: NO-GO
