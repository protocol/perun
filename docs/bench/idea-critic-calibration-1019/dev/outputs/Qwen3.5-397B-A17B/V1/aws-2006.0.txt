The claim is that a retailer should launch an unproven, capital-intensive cloud computing business despite lacking relevant experience, facing entrenched competitors, and enduring investor skepticism regarding capital spending.

Steelman: The company's massive, spiky internal infrastructure represents sunk capital that could be monetized during idle periods, creating a high-margin revenue stream that leverages existing assets rather than requiring entirely new build-outs, eventually surpassing the low-margin retail core.

However, the failure hypothesis is strong: this is a classic "distraction" trap. The proposal admits no one has run this business at scale. Reliability expectations for external developers are orders of magnitude higher than internal retail needs; a single outage could destroy reputation before revenue scales. The market is explicitly "unproven," meaning the company is betting billions on a hypothesis without a pilot.

Better ways exist: Do nothing and optimize internal costs. Alternatively, license the internal tooling to a single partner rather than building a public platform, reducing risk and capital exposure. A "do nothing" stance preserves capital for the core retail business, which investors are already questioning.

Fit: This contradicts the immediate need to reassure investors about capital discipline. It pivots from retail to infrastructure, a completely different operational model with different unit economics and support requirements.

Pre-mortem: Assume it failed in 18 months. Why? Because the engineering culture focused on retail velocity could not maintain the "undifferentiated heavy lifting" reliability required by enterprise clients, leading to churn. The capital spend ballooned, diluting retail margins, and the stock collapsed as the core business suffered from resource starvation.

Evidence: The proposal itself cites "no experience," "unproven market," and "investor questions." These are fatal flaws in the current context. There is no kill criterion or staged pilot proposed; it is a binary bet on a new industry.

The strongest attack is the lack of product-market fit validation combined with the operational mismatch. Selling reliability requires a culture the retailer does not have. Without a small-scale experiment to prove demand and operational capability, this is gambling, not strategy.

DECISION: NO-GO
