The claim is that a $699 Wi-Fi-connected juicer with a proprietary subscription model will succeed in the 2016 consumer market. The steelman: this creates a high-margin recurring revenue stream by locking users into a convenient, fresh-pressed ecosystem that justifies the hardware subsidy over time.

However, the attack reveals fatal flaws in unit economics and consumer behavior. First, the "razor-and-blade" model fails if the blade is too expensive; at $699, the device price exceeds the threshold for mass-market impulse buys, yet likely doesn't cover the cost of custom engineering and connectivity, leaving little room to subsidize the hardware via bag margins without making the per-juice cost prohibitive compared to fresh produce. Second, requiring internet connectivity for a countertop appliance introduces unnecessary friction and failure points (Wi-Fi drops, server outages) for a core mechanical function, alienating non-technical users. Third, the market for single-use, pre-packed produce bags is niche; most consumers prefer selecting their own fruit for freshness and cost, making the "proprietary" aspect a barrier rather than a benefit. Finally, the "app-tied freshness check" solves a problem that doesn't exist for most users, adding complexity without tangible value.

The "do nothing" alternative (buying a standard juicer and fresh fruit) is significantly cheaper, more flexible, and reliable. A cheaper, simpler path would be a non-connected device using standard containers, but the proposal insists on a locked ecosystem. The cost of delay is low; waiting allows competitors to test the subscription model with cheaper hardware first. The pre-mortem shows failure via low attach rates: users buy the machine, find the bags too expensive, and revert to manual juicing, leaving the company with unsold inventory and high churn. The kill criterion is clear: if the subscription cost per ounce exceeds 2x the market rate of fresh produce, the value proposition collapses. Given the high upfront cost, forced connectivity, and unproven demand for proprietary bags, the risk of total market rejection is extreme.

DECISION: NO-GO
