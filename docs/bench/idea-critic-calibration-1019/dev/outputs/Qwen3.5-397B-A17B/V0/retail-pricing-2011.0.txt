The claim is that replacing a coupon-driven model with everyday low pricing and branded shops will reverse falling sales for a mid-market department store chain. The steelman: this eliminates margin-eroding discounts and modernizes the brand by emulating a successful electronics retail format.

However, the attack reveals fatal flaws in the premises. First, the customer base is explicitly "older" and "coupon-driven," with most sales historically occurring at a discount. These customers are trained to wait for promotions; removing them does not shift behavior, it removes the purchase trigger. Second, the proposal imports a strategy from "branded electronics stores" to a "department store" context without accounting for category differences in purchase frequency and brand loyalty. Third, the plan is announced "without a test" and rolls out to the "entire chain at once." This is a one-way door executed with zero validation.

The "do nothing" alternative retains the known, albeit declining, revenue stream while allowing for targeted testing. The "cheaper alternative" is a pilot in 5–10 stores to measure elasticity before a national reset. The cost of delay is minimal compared to the risk of immediate, systemic revenue collapse if the core hypothesis (that customers will pay full price) is false.

The pre-mortem is clear: six months post-launch, sales plummet because the core demographic stops visiting, and the new "branded shop" aesthetic fails to attract a younger demographic quickly enough to offset the loss. The irreversible nature of a full-chain rebuild, combined with the lack of a pilot, violates basic decision hygiene for a company already in distress.

There is no evidence provided that the new CEO's past success in electronics translates to apparel/home goods, nor that the specific customer base will tolerate the shift. The risks (alienating the entire existing base, no pilot, total chain exposure) vastly outweigh the unproven benefits.

DECISION: NO-GO
