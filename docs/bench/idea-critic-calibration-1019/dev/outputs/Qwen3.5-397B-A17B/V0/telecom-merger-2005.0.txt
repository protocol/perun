The claim is that merging with a smaller carrier possessing a popular push-to-talk service justifies a $36 billion expenditure despite severe technological incompatibility. The steelman posits that acquiring the high-value business customer base immediately outweighs the massive integration costs and legacy debt, securing a dominant enterprise position before competitors can react.

However, the skeptic identifies a fatal flaw: the networks use different, incompatible technologies. In 2005, migrating a specialized, real-time service like push-to-talk across incompatible infrastructure is not merely an "integration cost"; it is a high-probability service failure scenario. If the service degrades during migration, the primary asset—the loyal business customer base—vanishes, leaving the acquirer with $36 billion in goodwill and no revenue to support it. The "better way" analysis suggests that building or licensing a compatible push-to-talk solution on the buyer's existing network would capture similar value without the existential risk of merging two disparate physical layers. The cost of delay in building organically is negligible compared to the risk of destroying the target's core value proposition through botched integration.

The pre-mortem reveals a likely outcome: the integration drags on, the legacy network requires expensive maintenance while generating declining returns, and business customers churn due to service instability, triggering a massive goodwill write-down. The kill criteria for this deal should have been a proven, low-risk migration path for the specific push-to-talk technology, which is absent here. The evidence provided highlights "incompatible technologies" and "limited data" capability on the target network, suggesting the asset is technically obsolete relative to the buyer's future state. Paying a premium for a legacy technology that must be dismantled to be integrated is economically unsound. The risk of service disruption during the transition poses an unacceptable threat to the very customer relationships being purchased. Without a verified, low-friction technical merger plan, the financial exposure vastly exceeds the strategic benefit.

DECISION: NO-GO
