The claim is that launching a new mid-priced division in 1957 will capture growth and fill a portfolio gap. The steelman: a dedicated brand prevents cannibalization while targeting a proven, expanding demographic with tailored styling.

However, the attack reveals fatal flaws in the current plan. First, the timing relies on a growing segment that faces imminent recession risk; launching a capital-intensive dealer network right before a downturn creates unsustainable fixed costs when consumers trade down. Second, "extensive research" shaped styling, not economic resilience; motivation studies do not predict macroeconomic contractions. Third, the requirement for a *new* dealer network multiplies the burn rate before a single unit is sold, ignoring the cheaper alternative of utilizing existing sales channels or a sub-brand strategy to share infrastructure.

The pre-mortem shows the division failing because the recession hits during the ramp-up phase, leaving isolated dealers with high overhead and shrinking demand. The cost of delay is low compared to the cost of failure; waiting for economic clarity or using a shared-network approach reduces exposure. The proposal treats a cyclical risk as a permanent opportunity.

To survive, the plan must prove the segment is recession-proof (unlikely for mid-priced autos) or decouple the launch from heavy infrastructure spend. As proposed, with a new dealer network and a 1957 debut coinciding with recession signals, the financial exposure is unchecked. The kill criterion is clear: if a recession begins before the network breaks even, the division collapses. Given the explicit recession risk and the high fixed cost of a new dealer network, the current path is a bet against the macroeconomy.

The strongest attack survived is the fear of missing the growth window, but this is outweighed by the certainty of high fixed costs meeting low demand in a downturn. The plan lacks a "go-if" condition tied to economic indicators or a lower-cost entry method.

DECISION: NO-GO
