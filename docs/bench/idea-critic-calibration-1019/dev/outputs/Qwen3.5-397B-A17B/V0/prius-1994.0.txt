Steelman: Committing now secures first-mover dominance in a future regulated, efficiency-focused market before competitors react. However, the proposal fails the "fit" and "evidence" gates. With fuel cheap and no regulatory mandate, the customer value proposition is weak; the "north star" of volume sales contradicts the lack of near-term demand signals. The plan bets the corporation on unproven battery durability and a novel transmission without a pilot phase to validate warranty exposure.

The "do nothing" alternative preserves capital while monitoring regulatory shifts. A "wait-and-see" approach allows competitors to absorb the R&D risk of early battery failures. The cost of delay is minimal given the absence of mandates, whereas the cost of failure (massive recalls, brand damage from unreliable tech) is catastrophic. The proposal treats a one-way door (mass production commitment) as reversible.

Pre-mortem: By 1998, the vehicles suffer widespread battery degradation in real-world conditions. Warranty claims exceed the program's total budget, forcing a halt in sales and a stock price collapse. The "bold public schedule" prevents a quiet pivot, locking the firm into a failing narrative. The kill criteria are missing: there is no defined metric for battery cycle life that must be hit before tooling begins, nor a cap on acceptable warranty costs.

The strongest attack is the economic mismatch: selling a premium, complex technology in a market that values low upfront cost and where the primary benefit (fuel savings) has no financial urgency for the consumer. Without a regulatory stick or a fuel price spike, the unit economics do not close. The proposal asks to scale before proving the core mechanism (battery life) or the market need.

DECISION: NO-GO
