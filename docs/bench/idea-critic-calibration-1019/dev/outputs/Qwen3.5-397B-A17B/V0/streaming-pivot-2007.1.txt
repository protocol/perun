The claim is that adding a free, limited streaming tier in 2007 protects a profitable DVD monopoly against future disruption. The steelman: this is a low-cost insurance policy to retain subscribers and gather data before giants dominate, accepting short-term margin pressure for long-term survival.

However, the skeptic identifies fatal flaws in the specific execution proposed. Offering streaming at **no extra charge** with a **small, old catalogue** and **poor quality** creates a negative value signal. It trains users to view the brand as "legacy" rather than "future," accelerating cannibalization of the high-margin DVD core without providing a compelling reason to stay. If the experience is worse than the current service, it degrades the brand promise. Furthermore, paying high licensing fees for a product designed to be inferior invites scrutiny from studios who may see this as a threat to their own emerging digital windows, potentially triggering rights revocation.

The better-way analysis suggests a "go-if" approach rather than a blanket launch. A paid add-on or a beta limited to specific demographics would validate willingness to pay and test infrastructure costs without exposing the entire subscriber base to a sub-par experience. Doing nothing is also a valid alternative if the unit economics of licensing old movies exceed the retention value.

The pre-mortem reveals that by the time broadband is universal, the company will have burned cash on licenses for a technology stack that big tech entrants will undercut with superior content and infrastructure. The "free" model destroys the ability to monetize the transition.

Kill criteria are absent. There is no threshold for cancellation if cannibalization exceeds a specific percentage, nor a metric for streaming engagement that justifies the licensing spend. Without a cap on losses or a clear pivot point, this is an uncapped bet on a hypothesis that the current execution actively undermines. The proposal fails to prove that a free, low-quality service prevents churn better than simply waiting to launch a competitive product later.

DECISION: NO-GO
