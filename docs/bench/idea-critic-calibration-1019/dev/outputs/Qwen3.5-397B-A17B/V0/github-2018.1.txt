Steelman: Acquiring the dominant code-hosting platform secures the industry's central nervous system, preventing a competitor from locking it down and aligning the buyer's cloud future with where developers actually work.

However, the claim fails the "fit" and "evidence" tests under the provided constraints. The target is loss-making with no permanent CEO, indicating operational instability that a $7.5 billion price tag exacerbates rather than solves. The core asset is a vocal community defined by active distrust of the buyer; history shows that acquiring a culture that hates you often results in value destruction through defection, not synergy. The existence of a free, viable alternative creates a specific, immediate kill criterion: if even 15-20% of high-value users migrate upon announcement, the network effect—the sole justification for the premium valuation—collapses.

The proposal assumes independence will neutralize cultural friction, but this is an unsupported assertion given the buyer's historical hostility. Without a verified retention plan or a named leader to bridge the trust gap, the risk of developer exodus is not a "maybe"; it is the primary failure mode. The price assumes current dominance persists post-acquisition, yet the very act of acquisition triggers the conditions (distrust, alternative availability) that undermine that dominance.

A "do nothing" or "partner" approach avoids the $7.5 billion downside while monitoring the target's struggle without a CEO. The cost of delay is low compared to the cost of buying a burning house at peak market rates. The pre-mortem is clear: the community forks or migrates, the platform becomes a ghost town, and the buyer writes down billions. No kill criteria or leading metrics are provided to manage this specific cultural and operational risk, only vague promises of independence.

The strongest attack survived is the strategic necessity of owning the layer; however, without a concrete mechanism to stop the bleeding of trust, the financial and reputational exposure outweighs the strategic optionality. The lack of a permanent CEO at the target suggests internal chaos that the buyer is ill-equipped to fix via checkbook alone.

DECISION: NO-GO
