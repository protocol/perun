The proposal presents a catastrophic misalignment between the new strategy and the established customer base. The core revenue driver for this chain is historically discount-driven; the majority of sales occur only when items are marked down. The existing clientele has been conditioned over years to wait for coupons and promotions. Abruptly eliminating this mechanism removes the primary incentive for these customers to purchase.

Furthermore, the leadership appointment introduces a critical domain mismatch. Success in branded electronics retail does not translate to mid-market department store dynamics, where product categories, margins, and consumer psychology differ significantly. The proposed shift to "everyday pricing" assumes a brand equity and customer loyalty that the data suggests does not exist. Without a strong emotional connection to the brand itself, customers will simply migrate to competitors who continue to offer the discounts they expect.

The execution plan exacerbates these fundamental flaws. Launching a total system overhaul across the entire chain without a pilot program is reckless. A test in a limited number of stores would have provided essential data on customer attrition rates and revenue impact before committing the whole organization. By skipping this step, the company exposes itself to immediate, unrecoverable financial damage. If the hypothesis is wrong, there is no off-ramp; the brand reputation will be shattered, and inventory will stagnate as full-price goods remain unsold.

The risks identified—alienating coupon-trained customers, lacking a pilot, and the speed of total implementation—are not merely concerns; they are predictors of failure. The strategy ignores the behavioral reality of the shopper in favor of a theoretical model that has not been validated in this specific context. The potential for a rapid, steep decline in sales is too high to justify proceeding. Immediate rejection is required to prevent irreversible harm to the company's viability.

DECISION: NO-GO
