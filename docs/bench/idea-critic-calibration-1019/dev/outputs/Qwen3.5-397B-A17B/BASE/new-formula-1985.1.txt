The data indicates a clear preference for the sweeter formula in controlled environments, suggesting the new product is objectively superior in blind taste scenarios. However, the proposal contains a critical strategic flaw: the complete withdrawal of the original formula. While taste tests validate the flavor profile, they failed to simulate the psychological impact of removing a century-old cultural icon. The research explicitly omitted informing participants that the original would disappear, rendering the results incomplete regarding consumer reaction to loss.

The identified risks of emotional attachment and brand heritage are not merely theoretical; they represent the core value of the brand beyond liquid content. By eliminating the original product entirely, the company transforms a product upgrade into an act of dispossession for its most loyal customers. This approach ignores the distinction between what consumers say they prefer in a sip test versus what they value in their daily lives and traditions.

Furthermore, the lack of a fallback product creates an unnecessary binary choice. If the new formula underperforms in the real market due to backlash, there is no immediate mechanism to retain customers who reject the change. The potential alienation of loyal bottlers and consumers could cause irreversible damage to market share, potentially driving customers to competitors not because their product tastes better, but because this brand discarded its heritage.

Approving this plan as written gambles the entire brand equity on incomplete data. The failure to test the specific scenario of removal invalidates the confidence placed in the focus groups. A successful launch requires offering choice, not enforcing it. The strategy must be revised to include the continued availability of the original formula alongside the new option to mitigate the severe risks of consumer revolt and brand erosion. Without this safeguard, the probability of a catastrophic market reaction is too high to proceed.

DECISION: NO-GO
