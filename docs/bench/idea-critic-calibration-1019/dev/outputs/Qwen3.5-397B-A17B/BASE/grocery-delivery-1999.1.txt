The proposal demands an immediate commitment of $1 billion to build highly automated warehouses and a truck fleet across a dozen new cities. This occurs while the company has operated in only one metropolitan area since June 1999. Crucially, the plan explicitly bypasses learning the unit economics of the initial market before expanding.

Grocery delivery is inherently a low-margin business. Success relies heavily on achieving high customer density to make the logistics of 30-minute delivery slots economically viable. The proposal acknowledges significant risks: extreme capital intensity, thin margins, and the absolute necessity of customer density. Yet, the strategy ignores these constraints by scaling rapidly without an operating track record or proven profitability models.

Investing such a massive sum before validating the core business model in the first city is financially reckless. If the unit economics in the initial market prove unfavorable due to the costs of rapid delivery and low margins, replicating this failure across a dozen cities will magnify losses exponentially. The lack of data on customer retention, order frequency, and actual delivery costs makes any projection for new cities purely speculative.

Furthermore, building very large, highly automated facilities locks the company into a rigid cost structure before understanding local demand variations. Should the market response be slower than anticipated, the company will be burdened with immense fixed costs and debt service obligations that low grocery margins cannot support. The venture capital raised, while large, is not infinite; burning it on unproven expansion leaves no runway for correction.

The prudent path requires validating the model in the first city, optimizing operations, and proving that the 30-minute delivery promise can be fulfilled profitably. Only then should expansion be considered, and even then, it should be gradual. Approving this proposal now invites catastrophic financial failure by prioritizing speed over sustainability in a sector that punishes inefficiency severely.

DECISION: NO-GO
