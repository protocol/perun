The proposal presents a high-stakes gamble with insufficient grounding in current market realities. Committing to mass production by 1997 requires assuming that unproven technologies will mature instantly without costly delays. The nickel-metal-hydride battery pack lacks a track record for durability in volume vehicles; failure rates could trigger catastrophic warranty costs that outweigh any brand equity gained. Furthermore, the novel power-split transmission introduces complex manufacturing challenges that have not been stress-tested at scale.

Market conditions strongly argue against this timeline. With fuel prices low in the primary export market, consumers have no financial incentive to pay the premium likely required for a hybrid vehicle. Without regulatory mandates forcing efficiency or competitors validating the segment, there is no immediate demand signal to absorb the initial production run. Launching a bold public schedule creates a fixed target that ignores the high probability of technical setbacks. If the battery fails or costs spiral, the public commitment turns a manageable R&D project into a reputational crisis.

The absence of near-term demand suggests that early units will sit unsold or require heavy subsidization, eroding margins. While being first offers potential marketing advantages, the risks of reliability issues and unproven supply chains outweigh the benefits of speed. A more prudent approach involves limited pilot programs to validate battery life and transmission reliability before locking in mass production tooling and public deadlines. Proceeding now exposes the corporation to unacceptable financial liability and brand damage based on assumptions rather than data. The technology is too nascent, and the market too indifferent, to justify a full-scale commitment at this specific moment.

DECISION: NO-GO
