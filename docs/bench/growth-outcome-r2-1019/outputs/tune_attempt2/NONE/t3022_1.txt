First, I check for Sample Ratio Mismatch (SRM). The total users are 44,261 (control) + 44,379 (treatment) = 88,640. The expected split is 44,320 per arm. The difference is only 59 users, which is well within random chance for this sample size. No SRM.

Next, I check for Denominator errors (DENOM). The metric is "order conversion rate." The readout provides orders and users. Calculating the rates: Control = 2,656 / 44,261 ≈ 6.001%. Treatment = 2,716 / 44,379 ≈ 6.120%. These match the reported percentages exactly. The denominator (users) matches the randomization unit (user id). No DENOM error.

Now, I check for Simpson's Paradox (SIMPSON). I calculate the conversion rates by device segment:
Desktop Control: 1,679 / 17,733 ≈ 9.47%
Desktop Treatment: 1,850 / 20,151 ≈ 9.18%
(Lift is negative: treatment is worse).

Mobile Control: 977 / 26,528 ≈ 3.68%
Mobile Treatment: 866 / 24,228 ≈ 3.57%
(Lift is negative: treatment is worse).

In both segments, the treatment performs worse than the control. However, the pooled result reports a positive lift (6.12% vs 6.00%). This reversal of direction between segments and the aggregate is the definition of Simpson's Paradox. This likely occurred because the treatment group had a higher proportion of desktop users (who convert at a higher base rate) than the control group, skewing the aggregate despite the treatment failing in both specific contexts.

Regarding Power (POWER): The test ran for 21 days as planned (no PEEK). The sample size (~88k) is sufficient to detect an 8% lift on a 6% baseline with 80% power. While the observed lift (2%) is smaller than the target (8%) and the p-value (0.4569) indicates the result is not statistically significant, this is a result of the experiment, not a design flaw like being "underpowered" to detect the *target* if it existed. The primary error is the misleading aggregation.

The daily lift shows no obvious fading trend (NOVELTY), fluctuating between 1.5% and 2.5%.

Because the aggregate positive lift is entirely driven by a mix of user types (Simpson's Paradox) while the treatment actually hurts conversion in both desktop and mobile segments, rolling this out would likely decrease overall orders. The analysis is fundamentally flawed.

ERRORS: SIMPSON
DECISION: HOLD
