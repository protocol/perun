First, I check for Sample Ratio Mismatch (SRM). The total users are 67,449 (control) + 66,099 (treatment) = 133,548. The expected split is 50%, so 66,774 per arm. The difference is roughly 675 users. While slightly uneven, this is likely within chance for this sample size and does not strongly indicate a broken assignment mechanism without a specific p-value for the split, but let's look closer at the segments.

Next, I check for Simpson's Paradox (SIMPSON).
Control Conversion Rates:
- Desktop: 1,996 / 31,282 ≈ 6.38%
- Mobile: 702 / 36,167 ≈ 1.94%
- Pooled: 2,698 / 67,449 ≈ 4.00%

Treatment Conversion Rates:
- Desktop: 2,090 / 33,790 ≈ 6.19%
- Mobile: 608 / 32,309 ≈ 1.88%
- Pooled: 2,698 / 66,099 ≈ 4.08%

In the pooled data, Treatment (4.08%) > Control (4.00%).
However, looking at the segments:
- Desktop: Control (6.38%) > Treatment (6.19%).
- Mobile: Control (1.94%) > Treatment (1.88%).

The treatment performs worse in both specific device segments but appears better when aggregated. This is a classic reversal caused by the treatment arm having a higher proportion of high-converting desktop users (33,790/66,099 ≈ 51.1%) compared to the control arm (31,282/67,449 ≈ 46.4%). The aggregation masks the negative effect. This confirms SIMPSON.

Regarding POWER: The reported p-value is 0.4484, which is not significant. The test ran for the planned 28 days (March 30 to April 27). There is no evidence of early stopping (PEEK). The daily lift is stable around 2%, showing no fading trend that would suggest a novelty effect disappearing (NOVELTY), though the effect isn't significant anyway. The metric denominator (users) matches the randomization unit (users), so no DENOM error. The primary issue is the misleading aggregate result driven by segment imbalance.

ERRORS: SIMPSON
DECISION: HOLD
