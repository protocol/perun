First, I check for Sample Ratio Mismatch (SRM). The total user count is 44,288 (control) + 45,356 (treatment) = 89,644. The expected split is 50%, so 44,822 per arm. The treatment arm has 534 more users than expected. The standard deviation for a binomial distribution here is $\sqrt{89644 \times 0.5 \times 0.5} \approx 150$. The difference is $534 / 150 \approx 3.56$ standard deviations. This exceeds the typical threshold of 2 (or even 3) for chance variation, indicating a significant SRM. Assignment or logging is likely broken.

Next, I check for Simpson's Paradox (SIMPSON). I calculate the conversion rates per device segment:
Desktop Control: $1,536 / 15,518 \approx 9.90\%$.
Desktop Treatment: $1,747 / 18,183 \approx 9.61\%$. (Treatment is lower).
Mobile Control: $1,122 / 28,770 \approx 3.90\%$.
Mobile Treatment: $1,028 / 27,173 \approx 3.78\%$. (Treatment is lower).
In both segments, the treatment performs worse than the control. However, the aggregated reported result claims the treatment (6.118%) is higher than the control (6.002%). Since the direction of the effect reverses when pooling the data, this is a clear case of Simpson's Paradox, likely driven by the unequal distribution of high-converting desktop users (who are overrepresented in the treatment arm due to the SRM).

Regarding Power (POWER): The test ran for 28 days as planned, and the sample size (~45k per arm) is generally sufficient to detect an 8% lift on a 6% baseline, though the observed lift was small. The primary issue isn't underpowering but the validity of the data itself.

Regarding PEEK, NOVELTY, and DENOM: The test ran the full planned duration (May 30 to June 27 is 28 days), so no peeking. The metric denominator (users) matches the randomization unit. While daily lift fades (3.6% down to 1.0%), the reported p-value is 0.46 (not significant), so projecting a non-significant result is flawed, but the structural data errors (SRM and SIMPSON) are the definitive blockers.

The SRM invalidates the randomization assumption, and the Simpson's paradox shows the aggregate metric is misleading compared to the segment-level reality (where treatment actually hurts conversion).
