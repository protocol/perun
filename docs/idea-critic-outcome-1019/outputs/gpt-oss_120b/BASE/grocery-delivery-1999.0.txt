The startup is entering a sector—grocery delivery—characterized by low margins and heavy reliance on high order density to achieve profitability. Its only operational data come from a single metropolitan launch in June 1999, providing no validated unit‑economics for the model. The proposal calls for a $1 billion outlay to build large, highly automated warehouses in more than a dozen cities and to acquire a fleet of delivery trucks before those economics are known.

Key concerns:

* **Capital intensity** – The scale of the proposed investment dwarfs the modest revenue base expected from a nascent, low‑margin service. Deploying such capital without proven cash‑flow coverage creates a high risk of cash‑burn and dilution of investor value.
* **Thin margins** – Grocery retail typically yields single‑digit gross margins. Adding the cost of rapid, 30‑minute delivery and sophisticated automation further compresses profitability, demanding very high order volumes to break even.
* **Customer‑density requirement** – Efficient 30‑minute slots depend on a dense concentration of orders within each warehouse’s catchment area. Expanding to many cities before confirming sufficient density in the first market is speculative.
* **Lack of operating track record** – No historical data exist on order frequency, churn, or logistics cost per order. Without this, forecasting cash‑flow, breakeven timing, and return on the massive capital spend is unreliable.

Given these risk factors, proceeding with the full‑scale rollout is premature. The prudent path is to consolidate operations in the initial city, rigorously measure unit economics, and only then consider incremental expansion and capital investment.

DECISION: NO-GO
