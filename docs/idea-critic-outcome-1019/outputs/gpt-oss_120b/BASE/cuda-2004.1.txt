The proposal seeks to transform every consumer graphics chip into a general‑purpose compute device by adding silicon, a compiler, a toolchain and university‑level education support. The intent is to create an early‑adopter ecosystem that could later become a sizable market for scientific‑computing workloads.

**Strategic fit** – The company’s revenue base is currently almost entirely gaming and workstation graphics. Its competitive advantage rests on delivering the highest‑performance graphics cores at the lowest cost per frame. Adding non‑graphics circuitry to every chip directly raises die size and per‑unit cost, which will erode price competitiveness against the main rival in the core market where revenue and cash flow are currently concentrated.

**Market timing** – In 2004–2006 there is no established, paying market for GPU computing. Academic users are still experimenting with patched graphics APIs; no commercial applications or large‑scale deployments exist. Funding a full compiler and software stack without guaranteed customers creates a classic “build‑then‑sell” risk: the company would bear all development expense while waiting for a market that may not materialise for years, if at all.

**Financial impact** – The cost increase is incurred on *every* unit sold, not just on a specialized product line. This dilutes margins across the entire consumer portfolio, which could force price cuts or loss of market share to the rival that remains focused on pure graphics performance.

**Engineering focus** – Diverting engineering resources to a non‑core feature set risks slowing the cadence of graphics‑only innovations that are critical to maintaining the lead in the primary market. The rival will likely exploit any slowdown, further threatening the company’s dominant position.

**Potential upside** – Early entry could give the firm a first‑mover advantage if a GPU‑compute market later emerges, and university outreach could seed future demand. However, these benefits are speculative and long‑term, whereas the immediate costs are concrete and affect the core business now.

Given the high per‑unit cost, absence of committed customers, and the danger of diverting resources from the core competitive battle, the proposal presents an unacceptable risk profile in its current form.

DECISION: NO-GO
