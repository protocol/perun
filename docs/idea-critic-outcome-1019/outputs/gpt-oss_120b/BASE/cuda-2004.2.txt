The proposal seeks to embed general‑purpose computing features in every consumer graphics chip, turning a pure‑gaming product line into a dual‑purpose platform. The expected benefits are a first‑mover foothold in an emerging scientific‑computing niche, the creation of a software ecosystem (compiler, toolchain, university curricula), and the possibility of future revenue streams once the market matures.

However, the company’s current revenue base is almost entirely dependent on high‑performance gaming and workstation graphics. Adding silicon for GPGPU capability will raise the bill‑of‑materials for each unit, directly increasing price or compressing margins in a market where cost sensitivity and performance per dollar are decisive. Without any committed customers, the cost uplift cannot be offset by immediate sales. Academic users are still “hacking” existing APIs, indicating that the demand is exploratory rather than commercial.

Diverting engineering effort to build compilers, runtime libraries, and teaching programs threatens focus on the core competitive race with the primary rival. Any delay or reduction in feature delivery for the flagship gaming chips could erode market share, which is the current engine of cash flow and brand strength.

A prudent approach would be to test the concept on a limited, optional product tier or a development‑board SKU, gather concrete demand signals, and only then scale the feature across the entire consumer line. This would preserve the core business while still seeding the ecosystem.

Given the lack of a paying market, the universal cost increase, and the risk of distracting critical engineering resources, the proposal as written cannot be approved in its current form.

DECISION: NO-GO
