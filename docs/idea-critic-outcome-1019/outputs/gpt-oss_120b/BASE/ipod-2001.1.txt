The proposal targets a market that, in early 2001, is still nascent and highly price‑sensitive. Competing handhelds such as the Creative Zen and Rio series sell for well under $200 and already have modest user bases. Even though those devices have not achieved mass‑market success, they demonstrate that consumers expect a sub‑$300 price point for a pure‑play digital‑audio player.

A $400 price tag places the new player above the best‑selling rivals and, unlike the forthcoming iPod, it will be usable only with the maker’s own low‑share PCs. The addressable market is therefore limited to a fraction of the already small PC install base, dramatically reducing potential volume and making it difficult to achieve economies of scale. Without a broader software ecosystem (e.g., a cross‑platform music manager) the product offers little advantage over existing players that work on any PC.

Component risk is also significant. Integrating a 5 GB hard drive into a pocket‑sized device on a sub‑year schedule demands secure supply chains for miniature drives, flash controllers, and battery packs—components that were still maturing in 2001. Any delay would push launch past the narrow window when portable digital audio is gaining consumer awareness but before legal download services become mainstream.

Finally, the broader industry context provides little support. Record‑label licensing for digital downloads is still in its infancy; without a legal content pipeline the hardware alone will struggle to attract buyers.

Given the steep price, restricted compatibility, and high supply‑chain risk, the venture is unlikely to reach profitable volumes or establish a foothold before competitors solidify their positions.

**DECISION: NO‑GO**
