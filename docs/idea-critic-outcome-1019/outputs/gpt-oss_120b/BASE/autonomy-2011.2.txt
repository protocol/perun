However, the economics of the transaction are unfavorable.

* **Price premium** – Paying $11.7 bn represents a 64 % premium to the current share price and roughly 11 × trailing revenue. Comparable software acquisitions in 2010‑11 typically ranged from 4‑7 × revenue. The excess premium must be justified by immediate, high‑impact synergies, yet the buyer lacks a proven software integration track record to guarantee such returns.

* **Accounting quality** – The target’s financial statements have been subject to media scrutiny since 2009, suggesting possible earnings volatility, aggressive revenue recognition, or undisclosed liabilities. This raises the risk of post‑close adjustments that could erode value.

* **Integration risk** – The buyer’s core competencies are in hardware manufacturing and supply‑chain management. Transforming into a software‑centric organization demands new talent, governance, and cultural shifts. Past attempts by hardware firms to assimilate large software assets have shown prolonged integration periods and frequent write‑downs.

* **Due‑diligence timeline** – A “few weeks” is a compressed window for deep forensic accounting, IP validation, and integration planning, especially given the red‑flagged financial reporting. Rushing could miss material issues that would later surface as costly contingencies.

* **Strategic alternatives** – Smaller, strategically focused software acquisitions or organic development could provide the needed capabilities at lower multiples and with reduced execution risk.

Given the inflated purchase price, questionable accounting, and the buyer’s limited software experience, the prudent course is to pause and either renegotiate terms or explore alternative pathways.
