The proposal rests on strong consumer‑preference data: blind taste tests, surveys and focus groups all show the new, sweeter formula outranks both the incumbent product and the rival’s offering. That evidence suggests a clear market opportunity to recapture lost share from diet and non‑cola segments that have eroded the brand’s position.

However, the plan also eliminates the only existing flagship product, removing any safety net should the new formula under‑perform after launch. The original cola carries a century‑long heritage and deep emotional attachment among a core loyal base. Removing it entirely risks alienating that segment, generating negative word‑of‑mouth, and potentially prompting bottlers—who have a vested interest in the legacy product—to push back or reduce distribution effort.

Because the testing did not inform participants that the original would be discontinued, the data may overstate acceptance; respondents could have reacted differently if they knew the historic brand would vanish. Moreover, the lack of a fallback product leaves the company exposed to any unforeseen quality, supply‑chain, or regulatory issues that could arise with the new formula.

A more prudent approach would retain the original as a limited‑edition or “classic” line, providing a safety valve and preserving brand heritage while still rolling out the new formula as the primary offering. This dual‑track strategy would mitigate backlash, give bottlers confidence, and allow the company to monitor real‑world performance before committing fully.

Given the significant risk of brand‑heritage erosion, bottler resistance, and the absence of a fallback, the current all‑or‑nothing proposal is too risky to approve as written.

DECISION: NO-GO
