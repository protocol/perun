The proposal tries to leap‑frog an emerging market that is still undefined. In early 2001 the portable‑audio segment is fragmented: a handful of low‑priced players (e.g., Creative’s ZEN, iRiver) have modest sales, while Apple’s forthcoming iPod (5 GB, $399) will soon reshape expectations around design, ease‑of‑use and brand cachet.  

**Key concerns**

1. **Addressable market** – Limiting the device to the maker’s own computers shrinks the potential customer pool to a tiny fraction of PC owners. Even if the PC line grew, most buyers will already own a compatible music player or will be attracted to a device that works with any PC.

2. **Pricing pressure** – At roughly $400 the unit will sit at the top of the price ladder, directly against the iPod’s launch price and above the cheaper, already‑available alternatives. Without a compelling differentiation (e.g., superior UI, exclusive content, or a strong brand), price alone will deter most buyers.

3. **Execution risk** – The company lacks consumer‑electronics manufacturing experience. Sourcing a 5 GB hard‑drive, battery, and rugged enclosure within a year is ambitious, especially given the tight component lead‑times typical of the era. Delays would erode any first‑mover advantage and inflate costs.

4. **Content ecosystem** – The music industry has not yet embraced legal digital downloads. A device sold only with proprietary software cannot leverage emerging licensing models, leaving the user dependent on ripped or illegal files, which could invite legal and public‑relations issues.

**Potential upside** – Early entry could give the firm a foothold in a market that will later explode, and the hardware experience could seed future consumer products. However, those benefits require a broader platform, competitive pricing, and a clear path to content licensing—none of which are present in the current plan.

**Recommendation** – The risks outweigh the speculative upside. Approve only after the proposal is re‑engineered to (a) support all major PC platforms, (b) target a lower price point or differentiated feature set, and (c) secure a realistic component supply chain. As presented, the project should not proceed.

DECISION: NO-GO
