The retailer’s domestic success shows it can operate a high‑volume, low‑margin model, but the proposed entry into a neighboring market introduces three inter‑linked challenges that outweigh the upside of a rapid, large‑scale launch.

**Operational tempo:** Opening 130‑150 stores in two years demands simultaneous site build‑out, staffing, and activation of a brand‑new supply‑chain platform. The logistics network has never been tested outside the home market, and the inventory‑management system is unproven at the scale required. Any delay in one store cascades to others, inflating costs and eroding the intended “discount” price advantage.

**Supply‑chain and regulatory learning curve:** Cross‑border freight, customs clearance, and bilingual product labeling add complexity and hidden expense. Early missteps (e.g., missed customs deadlines or labeling non‑compliance) can trigger fines, store closures, or brand damage in a market where the incumbent already enjoys strong consumer trust.

**Competitive dynamics and price perception:** The dominant local player benefits from scale, brand loyalty, and higher price points on certain categories. While the retailer can undercut on price, it must sustain thin margins while absorbing the higher operating costs of a new logistics network. If price cuts are perceived as a signal of inferior quality, the retailer may fail to attract the target price‑sensitive segment.

A more prudent approach would be a phased rollout: pilot 15‑20 stores, validate the supply chain, refine bilingual labeling processes, and calibrate pricing against the incumbent’s perception. Success in the pilot can then fund a staged expansion, preserving capital and limiting exposure.

Given the current risk profile, the proposal should not be approved in its present form.

DECISION: NO-GO
