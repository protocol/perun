The target is a six‑person team developing an open‑source mobile OS at a time when the mobile landscape is tightly controlled by carriers and dominated by a handful of entrenched platforms (Symbian, Windows Mobile, Palm, BlackBerry). The search‑advertising company has no hardware expertise and no existing foothold in the handset market, so the acquisition would be its sole entry point.

**Strategic upside**  
- Owning an OS could eventually give the advertiser control over the search experience on phones, driving web usage and ad impressions.  
- An open‑source model may lower entry barriers for handset makers, potentially creating a broad ecosystem that feeds search traffic.

**Key risks**  
1. **Revenue void** – The OS has no proven monetisation path; the company would have to invent a model (e.g., licensing, revenue share, pre‑installed search) before any cash flow arrives.  
2. **Adoption hurdle** – Handset OEMs have little incentive to abandon established platforms that already satisfy carrier requirements. Convincing them to adopt a nascent, untested OS is a long‑term effort.  
3. **Carrier gate‑keeping** – Carriers control which handsets reach consumers and have historically resisted platforms that threaten their control over services and pricing.  
4. **Payback horizon** – Even with optimistic adoption, the return would likely materialise only after several product cycles, far beyond typical investment horizons for a $50 M outlay.

**Financial perspective**  
Paying a minimum of $50 million for a team that currently produces no revenue is a high‑multiple bet on future market share. The size of the payment relative to the company’s cash flow and the lack of any near‑term revenue stream make the investment financially precarious.

**Alternative approaches**  
- Pursue a strategic partnership or joint‑development agreement to test the OS with a willing OEM before committing full acquisition capital.  
- Allocate a smaller, staged investment tied to milestones (e.g., prototype handset, carrier trial) to mitigate exposure.

Given the magnitude of the upfront cost, the absence of a clear monetisation route, and the structural barriers posed by carriers and OEMs, the proposal carries a risk profile that exceeds an acceptable level for immediate approval.

DECISION: NO-GO
