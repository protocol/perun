The proposal calls for a $1 billion commitment to build massive, highly automated warehouses and a large delivery fleet before the company has validated the economics of its core service. While the venture‑capital backing shows confidence, the facts presented expose several deal‑breakers:

1. **Capital intensity vs. thin margins** – Grocery retail operates on 1‑3 % net margins. Deploying billions in fixed assets creates a cost base that can only be covered by extremely high throughput and deep discounting, which the business has not yet demonstrated.

2. **Lack of operating track record** – The startup began taking orders in June 1999 in a single metro area. No data on order volume, basket size, delivery density, or cost‑to‑serve exists. Without these unit‑economics, any forecast of cash‑flow breakeven is speculative.

3. **Customer‑density requirement** – The 30‑minute delivery window demands a critical mass of orders per square mile to justify the automated warehouse footprint and fleet size. Expanding to a dozen additional metros multiplies the risk of under‑utilized capacity.

4. **Speed of expansion** – Entering new cities “at a rapid pace” bypasses the learning loop that would normally inform warehouse sizing, technology investment, and routing algorithms. Mistakes made at scale will be far more costly than those made in a single pilot market.

A prudent path would be to operate the first warehouse at a modest scale, collect hard data on order frequency, delivery routing efficiency, and margin contribution, then iterate the design before committing additional capital. This staged approach reduces exposure, preserves cash, and aligns spending with proven demand.

Given the current lack of validated economics and the disproportionate capital outlay, the proposal should not be approved in its present form.

DECISION: NO-GO
