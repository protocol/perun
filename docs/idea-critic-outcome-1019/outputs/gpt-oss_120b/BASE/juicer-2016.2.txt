The proposal targets a premium, convenience‑driven segment of the consumer‑hardware market. The startup’s strong financing (tens of millions raised) suggests it can absorb early‑stage development costs and sustain a subscription supply chain. A $699 price point positions the juicer as a luxury appliance, which may appeal to affluent early adopters who value seamless, “set‑and‑forget” nutrition solutions.

However, the core concerns are substantive:

* **Device cost vs. value** – $699 is substantially higher than typical countertop juicers, and the value proposition hinges entirely on the convenience of pre‑packed bags and the app. Without clear evidence that customers will pay a premium for this convenience, price resistance is likely.

* **Subscription economics** – The proposal does not disclose the per‑bag price or the total cost of ownership compared with buying fresh produce and juicing at home. If the subscription is not demonstrably cheaper or at least competitive on a per‑serving basis, the model may be perceived as overpriced and lock‑in‑driven.

* **Internet dependence** – Requiring Wi‑Fi for freshness checks adds a barrier for users with limited connectivity or privacy concerns, potentially narrowing the addressable market.

* **Market size uncertainty** – The “size of the market” is flagged as a concern. The intersection of high‑price kitchen appliances, subscription food services, and mandatory connectivity is a niche that has not been proven at scale. The risk of low adoption is amplified by the upfront hardware cost.

Given the high financial exposure (both hardware and ongoing supply chain) and the unresolved questions around pricing, customer willingness to pay, and market reach, proceeding without additional validation (price testing, pilot subscription rollout, market sizing) would be premature.

**DECISION: NO-GO**
