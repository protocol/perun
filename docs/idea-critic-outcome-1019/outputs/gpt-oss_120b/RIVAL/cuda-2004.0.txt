The plan aims to turn a gaming‑focused GPU into a general‑purpose scientific accelerator by adding hardware, a compiler, and a software stack, then subsidising university teaching. While this could open a future market, the proposal carries several decisive problems:

* **Market uncertainty** – In 2004‑2006 there is no established paying customer base for GPU computing. Academic users are still experimenting with hacked APIs, meaning revenue would rely on speculative adoption rather than firm contracts.
* **Cost impact** – Adding area and a full toolchain raises the bill‑of‑materials on every consumer chip. Without a guaranteed buyer for the extra capability, the price increase will erode competitiveness in the core gaming/workstation segment.
* **Strategic distraction** – The company’s primary battle is a performance race against its main rival. Diverting engineering resources to a speculative feature set risks falling behind in its core market, where revenue is already dominant.
* **Niche demand** – Early scientific users represent a small, fragmented community. Funding university teaching does not create immediate demand and may not translate into enough volume to offset higher unit costs.

Given the lack of a large, paying market, the unavoidable cost increase on all units, and the potential to weaken the company’s core competitive position, the proposal is premature. It would be wiser to wait for a clearer demand signal before committing resources.

DECISION: NO-GO
