The proposal targets a speculative, long‑term market that does not yet exist. Adding general‑purpose compute units and a full compiler to every consumer GPU will raise silicon area and unit cost across the entire product line, eroding price competitiveness in the core gaming and workstation segments that currently drive the company’s revenue. Without committed customers, the extra cost cannot be amortized, and the price increase will likely reduce market share against the primary rival, especially as performance margins are already tight.

Engineering effort to build a universal toolchain and support academic teaching will divert talent from the critical race to improve graphics performance, power efficiency, and driver stability—areas where the rival is gaining ground. The risk of “distracting” the core team is amplified by the fact that academic users are currently using hacked APIs, indicating that a robust, production‑grade stack is still many years away and will require sustained investment with little near‑term return.

While pioneering programmable GPUs could eventually open a high‑value scientific‑computing market, the timeline (2004‑2006) is too short to capture sufficient demand, and the company lacks a clear go‑to‑market strategy or large‑scale partners willing to pay a premium now. A more prudent approach would be to continue monitoring emerging GPU‑computing workloads, form strategic alliances with early adopters, and explore incremental hardware extensions only after a demonstrable commercial demand appears.

Given the immediate cost impact, the absence of a paying market, and the high risk of diverting resources from the core competitive battle, the proposal should not be approved in its current form.

DECISION: NO-GO
