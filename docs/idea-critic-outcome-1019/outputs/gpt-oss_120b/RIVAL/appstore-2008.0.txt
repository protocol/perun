The proposal pivots the company from a web‑only strategy to a native‑app ecosystem, aligning it with emerging market expectations for richer, offline‑capable experiences. A single, company‑run store gives the firm direct control over pricing, revenue (30 % cut), and quality through mandatory review, which can mitigate many of the listed risks.

**Security & stability** – A rigorous review process, combined with sandboxing at the OS level, can contain untrusted code. While this adds workload, the same model proved scalable for contemporaries that launched successful stores.

**Carrier objections** – Carriers will object only if apps bypass their billing or services. The store can enforce policies (e.g., disallowing carrier‑bypassing APIs) and negotiate revenue‑share agreements, turning a potential conflict into a partnership opportunity.

**Review workload** – Initial volume will be modest; the company can staff a dedicated review team and automate parts of the pipeline. As the ecosystem matures, workload grows predictably and can be scaled with incremental hires or outsourced triage.

**Product polish** – Curated acceptance criteria and a “golden‑set” of UI/UX guidelines preserve the brand’s quality. Bad apps can be removed quickly, and the store’s rating system will surface high‑quality offerings.

**Competitive pressure** – Rival platforms already provide open distribution, meaning users and developers expect native app availability. Not entering the market risks irrelevance and loss of developer goodwill.

Overall, the strategic upside (new revenue, ecosystem lock‑in, differentiation) outweighs the manageable risks, provided the company commits resources to review, security, and carrier alignment.

**Recommendation:** Proceed with the native‑app store as outlined, monitoring the identified risk areas closely.

DECISION: GO
