The proposal calls for a $1 billion rollout of massive, highly automated warehouses and a fleet of delivery trucks across more than a dozen metropolitan areas before the company has validated the unit economics of its first market.  

**Key concerns**

1. **Capital intensity vs. thin margins** – Grocery delivery operates on low profit per order. Deploying expensive, large‑scale infrastructure before proving that each order covers its variable and fixed costs creates a high risk of cash‑burn without a clear path to profitability.  

2. **Customer‑density requirement** – The model depends on a dense concentration of orders within a 30‑minute delivery window. Without demonstrated demand patterns, the company cannot guarantee sufficient order volume to justify the size and automation level of the planned warehouses.  

3. **Lack of operating track record** – The business has only just begun taking orders in its first city. There is no historical data on order frequency, basket size, churn, or fulfillment costs to inform the design of the warehouses or the optimal fleet size.  

4. **Speed of expansion** – Entering many new cities rapidly magnifies the above risks. Each new market will repeat the same learning‑curve costs, multiplying cash outflow while the company is still figuring out its core economics.  

5. **Strategic alternative** – A phased approach—starting with a modest, flexible warehouse footprint, iterating on automation based on real‑world performance, and expanding only after achieving a proven, positive contribution margin—reduces risk and preserves capital for growth when the model is validated.

**Conclusion**  
Given the extreme capital commitment, the low‑margin nature of grocery delivery, the need for high order density, and the absence of any operating data, proceeding with the proposed $1 billion, multi‑city build‑out is premature. The company should first prove unit economics in its initial market, then scale incrementally.

DECISION: NO-GO
