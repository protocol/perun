**Problem & Stakes**  
The company’s mobile OS holds only a low‑single‑digit share of smartphones. Its survival depends on preventing the primary handset maker from abandoning the OS for the dominant rival platform, which already enjoys a massive app ecosystem. The proposal is to acquire that handset maker’s smartphone division for €5.4 bn, thereby gaining hardware control and a captive device base.

**Key Risks**  
1. **Integration Complexity** – Merging a large workforce, supply chain, and manufacturing operations is costly and disruptive; past tech‑hardware acquisitions have shown high failure rates.  
2. **Industry Reaction** – Other handset OEMs may accelerate migration to the rival platform, reducing the acquired division’s sales further and diminishing any network effects.  
3. **Ecosystem Fixability** – Owning hardware does not automatically grow the app ecosystem; developers choose platforms based on user reach, not device ownership. Without a clear plan to attract developers, the acquisition may not solve the core app‑availability problem.  
4. **Financial Exposure** – €5.4 bn is a sizable outlay for a business whose revenue is already declining; the deal could strain cash flow and divert resources from core software improvements.

**Recommendation**  
Do **not** proceed with the acquisition at this time. The strategic benefit (hardware‑software alignment) is outweighed by integration risk, uncertain ecosystem impact, and the likelihood of further OEM defections. Instead, focus on strengthening the OS’s developer incentives, forming strategic hardware partnerships, and improving the user experience to organically grow market share.

**Conclusion**  
Given the high integration risk, limited upside on the app‑ecosystem problem, and potential for adverse market reactions, the proposal should be rejected or significantly re‑scoped before any commitment.

DECISION: NO-GO
