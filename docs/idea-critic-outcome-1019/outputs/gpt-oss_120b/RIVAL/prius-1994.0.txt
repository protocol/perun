The proposal aims to launch a first‑generation petrol‑electric hybrid by 1997, leveraging an untested power‑split transmission and nickel‑metal‑hydride (NiMH) batteries. While the market currently enjoys cheap fuel and there are no regulatory pressures or competitors, the commercial case rests on three fragile pillars:

1. **Technology risk** – Both the power‑split drivetrain and NiMH packs have not yet proven durability in a volume‑production environment. Early battery degradation would drive warranty costs and damage brand reputation.

2. **Cost risk** – Novel components will carry premium pricing, and without economies of scale the vehicle’s price could exceed what price‑sensitive consumers are willing to pay, especially when fuel is inexpensive.

3. **Demand risk** – No clear near‑term consumer signal exists for hybrids; the market may not materialize until fuel prices rise or regulations compel adoption.

Mitigating these risks would require a lengthy validation program, supplier qualification, and a realistic production timeline that exceeds the 1997 target. Proceeding now would likely expose the automaker to high warranty exposure, potential recalls, and a costly product that could under‑sell.

Given the current environment—cheap fuel, no regulatory mandate, and unproven core technologies—the prudent course is to pause the public schedule, invest in a focused prototype and durability program, and gather market data before committing to mass production.

**Recommendation:** Hold the proposal for further technical validation and demand assessment.
