The plan targets a market that already offers abundant free, ad‑supported short video—both user‑generated and professionally produced—on platforms with massive user bases. While the proposed technology that auto‑adjusts video orientation is novel, it does not solve the core demand problem: there is no proven appetite for a premium, subscription‑based short‑form service. Existing short‑form consumption is driven by low‑cost, free content; users are already experiencing “subscription fatigue” from numerous paid services, making them reluctant to add another recurring fee, especially for content under ten minutes.

The financial commitment (> $1 billion) is extraordinary for an untested product class. Even with A‑list talent and high production budgets, the cost structure will be heavy, requiring a large subscriber base at a price point that can cover both talent fees and the technology investment. The proposal does not present a pricing strategy, break‑even analysis, or clear path to acquire and retain enough paying users to justify the capital outlay.

Investor composition (major studios and brands) adds credibility but also creates potential conflicts of interest and expectations that may pressure the service to favor studio content over truly differentiated programming. Competition for phone attention is intense; users already split time among social media, streaming, and gaming apps. Without a compelling, exclusive value proposition beyond orientation flexibility, the service is unlikely to capture sufficient mindshare.

Given the scale of risk—unproven demand, high price sensitivity, massive capital requirement, and fierce attention competition—the proposal should be revisited. A phased approach (pilot with limited budget, market testing, and clear pricing research) would reduce exposure before committing a billion‑plus dollars.

DECISION: NO-GO
