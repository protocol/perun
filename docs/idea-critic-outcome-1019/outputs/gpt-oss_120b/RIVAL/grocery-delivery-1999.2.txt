**Issue**  
The startup wants to spend roughly $1 billion on massive, highly automated warehouses and a fleet of delivery trucks to expand into more than a dozen cities before it has validated the unit economics of its inaugural market. Grocery delivery is a low‑margin, capital‑intensive business that depends on high customer density and tight cost control.  

**Stakes if we pick wrong**  
If the economics prove unfavorable, the company will be saddled with under‑utilized assets, massive debt, and a burn rate that can outpace cash flow, jeopardizing the entire venture and the investors’ capital.  

**Options**  

A) **Approve the full $1 B rollout now (recommended)** – Accelerates market dominance but locks in huge fixed costs before any proof of profit.  
✅ Captures early geographic advantage and builds a barrier to entry.  
✅ Leverages the current VC funding wave to secure long‑term infrastructure.  
❌ Exposes the firm to massive financial risk without validated unit economics; low‑margin grocery margins may not sustain the capital intensity.  

B) **Phase the expansion** – Begin with a limited number of modestly sized fulfillment centers in the current and one adjacent market, using the capital to prove unit economics before scaling.  
✅ Aligns spend with data‑driven validation, reducing downside risk.  
✅ Allows iterative learning on customer density, delivery slot pricing, and automation ROI.  
❌ Slower geographic rollout could cede first‑mover advantage to competitors.  

C) **Hold and redesign the model** – Reassess the business case, explore partnership or shared‑warehouse models, and possibly target higher‑margin product categories first.  
✅ Minimizes exposure to capital‑intensive assets.  
❌ Delays growth and may miss the current market window.  

**Recommendation**  
Choose **Option B** – a phased, data‑driven expansion. It balances the need for growth with the imperative to validate economics, preserving capital and protecting the venture’s long‑term viability.  

DECISION: NO-GO
