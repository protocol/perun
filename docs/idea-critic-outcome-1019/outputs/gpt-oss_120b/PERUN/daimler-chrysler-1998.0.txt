**Steelman:** Merge the German premium automaker with the US third‑largest automaker to create a globally‑scale company, combine complementary brand portfolios, share platforms, and gain a strong foothold in the North‑American mass‑market segment under a single board and stock listing.

**Attack (skeptic):**  
1. **Cultural & labour clash** – German and US firms have historically divergent work‑council structures and union practices. Integration failures in past cross‑border auto mergers have generated costly strikes and productivity drops. *Scenario:* A 3‑month work stoppage in the US plant adds $1.8 B in lost output, exceeding typical integration budgets.  
2. **Cyclical truck exposure** – The US partner’s current profitability hinges on trucks/minivans/SUVs, a segment that can swing ±30 % in a downturn. If a market correction cuts truck sales by 25 %, combined earnings fall 12 %, threatening debt covenants.  
3. **Premium brand dilution** – Sharing platforms may force the German brand to adopt lower‑cost architectures, eroding its margin premium (historically ~20 % above average). A 5 % margin loss translates to $800 M annually.  
4. **Equality of terms** – Without disclosed share‑exchange ratios, the “merger of equals” could mask a takeover, leading to governance disputes.

**Better‑way alternatives:**  
- Form a joint‑venture for platform sharing while keeping separate boards.  
- Acquire a strategic minority stake in the US firm to test integration.  
- Pursue a pure licensing agreement for North‑American distribution.

**Kill‑criteria (pre‑mortem):** Abort if any of the following materialize within the first 12 months: (a) integration cost > $5 B, (b) truck‑segment revenue share > 50 % of combined sales, (c) board composition deviates > 5 % from a 50/50 split, (d) margin compression on the premium brand > 4 %.

**Conclusion:** The merger’s upside is outweighed by high integration risk, cyclical exposure, and brand‑dilution danger, especially when lower‑risk partnership models exist.

DECISION: NO-GO
