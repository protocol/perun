**Steelman claim**  
Merge the 20 M‑subscriber dial‑up ISP (highly‑valued but volatile) with the world’s largest traditional media conglomerate in an all‑stock deal, giving ISP shareholders majority control, to combine content and distribution, cross‑promote media over the Internet, and use the conglomerate’s cable network for high‑speed broadband.

**Skeptic attacks**  
1. **Dial‑up decline** – Broadband adoption in the U.S. was already surpassing 10 % in 1999 and projected to exceed 30 % within two years; the ISP’s core revenue base is therefore on a rapidly eroding foundation.  
2. **Valuation volatility** – The ISP’s market cap is driven by dot‑com hype; a 50 % price correction in six months would wipe out the majority stake held by its shareholders, leaving the conglomerate’s owners with a severely diluted equity position.  
3. **Cable access uncertainty** – The cable network is not owned by the conglomerate; access must be negotiated with third‑party operators who may charge high fees or refuse cooperation, undermining the promised broadband rollout.  
4. **Cultural mismatch** – Prior large media‑tech mergers (e.g., AOL‑Time Warner) have shown that divergent corporate cultures and decision‑making styles lead to integration paralysis and loss of focus.  
5. **Regulatory headwinds** – An all‑stock merger of this size would trigger antitrust review; given the vertical integration of content and distribution, approval is far from guaranteed.

**Better‑way alternatives**  
- **Strategic partnership**: licensing content to the ISP and jointly investing in broadband without equity exchange.  
- **Separate broadband venture**: the conglomerate builds its own ISP or partners with an established broadband provider, preserving focus and limiting exposure to the ISP’s valuation risk.  
- **Do nothing**: maintain current operations and let market forces dictate organic growth, avoiding integration costs.

**Kill‑criteria** (must be met to proceed)  
- Broadband penetration > 30 % within 24 months **or** ISP dial‑up revenue < 15 % of total revenue.  
- ISP market‑cap volatility > 50 % in any 6‑month window.  
- Failure to secure long‑term, price‑capped access to the cable network.  
- Regulatory approval not obtained within 12 months.

**Conclusion** – The merger’s upside is outweighed by severe execution risk, valuation uncertainty, and regulatory obstacles. A partnership or independent broadband effort offers comparable strategic benefits with far lower downside.

DECISION: NO-GO
