This proposal represents a high-stakes gambit to define the future of personal transportation before the market demands it. The strategy relies on first-mover advantage to establish a technological moat, betting that regulatory landscapes and fuel economics will shift in favor of efficiency before competitors can react.

However, the risk profile is extreme. Committing to mass production by 1997 with unproven battery durability and a novel transmission architecture invites catastrophic warranty exposure. In the mid-1990s, nickel-metal-hydride technology at volume scale is an engineering unknown, not a validated component. With fuel cheap and no regulatory mandate, the economic value proposition for the consumer is weak. The cost premium of the hybrid system cannot be justified by fuel savings alone in the current market environment.

The "bold public schedule" amplifies the danger. Announcing a fixed date for unproven technology creates a binary outcome: succeed perfectly or suffer reputational damage that could cripple the brand's engineering credibility for a decade. If the batteries fail prematurely or the transmission proves unreliable, the recall costs and brand erosion would outweigh any first-mover marketing benefits.

Furthermore, developing this as a corporate-level program without a near-term demand signal violates basic capital allocation discipline. This is not an incremental improvement; it is a bet-the-company pivot on unvalidated assumptions about consumer behavior and future regulation. While the vision of a hybrid future is sound, the execution plan ignores the valley of death between prototype and reliable mass production.

A "GO" decision here locks the organization into a rigid timeline that leaves no room for the inevitable technical iterations required to mature the battery and transmission systems. It prioritizes marketing bravado over engineering reality. The prudent path is to continue development as a lower-volume learning program or technology demonstrator until battery cycle life is proven and regulatory signals clarify the market landscape. Committing to full mass production now is premature and financially reckless.

DECISION: NO-GO
