The proposal to launch a curated, company-run app store in 2008 represents a strategic pivot from the CEO's previous stance that web apps were sufficient. This shift acknowledges a critical market reality: while web apps offer accessibility, they cannot match the performance, hardware integration, and user experience fidelity of native code. Rival platforms already leverage this advantage; failing to match them risks relegating the device to a secondary role despite its hardware innovations.

The concerns raised are valid but manageable through the proposed model. Security and stability risks are mitigated precisely by the mandatory review process; a walled garden ensures that only vetted, high-quality code reaches the device, preserving the brand's reputation for polish. While carrier objections regarding bypassed services are a political hurdle, the revenue share model (70/30 split) creates a powerful economic incentive for developers to prioritize this platform, eventually forcing carriers to adapt rather than block. The review workload is a necessary operational cost to maintain ecosystem health, acting as a quality filter that open distribution lacks.

Critically, the 30 percent commission establishes a sustainable business model that funds the platform's growth while remaining attractive enough to draw top-tier talent. By controlling distribution, the company dictates the pace of innovation, preventing the fragmentation and malware issues plaguing open rivals. This approach transforms the phone from a static appliance into a dynamic platform, unlocking value far beyond hardware sales. The risk of hurting product polish is paradoxically reduced by curation; without it, the user experience would degrade under the weight of unoptimized, unstable third-party software.

The timing is urgent. With competitors already entrenched, waiting for a "perfect" solution or clinging to the web-app-only doctrine guarantees irrelevance. This proposal balances openness with control, creating a virtuous cycle where better apps drive hardware sales, which in turn attract more developers. It is the definitive move required to transition from a novel hardware manufacturer to a dominant ecosystem player. The concerns are execution details, not strategic flaws.
