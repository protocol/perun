This proposal represents a high-risk pivot from a struggling hardware manufacturer into capital-intensive retail during an economic downturn. The company currently holds a minor market share (3–5%) and is unprofitable, yet it plans to incur massive fixed costs via prime mall leases in a low-margin sector.

The strategic logic relies on two premises: that third-party retailers are failing to demonstrate the product's value, and that a "digital-music strategy" requires physical ownership of the customer experience. While controlling the narrative is sound, the execution method is dangerously misaligned with the company's financial reality.

The risks are immediate and severe. High fixed leases create an operational break-even point that will be difficult to reach with low volume, especially given the company's recent losses. Inventory risk shifts entirely to the balance sheet. Furthermore, alienating existing resellers could collapse current revenue streams before new stores become profitable. The industry track record for manufacturer-owned retail in this era is overwhelmingly negative, suggesting structural headwinds rather than isolated failures.

The "digital-music strategy" is a compelling differentiator, but betting the company's survival on a retail rollout during a recession is speculative. A company with limited cash reserves cannot afford the long ramp-up time required for retail profitability. The proposal treats retail as a marketing expense but structures it as a heavy-asset operational burden.

A viable alternative would be to establish small "shop-in-shop" demonstrations within existing partner stores or launch limited experiential pop-ups in key cities. This would test the "demonstration" hypothesis without locking the company into decade-long leases and massive overhead. The current plan assumes instant success in a hostile environment, ignoring the liquidity constraints of a loss-making entity.

The ambition to control the customer journey is correct, but the vehicle chosen threatens to bankrupt the company before the strategy can mature. The fixed costs are too high, the timing is too dangerous, and the channel conflict is too great to proceed as proposed. The company must stabilize its core profitability before attempting a vertical integration of this magnitude.

DECISION: NO-GO
