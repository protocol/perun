This $36 billion merger presents a classic "buy vs. build" dilemma wrapped in massive technical debt. The strategic logic is sound: acquiring a dominant position in the high-value construction and business push-to-talk (PTT) segment is faster than attempting to displace an entrenched competitor with a homegrown solution. The target's customer base offers immediate revenue stability and high switching costs, which are attractive defensive moats.

However, the operational risks are severe. Integrating two incompatible network technologies in 2005 is not merely an IT challenge; it is a civil engineering feat. Running parallel networks indefinitely destroys margin synergy and complicates maintenance, while a forced migration risks alienating the very PTT customers being acquired if service reliability dips during the transition. The "large goodwill" noted is a financial red flag; it implies the purchase price significantly exceeds the tangible value of assets, leaving the combined entity vulnerable to future write-downs if integration stalls or the PTT market shifts toward cellular alternatives.

The core failure point lies in the technology mismatch. If the buyer's network is data-limited compared to modern standards, and the target's tech is incompatible, the combined company risks owning two legacy liabilities rather than one streamlined future-proof asset. The integration cost will likely exceed initial estimates, consuming capital that should be deployed for next-generation network upgrades. Furthermore, retaining the legacy equipment to support the acquired base creates a permanent drag on operational efficiency.

While the revenue potential from the business sector is compelling, the execution risk is too high to approve the deal in its current form. The proposal lacks a credible, funded technical migration path that guarantees service continuity for the critical PTT users. Without a clear plan to harmonize the technologies or a strategy to migrate customers without churn, this acquisition threatens to become a value-destructive albatross. The financial leverage required for a $36 billion deal leaves no room for the inevitable integration overruns.

DECISION: NO-GO
