This proposal represents a high-stakes gambit to define the future of personal mobility before the market demands it. The strategy relies on first-mover advantage to establish an insurmountable technological moat, betting that regulatory landscapes and fuel economics will shift in favor of efficiency before competitors can react.

However, the factual basis for approval is critically weak. Committing to mass production by 1997 requires assuming success in three unproven variables: the reliability of a novel power-split transmission, the durability of nickel-metal-hydride batteries in real-world cycles, and the existence of a customer base willing to pay a premium for unrequested efficiency. History demonstrates that early hybrid attempts often faltered due to battery degradation and excessive cost, leading to massive warranty liabilities that can cripple a balance sheet.

With fuel cheap and regulators inactive, there is no immediate economic or legal pressure forcing adoption. The "no near-term demand signal" is a fatal flaw for a capital-intensive consumer product launch. Launching a complex, unproven powertrain without a clear path to profitability or a proven reliability record exposes the corporation to existential financial risk. If the batteries fail prematurely or the transmission proves fragile, the brand reputation damage will outweigh any technological prestige gained.

The bold public schedule removes the option for quiet iteration. If the 1997 target is missed, or if the initial units fail, the program becomes a public relations disaster. A more prudent approach would be a limited fleet trial to validate durability and cost structures before committing to mass production tooling. The current proposal skips the validation phase required for such novel technology.

While the vision of leading the industry is compelling, the execution plan ignores the fundamental engineering and market realities of the mid-1990s. The risks of cost overruns, reliability failures, and market rejection are too high to justify a full corporate commitment at this stage. The company should not bet its future on unproven technology without a proven demand signal or a fallback position.

DECISION: NO-GO
