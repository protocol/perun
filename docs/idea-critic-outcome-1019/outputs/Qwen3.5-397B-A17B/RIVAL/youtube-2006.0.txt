This proposal represents a high-stakes, asymmetric bet on the future of digital media. The target is the clear market leader in a nascent but rapidly exploding category: online video. At 20 months old, it has achieved dominant cultural traction with minimal resources, proving product-market fit far beyond the buyer's internal efforts.

The $1.65 billion price tag is staggering for a company with no revenue and massive liabilities. However, in technology shifts, being second often means being last. The buyer's current video service is trailing; organic growth is unlikely to overtake the incumbent's network effects. Acquiring the leader instantly solves the distribution problem and captures the defining user behavior of the next decade.

The risks are severe but manageable with the right strategy. Copyright litigation is a certainty, but it is also a barrier to entry that will crush smaller competitors who cannot afford legal battles or licensing deals. A giant acquirer can absorb these costs and negotiate industry-wide licenses that others cannot. Bandwidth costs will drop over time due to infrastructure efficiencies and Moore's Law, turning a current liability into a manageable operational expense.

The ad model is unproven for long-form video, but the volume of engagement provides the data necessary to crack it. Traditional TV advertising is ripe for disruption; owning the primary destination for online video positions the buyer to lead that transition rather than react to it.

Passing on this deal preserves short-term cash but cedes the long-term platform war. The cost of *not* buying is the risk of irrelevance in a video-first internet. The premium paid is not for current earnings, but for the exclusive option on the future of television. The strategic necessity of owning the primary video destination outweighs the financial noise of the present.

DECISION: GO
