The proposal attempts to transplant a high-margin, brand-loyal electronics model onto a low-margin, promotion-addicted department store base. This is a category error.

Electronics buyers value innovation and specific brand features; they tolerate stable pricing because products evolve rapidly. Department store shoppers in this demographic are trained for the hunt. Their loyalty is to the discount, not the merchant. Removing coupons does not signal "fairness"; it signals a price hike. The psychological contract is broken immediately.

The risk of "no pilot" is catastrophic here. In electronics, the product sells itself. In department stores, the *mechanism* of the sale (the coupon, the event) is the product. Eliminating it chain-wide without validating whether the core base can be retrained is negligence. If the hypothesis fails, there is no fallback; the brand has alienated its only remaining customers before acquiring new ones.

The "branded in-store shops" concept requires massive capital expenditure (CapEx) during a sales decline. This burns cash while revenue likely plummets due to pricing confusion. The electronics CEO is solving for a "cool factor" that the department store customer does not value, ignoring the operational reality that this chain runs on volume driven by perceived savings.

A viable strategy would have been a hybrid approach: test everyday pricing on a specific category (e.g., basics) in a limited region while maintaining promotions on discretionary goods. Or, phase the transition over 24 months, using data to migrate customers rather than shocking them.

This plan assumes customer behavior is rational and malleable. It is not. It is habitual. Breaking the habit without a safety net invites a death spiral. The pace is too fast, the scope is too broad, and the analogy to electronics is flawed. The proposal ignores the fundamental unit economics and behavioral psychology of the existing business.

DECISION: NO-GO
