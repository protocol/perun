This proposal represents a classic "innovator's dilemma" pivot, trading guaranteed, high-margin licensing revenue for uncertain, high-growth direct-to-consumer scale. The strategic logic holds only if the long-term value of owning the customer relationship and data exceeds the immediate cash flow from licensing.

**The Bull Case:**
Owning the platform captures 100% of the subscription lifetime value rather than a fixed licensing fee. It prevents rivals from using your content to build competing moats. If the target of tens of millions of subscribers is met, the recurring revenue model commands a significantly higher market valuation multiple than traditional media licensing. The low price point is a necessary customer acquisition cost (CAC) to disrupt the incumbent's dominance quickly.

**The Bear Case:**
The risks are existential. Licensing revenue is immediate profit; the proposed path requires massive, multi-year cash burn with no guarantee of success. Building a global technology platform and recommendation engine is outside the company's core competency, introducing high execution risk. Cannibalizing existing cable and theatrical windows could destroy current cash flows before the new service becomes profitable. If subscriber growth stalls below the break-even point, the company faces a "wor of both worlds" scenario: diminished licensing income and a bleeding standalone service.

**Critical Assessment:**
The proposal assumes the market will remain static while the company burns cash to catch up. However, it underestimates the difficulty of changing consumer behavior and the technical debt of building a streaming infrastructure from scratch. The "low price" strategy compresses margins, meaning the volume required to break even is exceptionally high. Without a clear path to profitability beyond "build audience first," this is a speculative gamble with the company's core cash cow.

The loss of licensing revenue creates an immediate hole in the P&L that must be funded by debt or existing reserves. If the technology platform falters or content cadence slips, churn will spike, and the low price will not retain users. The cannibalization risk is real; moving windows to streaming often depresses theatrical box office and cable affiliate fees, which are currently highly profitable.

While the direction toward direct-to-consumer is inevitable for media survival, the specific terms—deep discounts, unlimited burn, and immediate withdrawal from rivals—lack necessary guardrails. A phased approach retaining some licensing revenue while testing the tech stack would de-risk the transition. As proposed, the speed and total commitment expose the conglomerate to catastrophic failure if execution lags.

DECISION: NO-GO
