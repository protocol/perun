This proposal commits a fundamental strategic error: it conflates sip-by-sip preference with brand value. The data confirms the new formula wins on immediate sweetness in blind tests, but these tests explicitly excluded the single most valuable asset—the brand itself. By withholding the fact that the original would be removed, the research measured taste in a vacuum, ignoring the emotional equity, nostalgia, and identity built over a century.

Consumers do not buy cola solely for flavor; they buy it for the feeling of continuity and heritage. Removing the original formula entirely transforms a product extension into a hostile severance of that relationship. The risks identified—emotional attachment, brand heritage, and lack of a fallback—are not minor concerns; they are existential threats. Loyalists who define themselves by the original taste will not migrate; they will revolt. Without a fallback option, the company forces a binary choice: accept the new taste or abandon the brand entirely. This alienates the core base that provides stable revenue, chasing a hypothetical gain in "preference" that may not translate to long-term loyalty.

Furthermore, the bottler network relies on predictability. A total replacement disrupts inventory, marketing, and consumer trust simultaneously. The absence of a parallel launch strategy (keeping both) suggests a dangerous overconfidence in the data's completeness. History suggests that when you remove a cultural touchstone, the backlash is disproportionate to the sensory improvement. The data is right about the taste, but wrong about the product. The product is the taste plus the history. You cannot test the history in a blind sip.

To proceed as proposed is to gamble the entire franchise on a metric that explicitly ignored the brand's primary driver of value. The risk of permanent brand damage and market share collapse outweighs the potential gain of winning a blind taste test. The strategy must include maintaining the original as a legacy option to preserve brand equity while introducing the new formula as a choice, not a mandate.

DECISION: NO-GO
