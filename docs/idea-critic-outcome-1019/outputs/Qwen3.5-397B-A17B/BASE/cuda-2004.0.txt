The proposal asks a company dependent on gaming revenue to fundamentally alter its mass-market architecture for a non-existent commercial market. The plan requires adding silicon area, building a complete compiler stack, and funding academic programs, all of which directly increase the unit cost of every consumer chip sold. In 2004, the target market for general scientific computing on these chips is purely experimental, relying on hacked APIs with no committed paying customers.

The risks identified are severe and immediate. Increasing the cost of every unit threatens competitiveness in the core gaming sector, where price and performance are paramount. The company is currently engaged in an intense rivalry; diverting engineering resources to build unproven general-purpose features could cause a loss of market share to competitors focused strictly on gaming dominance. Furthermore, without a clear path to monetization, the investment in toolchains and university funding represents a significant cash outflow with no guaranteed return.

While the long-term vision of programmable graphics chips has theoretical merit, executing this as a mandatory feature on all consumer hardware in 2004 is premature. The absence of a large paying market means the company would bear the entire burden of ecosystem development without immediate revenue to offset the increased manufacturing costs. The distraction from the core business during a critical competitive race poses an existential threat that outweighs the potential future benefits. The proposal lacks a phased approach, pilot customers, or a mechanism to validate demand before committing to a costly, multi-year overhaul of the entire product line. Approving this now would jeopardize the company's primary revenue stream based on speculation rather than validated market need.

DECISION: NO-GO
