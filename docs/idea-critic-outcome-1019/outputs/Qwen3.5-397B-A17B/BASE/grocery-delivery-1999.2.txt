The proposal demands an immediate commitment of $1 billion to build highly automated warehouses and a delivery fleet across over a dozen new markets. This expansion is planned before the company has established a single operating track record or validated unit economics in its initial metropolitan area, which only began accepting orders in June 1999.

The grocery sector is inherently characterized by thin margins, making operational efficiency and density critical for survival. The current plan ignores these fundamentals by prioritizing rapid geographic expansion over learning. Scaling into new cities without understanding the cost per order, customer acquisition costs, or retention rates in the first city creates an unacceptable level of risk. The capital intensity of constructing massive, automated facilities locks the company into a rigid cost structure before proving that customers will consistently utilize the service enough to justify such infrastructure.

Furthermore, the requirement for high customer density to make 30-minute delivery slots economically viable cannot be assumed; it must be proven through data gathered in the initial market. Expanding prematurely dilutes management focus and burns through venture capital on unproven assumptions. If the unit economics in the first city prove unfavorable, the company will be left with billions in stranded assets across multiple regions with no path to profitability. The risks of capital intensity, combined with low margins and a lack of operational history, dictate a cautious approach. Resources should be concentrated on mastering the first market, optimizing the delivery model, and achieving positive unit economics before considering any expansion. Approving this proposal now would likely lead to catastrophic financial failure.

DECISION: NO-GO
