The claim is that acquiring the dominant code-hosting service for $7.5B in stock will secure the buyer's open-source pivot despite cultural hostility and target instability.

Steelman: Only immediate ownership prevents a competitor from capturing the platform, forcing the industry standard onto the buyer's ecosystem before the target collapses under leadership void.

However, the attack reveals fatal flaws in the current proposal structure. First, the "run independently" promise contradicts the buyer's historical hostility; the vocal developer community already distrusts the buyer, and a free competitor is actively marketing this fear. Without an immediate, binding technical guarantee (not just corporate policy) against integration or data mining, mass defection to the free alternative is the probable baseline, rendering the asset worthless post-acquisition. Second, paying a premium in stock for a loss-making company without a permanent CEO transfers massive value to existing shareholders while the buyer absorbs all execution risk. The lack of leadership suggests internal rot that $7.5B cannot fix if the community abandons ship during the transition. Third, antitrust scrutiny is guaranteed given the target's dominance; regulatory delay could freeze integration precisely when decisive action is needed to retain users.

The proposal fails the "do nothing" comparison. If the target is truly failing due to lack of leadership, its valuation should drop, allowing a cheaper entry later or a hire-based rescue. If the community is ready to flee, no amount of "independent" branding from a historically hostile owner will stop them without concrete, verifiable product safeguards which are absent here. The cost of delay is lower than the cost of overpaying for a collapsing community asset.

This is a classic "empire building" move disguised as strategic defense. The risks of developer defection and cultural clash are not mitigated; they are assumed away by a structural promise that history suggests will be broken. The price is too high for a loss-making entity with no captain and a mutinous crew.

DECISION: NO-GO
