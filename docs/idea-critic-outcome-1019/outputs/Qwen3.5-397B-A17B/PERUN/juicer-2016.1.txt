**Steelman:** A premium, connected juicer creates a recurring revenue moat by guaranteeing freshness and convenience, justifying a high upfront hardware subsidy through lifetime subscription value.

**Attack:** The unit economics and market fit are fatally flawed.
1.  **Price Dissonance:** At $699, the device costs ~3-4x a high-end traditional juicer. Consumers will not subsidize hardware for a subscription that likely costs more per ounce than fresh produce or third-party cold-press delivery.
2.  **Unnecessary Friction:** Requiring Wi-Fi to verify "freshness" of a physical bag is a solution in search of a problem. It introduces failure points (connectivity loss) without tangible user benefit, signaling "rent-seeking" rather than utility.
3.  **Market Size vs. Burn:** "Tens of millions" raised implies a need for massive scale. The niche of users willing to pay a premium for proprietary bags *and* tolerate internet dependence for a countertop appliance is vanishingly small. This is a "one-way door" with high manufacturing sunk costs and low reversibility.
4.  **Better Way:** Sell a high-quality open-system juicer or partner with existing grocery delivery services. Do not lock users into a single-supplier ecosystem for a commodity ingredient.

**Pre-mortem:** The device launches, early adopters balk at the ongoing bag costs compared to local juice bars, the app connectivity bugs frustrate users, and the company burns cash on inventory and hardware subsidies before reaching critical mass. The "freshness check" is perceived as anti-consumer DRM.

**Kill Criteria:** If the projected subscription margin does not cover the hardware subsidy within 12 months per unit, the model fails. If the addressable market (users willing to pay >$5/juice equivalent) is under 100k households, the raise is insufficient.

**Verdict:** The proposal relies on consumers accepting higher costs and lower flexibility for a feature (app-checked freshness) that adds no real value. The friction outweighs the convenience.

DECISION: NO-GO
