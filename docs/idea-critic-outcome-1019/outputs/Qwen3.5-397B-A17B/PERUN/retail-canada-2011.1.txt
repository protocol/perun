The claim is that rapid, large-scale international expansion via lease acquisition and new systems will succeed despite operational inexperience and entrenched competition. The steelman: leveraging existing capital to seize 130–150 sites quickly creates immediate scale, blocking competitors and capturing market share before local prices adjust.

However, the "better way" analysis reveals fatal flaws in the proposed approach. Doing nothing preserves capital; a phased pilot (5–10 stores) tests cross-border logistics and bilingual labeling without systemic risk. The proposal ignores Chesterton's Fence: the incumbent's dominance suggests hidden barriers (regulatory, cultural, or supply chain) that a "big bang" approach will hit simultaneously rather than iteratively.

The pre-mortem is clear: the new inventory system, unproven in this specific cross-border context, fails to synchronize with the new supply chain during the rapid rollout. With 130 stores opening nearly at once, a single systemic glitch causes massive stockouts or overstock across the entire network, burning cash and reputation before a fix is possible. The cost of delay (waiting to learn) is negligible compared to the cost of failure (collapsing under unmanageable complexity).

The evidence provided highlights "risks raised" regarding pace, unproven systems, and price perception, yet the proposal proceeds as if these are manageable details rather than existential threats. Expanding into a market with higher prices on some goods contradicts the "discount" value proposition unless the cost structure is guaranteed to be lower, which is unverified. The plan assumes the retailer can learn complex cross-border logistics, regulation, and labeling while simultaneously executing a hyper-aggressive build-out. This violates the basic principle of separating learning phases from scaling phases.

The strongest attack survived? None. The proposal fails to address how the unproven inventory system will handle the specific complexities of cross-border trade during a mass rollout. There is no kill criterion defined for the pilot phase because there is no pilot; it is all-or-nothing. The lack of a reversible, low-cost entry point makes this a one-way door with a high probability of catastrophic failure. The "loyal domestic following" offers no shield against local execution failures.

DECISION: NO-GO
