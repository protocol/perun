The claim is that a $6 billion cash acquisition of an ad-serving platform is the necessary and sufficient move to catch the search leader in display advertising. The steelman: owning the full stack prevents the competitor from monopolizing publisher inventory and instantly grants scale no organic build could match by 2008.

However, the skeptic identifies fatal flaws. First, the price represents a massive premium in a fast-changing market; if display shifts away from current serving tech (e.g., toward video or rich media not covered by this platform), the asset becomes a stranded cost. Second, the "conflict with existing agency customers" is not a minor hurdle but a structural revenue threat; if major agencies flee the buyer's ecosystem due to perceived competition, the net gain could be negative. Third, the "do nothing" or "partner" alternative is ignored: licensing technology or building internally avoids the $6 billion cash outflow and integration risk while the market definition remains fluid.

The pre-mortem reveals a likely failure mode: integration paralysis causes agency defections while the technology becomes obsolete within 18 months, leaving the buyer with a $6 billion write-down and a weakened core search business. The cost of delay is low compared to the cost of a wrong, irreversible bet; waiting six months to see how the competitor's integration fares offers valuable data without committing capital.

The kill criteria are missing. There is no defined metric for agency retention post-acquisition, no cap on integration costs, and no pivot trigger if display CPMs shift. Without a verified path to retaining agency partners or a guarantee of technology longevity, the risk profile is unacceptable. The proposal assumes the current market structure is static, which contradicts the "fast-changing" admission.

This is a one-way door decision with insufficient evidence that the premium buys sustainable advantage rather than immediate obsolescence and channel conflict. The safer path is to wait, observe the competitor's stumble, and acquire smaller, specialized tech later.

DECISION: NO-GO
