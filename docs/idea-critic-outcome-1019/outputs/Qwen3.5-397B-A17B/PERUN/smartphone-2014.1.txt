The proposal fails the "better-way" and "fit" tests immediately. The market is a duopoly where network effects dictate value; launching a forked OS without mainstream apps creates a fatal utility gap that no amount of hardware novelty can bridge. The "3D-like display" is a solution searching for a problem, adding cost and battery drain without solving a core user need, while the "one-touch purchase" feature is a nice-to-have that cannot compensate for the inability to run essential daily applications.

The pricing strategy is incoherent: asking flagship prices for a device with a fragmented ecosystem and carrier exclusivity ignores the primary driver of smartphone adoption in 2014—app availability. The "saturated market" risk is understated; it is a barrier to entry that requires overwhelming advantage, not niche differentiation. A "do nothing" or "partner" approach (licensing the dominant OS) offers significantly lower risk and higher potential adoption than building a walled garden from scratch.

The pre-mortem is clear: the device launches to initial curiosity, but daily usability friction causes rapid churn. Developers ignore the platform due to low share, reinforcing the app gap. The carrier, seeing poor sales, drops support. The investment yields no strategic foothold and distracts from the successful e-reader and tablet lines. The "kill criteria" here are already met by the premise: entering a two-horse race with a broken leg (no apps) at full price is a guaranteed loss. There is no credible path to the "north star" of retail integration if the device itself is rejected by the mass market.

This is a classic case of overestimating hardware differentiation while underestimating platform ecosystem lock-in. The resources required to sustain this fork would be better spent on deepening the tablet experience or expanding the e-reader lineup.

DECISION: NO-GO
