The claim is that replacing a coupon-dependent model with everyday low pricing (EDLP) and branded shops will reverse sales declines for a mid-market department store chain. The steelman: eliminating promotional complexity reduces operational costs and restores margin integrity, while branded shops modernize the experience to attract broader traffic.

However, the skeptic hat identifies a fatal mismatch between the customer base and the proposed strategy. The customers are explicitly described as "older" and "coupon-driven," with most historical sales occurring at a discount. This behavior indicates that perceived value is tied to the *act* of saving via a coupon, not just the final price point. Removing coupons removes the psychological trigger for purchase for this specific demographic.

The "better-way" analysis suggests a phased approach. A "do nothing" scenario maintains the status quo but preserves the existing, albeit shrinking, revenue stream. A superior alternative to an immediate chain-wide rollout is a pilot program in a limited number of stores. This would validate whether the specific customer base can be retrained or if the transition causes an immediate, unrecoverable collapse in foot traffic and volume.

The pre-mortem reveals a high probability of catastrophic failure. If the core customer base stops shopping because the "game" of couponing is removed, sales volume will plummet before any new customer segment can be acquired to replace them. The proposal admits "no pilot" and plans to change the "entire chain at once." This is a one-way door with immense downside risk and no verified evidence that the current customers will accept the new value proposition. The pace is too fast, and the assumption that brand prestige alone will drive volume without the promotional hook is unproven and likely false for this demographic.

The strongest attack survived is the operational efficiency gain from EDLP; however, efficiency gains are irrelevant if the top-line revenue collapses due to customer alienation. Without a pilot to prove demand elasticity, the plan bets the entire company on an unverified hypothesis regarding customer behavior. The risk of total revenue failure outweighs the potential margin benefits.

DECISION: NO-GO
