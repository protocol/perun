The claim is that a 1999 grocery startup with no operating history should immediately commit $1B to build automated warehouses in a dozen cities before validating unit economics.

**Steelman:** Rapid infrastructure deployment creates an unassailable moat by locking in real estate and capacity before competitors, leveraging abundant venture capital to buy speed in a low-margin market where density dictates survival.

**Attack:** This strategy ignores the fundamental fragility of the premise. Groceries have razor-thin margins; a single miscalculation in customer density or order frequency renders massive fixed costs fatal. The proposal explicitly admits "no operating track record" and unknown "unit economics." Building highly automated warehouses assumes a stable, predictable order volume that does not yet exist. In 1999, consumer trust in online payments and fresh food delivery is unproven. If the first city reveals that customers prefer wider slots or that spoilage rates are higher than modeled, the company is left with a dozen white elephants and a fleet of idle trucks. The cost of delay (waiting for data) is negligible compared to the cost of error (bankruptcy from over-expansion).

**Better Way:** Deploy capital to validate the model in the first city only. Use third-party logistics or manual picking to prove density and margin requirements before pouring concrete. Expand to the second city only after the first hits profitability targets.

**Kill Criteria:** The proposal lacks any stop condition. There is no metric defined for success in the first market before triggering the billion-dollar spend. Without a verified unit economic model, this is not a business plan; it is a gamble with near-certain ruin due to fixed-cost bloat.

**Verdict:** The risk is catastrophic and irreversible. The "door" is one-way; once $1B is spent on specialized automation, it cannot be recovered. The lack of data makes the bet blind.

DECISION: NO-GO
