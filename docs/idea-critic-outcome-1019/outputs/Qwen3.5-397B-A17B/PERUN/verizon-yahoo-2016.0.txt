The claim is that acquiring a declining portal for $4.8 billion will create a scaled mobile media competitor to dominant ad platforms. The steelman: combining two legacy assets creates critical mass in mobile data and content, leveraging the carrier's distribution to reverse the target's decline before it becomes worthless.

However, the skeptic hat reveals fatal flaws. First, the "scale" argument ignores the trajectory: both assets are losing traffic and revenue. Combining two declining curves does not create growth; it creates a larger decline. Second, the acquirer has zero history in consumer media, while the target suffers from repeated management failures, suggesting deep cultural or structural rot that a wireless carrier lacks the expertise to fix. Third, the $4.8 billion price tag for a "declining" business with falling ad revenue implies paying a premium for assets that are actively deteriorating, destroying capital rather than deploying it.

The better-way analysis shows superior alternatives. The capital could be invested directly in building a modern mobile-first ad stack, acquiring smaller agile tech firms, or returning cash to shareholders. "Doing nothing" avoids the massive integration risk and the certainty of overpaying for a dying model. The cost of delay is low because the ad market is shifting to programmatic and search dominance, not portal aggregation; waiting avoids catching a falling knife.

The pre-mortem is clear: one year post-acquisition, integration chaos stalls product development, ad revenue continues its double-digit decline, and the carrier writes down the goodwill. This is a one-way door with irreversible capital loss. The evidence provided—falling traffic, falling revenue, management instability, and acquirer inexperience—overwhelmingly contradicts the success hypothesis. There is no verified path where this specific combination beats the status quo or alternative investments. The kill criteria for any such deal (revenue stabilization, management retention) are already failed by the target's current state.

This proposal relies on the fallacy that size equals competitiveness in a quality-driven ad market. It commits to a high-cost, high-risk integration of two mismatched, declining entities.

DECISION: NO-GO
