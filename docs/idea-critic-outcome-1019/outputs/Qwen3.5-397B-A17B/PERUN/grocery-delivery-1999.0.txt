The claim is that a pre-revenue grocery startup should commit $1 billion to automated warehouses and rapid expansion before validating unit economics in its first market. The steelman argues that speed creates an insurmountable moat, securing real estate and market share before competitors react, justifying the burn with future density efficiencies.

However, this fails the "what must be true" test. It requires assuming that 1999 internet adoption and logistics technology can immediately support high-density, low-margin delivery at a scale that justifies billion-dollar automation. Historically, early e-commerce grocery ventures failed precisely due to the mismatch between projected density and actual customer acquisition costs. The proposal ignores the "do nothing" alternative of iterating in one city to prove the model; if the unit economics are negative in the first city, scaling them across a dozen simply multiplies the loss rate.

The pre-mortem is clear: the company runs out of cash within 18 months because the anticipated customer density never materializes, leaving billions stranded in specialized, unusable infrastructure. This is a one-way door; once capital is deployed into concrete and custom automation, it cannot be recovered or pivoted easily. The cost of delay is negligible compared to the cost of premature scaling; waiting six months to validate margins in the initial metro area reduces the risk of total capital destruction without sacrificing long-term viability if the model works.

The strongest attack is the lack of verified demand. No evidence exists that customers will adopt online grocery shopping at the frequency required to fill these automated warehouses. Without a proven track record, committing such vast resources is not strategy; it is gambling the entire venture on unproven assumptions. The kill criterion is simple: do not proceed without positive unit economics in the pilot city. Since this condition is not met, the proposal must be rejected.

DECISION: NO-GO
