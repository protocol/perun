The proposal assumes the $36 billion premium is justified solely by the target's business customer base, ignoring the massive technical debt of integrating incompatible networks. The steelman argues that acquiring the push-to-talk monopoly instantly secures the high-margin enterprise sector, which organic growth cannot match quickly enough. However, the "better way" analysis reveals a fatal flaw: running parallel networks indefinitely destroys margins, while migrating legacy users to a new standard risks churning the very customers being purchased.

The skeptic identifies a critical unsupported claim: that integration costs will not exceed the synergies. In 2005, merging CDMA/GSM or iDEN/GSM architectures requires replacing handsets and tower infrastructure, a capital expenditure likely understated in the deal thesis. The pre-mortem shows the combined entity becoming a "zombie carrier," burdened by debt and technical complexity while agile competitors launch data-first services on unified networks. The goodwill asset is fragile; if integration fails, it becomes an immediate write-down.

The kill criteria are missing. There is no defined threshold for integration spending caps, no timeline for shutting down the legacy network, and no metric for customer migration retention rates. Without a "go-if" condition capping the integration budget or mandating a specific technology sunset date, the deal is an open-ended money pit. The risk of cultural and technical collision is extreme, and the "do nothing" alternative (building enterprise features on the existing network) offers lower risk with slower but safer returns.

The strongest attack survived is the immediate revenue boost from the target's base. Yet, this revenue is at risk the moment integration begins. If the push-to-talk service degrades during migration, the core value proposition vanishes. The proposal treats the acquisition as a financial engineering win rather than an operational nightmare. Given the incompatibility, the massive goodwill exposure, and the lack of defined kill criteria for integration costs, the risk profile is unacceptable as stated. The deal structure invites catastrophic value destruction if the technical integration hits any friction, which is highly probable.

DECISION: NO-GO
