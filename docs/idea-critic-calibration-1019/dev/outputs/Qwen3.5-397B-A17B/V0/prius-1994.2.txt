Steelman: Committing now secures a decade-long technological moat and defines the brand as the future of mobility before regulators force the issue. However, the proposal fails the "fit" and "evidence" gates. With fuel cheap and no regulatory mandate, the market signal is absent; customers rarely pay a premium for efficiency they do not financially need. The "novel" power-split transmission and unproven battery durability create massive warranty exposure that could bankrupt the program post-launch. 

Better-way analysis suggests a "do nothing" or "limited pilot" approach is superior. Releasing a niche volume to test real-world battery degradation and consumer willingness to pay preserves capital while gathering data. A full mass-production commitment with a bold public schedule is a one-way door that locks the company into high fixed costs before the technology is de-risked. The cost of delay is negligible given the lack of competitive threat; being first to fail is worse than being second to succeed.

The pre-mortem is clear: The car launches, battery packs fail prematurely in hot climates, warranty claims explode, and the novelty wears off when fuel prices remain low. The company is forced to buy back vehicles or absorb unsustainable losses, damaging the core brand. There is no kill criterion defined here to stop the bleed if early durability tests show <80% capacity retention after 50,000 miles. Without a mandated regulatory driver or a proven battery lifecycle, this is a speculative bet with asymmetric downside. The proposal asks for a binary go/no-go on mass production without the necessary evidence of durability or demand. It must be revised to a limited pilot program with strict durability gates before any mass-production commitment.

DECISION: NO-GO
