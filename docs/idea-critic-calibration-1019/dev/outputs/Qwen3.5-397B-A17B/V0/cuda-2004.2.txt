**Steelman:** Embedding general-purpose compute capability into mass-market GPUs creates a ubiquitous, low-cost supercomputing fabric, unlocking massive parallel processing for science and AI before competitors realize the shift, eventually creating a dominant new revenue stream beyond gaming.

**Attack:** The proposal demands raising unit costs and diverting critical engineering resources from a fierce, existential war for gaming market share to build a toolchain for a market that currently generates zero revenue. The "niche" academic demand does not justify the capital expenditure of adding silicon area to every consumer chip. If the anticipated explosion in parallel computing workloads (AI, physics simulation) fails to materialize or migrates to specialized CPUs, the company will have permanently degraded its price competitiveness in its only proven market without gaining a foothold in a new one. The risk is not just wasted R&D, but a loss of the core gaming franchise due to distraction and higher prices.

**Better Way:** Wait for clear market signals. Develop programmability first on high-margin workstation cards where early adopters exist, avoiding the cost penalty on mass-market units. License technology to universities without baking it into every consumer silicon die.

**Fit & Pre-mortem:** This contradicts a strategy focused on winning the immediate graphics race. In a pre-mortem: Two years later, the core gaming division has lost share to a rival offering cheaper, faster gaming-only chips; the software stack is unfinished; and scientific users remain a tiny fraction of sales, unable to cover the increased BOM cost. The "one-way door" of adding silicon area cannot be undone once fabricated.

**Verdict:** The proposal bets the company's current profitability on a speculative future with no committed customers, directly threatening its survival in its primary market. The cost of delay is negligible compared to the cost of failure.

DECISION: NO-GO
