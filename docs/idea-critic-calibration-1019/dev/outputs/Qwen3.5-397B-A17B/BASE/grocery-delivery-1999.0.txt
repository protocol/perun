The proposal demands an immediate commitment of approximately $1 billion to construct highly automated regional warehouses and acquire a delivery fleet across over a dozen new metropolitan areas. This expansion is planned before the company has established a viable operating track record or validated unit economics in its single current market, which only began accepting orders in June 1999.

The grocery sector is inherently characterized by thin profit margins, making operational efficiency and density critical for survival. The current plan ignores these fundamental constraints by prioritizing rapid geographic scaling over learning. Expanding into new cities without first proving that the 30-minute delivery slot model can be profitable in the initial location introduces extreme financial risk. The capital intensity required for such massive infrastructure, combined with the unproven nature of the business model, creates a scenario where failure in early markets could drain resources before corrections are possible.

Furthermore, the reliance on high customer density to make low-margin deliveries economical cannot be assumed across diverse new markets without empirical data. Proceeding with this level of investment based solely on venture capital availability, rather than operational proof, disregards the specific risks of capital intensity and unverified demand. The lack of an operating history means there is no baseline to predict whether the proposed automation and logistics will function as intended at scale. Approving this now would lock the company into a rigid, expensive infrastructure before understanding the true cost to serve a customer. Prudent management requires validating the model in the first city, optimizing margins, and then scaling gradually. The proposed pace and magnitude of investment are disproportionate to the current level of knowledge and validation.

DECISION: NO-GO
