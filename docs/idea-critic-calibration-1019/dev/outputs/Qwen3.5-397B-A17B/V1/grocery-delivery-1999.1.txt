The claim is that a 1999 grocery startup should immediately commit $1 billion to build automated warehouses in a dozen cities before validating unit economics in its single launch market.

Steelman: Aggressive infrastructure pre-commitment secures first-mover dominance and prevents supply bottlenecks during rapid scaling.

However, this fails basic survival tests. First, the "do nothing" alternative (organic growth in the first city) dominates because it preserves capital while the core hypothesis—whether customers will accept online grocery margins and density constraints—remains unproven. Second, the pre-mortem is catastrophic: if the first city fails to achieve profitability due to low margins or logistical complexity (highly likely given the 30-minute slot constraint), the company is left with billions in stranded, specialized assets across eleven other cities. Third, the cost of delay is negligible compared to the cost of error; waiting one quarter to validate the first market costs little but prevents total insolvency.

The proposal ignores the "low-margin" reality of groceries. High fixed costs from automation require immediate, massive volume to break even, creating a deadly feedback loop if adoption lags. There is no operating track record to justify such scale. The risks (capital intensity, thin margins, density needs) are not just noted; they are fatal flaws when combined with a "build it all now" strategy. A safer, reversible path exists: lease smaller spaces, use manual picking initially, and expand only after proving the model in the first metro.

This is a one-way door decision with no evidence to support the leap. The lack of a pilot in the first city before expanding to a dozen is a concrete failure scenario: the company runs out of cash before learning how to make a single delivery profitable. No kill criteria or leading metrics are offered to mitigate this; the plan is simply to spend everything immediately.

DECISION: NO-GO
