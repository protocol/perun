The claim is that a differentiated display and shopping feature justify launching a flagship-priced smartphone on a forked OS with a limited app store and single-carrier distribution in a saturated duopoly.

**Skeptic:** The premise relies on hardware novelty overcoming ecosystem gravity. In 2014, mainstream app availability is a primary purchase driver; lacking key apps (social, messaging, banking) at a flagship price creates an immediate value mismatch. Head-tracking 3D is a niche gimmick, not a sustained utility, and "one-touch shopping" is a feature, not a platform substitute. The "unsubsidized-equivalent" pricing removes the carrier's primary sales lever (subsidy contracts), making the device expensive for consumers while offering less utility than competitors.

**Better-way:** The "do nothing" option preserves capital. A cheaper path is licensing the shopping feature as an app on existing dominant platforms, reaching 100% of the market instantly without hardware risk. Alternatively, a low-cost device strategy (matching their tablet success) would target price-sensitive users, but the proposal explicitly chooses the high-end segment where brand loyalty to the OS is highest.

**Kill-criteria & Pre-mortem:** Assume failure: The device launches, early adopters try the 3D feature, then return it due to app incompatibility. Carrier sales stall because reps cannot sell a $600+ phone without Instagram or Snapchat. The kill criterion is clear: if the device cannot secure top-tier apps within six months, the platform dies. The cost of delay is low; the market is not moving toward 3D screens. This is a one-way door with massive inventory risk. The proposal fails to address how it solves the "app gap" fatal flaw. The evidence of market saturation and duopoly dominance suggests this specific combination (high price, low apps, niche feature) has no viable path to scale.

DECISION: NO-GO
