The claim is that a closed, curated native app store will succeed despite prior executive skepticism of native apps and existing open rival ecosystems. The steelman: a single controlled storefront balances developer reach with product integrity, turning security and stability risks into a premium brand differentiator while capturing revenue via a 30% cut.

Skeptic analysis reveals a critical tension: the CEO previously argued web apps were sufficient. Reversing this stance without a clear catalyst risks perceived strategic flailing. However, the existence of rival open ecosystems creates a "do nothing" failure mode where the platform becomes a second-class citizen. The 30% fee is high compared to open distribution, potentially stifling early adoption, but funds the necessary review process to mitigate the stated security risks. Carrier objections are a real political hurdle; bypassing their services could jeopardize distribution deals essential for a young hardware maker.

Better-way analysis suggests a hybrid approach: allow web apps for low-risk utilities to appease carriers and maintain speed, while reserving the store for high-value native experiences. Yet, fragmenting the developer message might weaken the ecosystem effect. The proposed "review every app" model is the only viable path to guarantee the "polish" the company prizes, whereas open distribution would almost certainly degrade the user experience given the security constraints.

Pre-mortem: The review process becomes a bottleneck, angering developers and slowing innovation, causing the store to launch with barren shelves. Alternatively, carriers block the phone entirely due to tethering or VoIP apps. The one-way door is locking out open sideloading; if the curated model fails, regaining developer trust is difficult.

Kill criteria: If the review backlog exceeds two weeks within the first quarter, or if carrier partnerships are threatened by specific app categories, the model must pivot to faster automated checks or limited sideloading. The leading metric is not download volume, but the ratio of approved high-quality apps to total submissions, ensuring the "polish" promise holds.

The risk of having no ecosystem outweighs the operational cost of a walled garden. The 30% cut is justified as a quality assurance tax. The reversal on web apps is a necessary adaptation to market reality, not a flaw, provided the curation is ruthless.

DECISION: GO
