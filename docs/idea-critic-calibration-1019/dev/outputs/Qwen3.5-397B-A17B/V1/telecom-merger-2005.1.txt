The claim is that merging with a smaller, incompatible carrier creates sufficient value to justify a $36 billion price tag and massive integration risk. The steelman: acquiring a sticky, high-margin business segment (push-to-talk) justifies the cost if retention holds and migration is feasible.

However, the attack reveals fatal flaws in the 2005 context. First, **technology incompatibility** is not merely an engineering hurdle but a strategic anchor; running dual networks indefinitely destroys margin, while forcing migration risks churning the very "sticky" customers being purchased. Second, the **$36 billion valuation** likely prices in synergies that assume smooth integration, ignoring the historical failure rate of merging incompatible legacy stacks. Third, **goodwill risk**: if the acquired technology is "limited for data," the buyer is paying a premium for a dying asset in a rapidly evolving data market, potentially stranding capital.

Alternatives exist: building a native push-to-talk solution on the buyer's existing infrastructure or partnering via API would avoid the $36 billion outlay and integration nightmare. The "do nothing" option preserves capital for compatible data growth. The cost of delay is low compared to the cost of a botched integration that alienates the core business base.

The pre-mortem is clear: integration drags on, business customers flee due to service degradation during migration, and the buyer is left with a bloated debt load and obsolete hardware. The one-way door nature of this merger (once networks are tangled, unwinding is impossible) demands near-certainty of success, which the incompatibility factor negates.

Without a proven, low-risk migration path for the specific push-to-talk users and a valuation adjustment for the technical debt, the risk of value destruction outweighs the potential revenue gain. The proposal relies on hope rather than a verified mechanism to harmonize incompatible technologies without customer loss.

DECISION: NO-GO
