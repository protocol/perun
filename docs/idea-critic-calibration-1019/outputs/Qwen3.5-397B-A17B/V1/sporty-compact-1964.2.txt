**Steelman:** Leveraging existing compact mechanics to rapidly deliver a stylish, customizable coupe at a low entry price captures an emerging demographic shift toward sporty affordability before competitors react.

**Skeptic:** The primary risk is brand perception; sharing parts with a "dull" economy car could stigmatize the new model as a cheap upcycle rather than a genuine sportscar. However, the proposed "long hood, short deck" styling specifically counteracts the visual language of economy cars, decoupling the aesthetic from the mechanical donor. The "unproven demand" fear is mitigated by the low base price, which lowers the barrier to trial, and the extensive options list, which allows revenue capture from enthusiastic buyers without inflating the sticker price that deters entry.

**Better-Way:** Alternatives include developing a dedicated chassis (too slow, too costly for an unproven segment) or waiting for market data (cedes first-mover advantage to rivals). Doing nothing ignores the specific, time-sensitive influx of young buyers with disposable income. The proposed path is the only one balancing speed, cost, and market fit.

**Kill-Criteria & Pre-mortem:** If the styling fails to hide the economy roots, sales will lag among image-conscious youth. The kill criterion is clear: if initial launch month sales do not exceed the volume of the donor compact car by at least 20% within the first quarter, the marketing narrative has failed, and production should pivot to standard trims. The heavy ad spend is a two-way door; it can be halted immediately if early indicators show the "sporty" message isn't resonating, limiting downside. The risk of looking "cheap" is outweighed by the opportunity to define a new category. The proposal explicitly addresses the risks with design differentiation and a flexible pricing structure. No fatal flaws exist; the risks are managed by the very mechanics of the proposal (low base price, high margin options, distinct styling).

DECISION: GO
