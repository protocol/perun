The claim is that launching two social-networking phones on a new, app-less OS via a single carrier with a high-cost data plan will successfully capture the teenage market in 2010.

Steelman: Leveraging a popular messaging brand and exclusive carrier placement creates a focused, low-friction entry for teens who prioritize social connection over general apps, justifying the sunk costs of a custom OS.

However, the attack reveals fatal flaws. First, the "no application store" constraint contradicts the 2010 market reality where rival smartphones offer large app catalogs; teens, the most demographic-sensitive to trends, will perceive a device without apps as obsolete immediately upon launch. Second, the pricing strategy is misaligned: a data plan costing as much as a full smartphone plan is prohibitive for teenagers, who typically rely on parental subsidies or prepaid options, making the total cost of ownership uncompetitive. Third, abandoning the company's existing mobile platform for a new, unproven OS fragments support and increases risk without a clear technical advantage, especially when the primary value proposition (messaging) could likely run on the established platform. Finally, the "sunk cost" of two years of development is a fallacy; continuing to invest in a flawed product based on past spending guarantees future losses.

The proposal fails the "do nothing" or "cheaper alternative" test. A better path would be to port the messaging features to the company's existing platform or a dominant third-party OS, removing the barrier of a new ecosystem and allowing for a competitive hardware price point. The current plan combines high price, low utility (no apps), and high execution risk (new OS).

There is no kill criterion that saves this; the fundamental value proposition is broken at launch. The lack of an app store alone is a fatal defect in this era, regardless of the messaging brand's popularity. The high data cost further shrinks the addressable market to near zero.

DECISION: NO-GO
