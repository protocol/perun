**Steelman:** An annual fee prepaid by customers funds unlimited two-day shipping, converting variable friction into fixed revenue to lock in loyalty and increase order frequency, accepting short-term margin compression for long-term lifetime value growth.

**Attack & Alternatives:**
The primary failure mode is adverse selection: heavy buyers extract more value than they pay, while light buyers subsidize them, causing net margin erosion if frequency uplift exceeds the $79 break-even point (approx. 8-10 extra orders depending on average shipping cost). The "hard to reverse" risk is real; cancelling mid-year triggers refunds and churn, creating a ratchet effect.

Alternatives include:
1.  **Do Nothing:** Maintain status quo; retain margin but lose repeat customers to emerging competitors offering free shipping.
2.  **Threshold-Based Free Shipping:** Offer free shipping only on orders over a high dollar amount (e.g., $50+). This protects margins on small baskets but fails to solve the "friction" problem for frequent, small purchases.
3.  **Paid Tier with Caps:** A membership that offers discounted shipping rather than free, or limits the number of free shipments. This reduces the value proposition and adoption rates.

**Pre-mortem:** The program launches, heavy users double their order frequency immediately. Shipping costs per user rise to $120 annually against $79 revenue. The company burns cash on every loyal customer. Because the promise was "unlimited," pulling back causes a PR disaster and mass cancellations. The short-term hit becomes a long-term structural loss.

**Calibration:**
Is this fatal? Only if the retailer cannot sustain the cash burn long enough for increased order volume to drive downstream margin (product sales) that offsets shipping losses. The proposal admits economics are "unproven." Launching broadly without a controlled pilot to verify the elasticity of demand and the net margin impact per user is reckless. However, the strategic logic (removing friction to drive frequency) is sound if the unit economics eventually work. The risk is manageable *if* treated as a calculated bet with a strict kill switch based on net margin per member, not just revenue.

The proposal lacks a specific pilot plan or defined kill criteria, but the core mechanism is a viable strategy to dominate market share if the company has the balance sheet to endure the initial loss leader phase. The "unproven" nature demands a go-forward with extreme caution, not a stop.

DECISION: GO
