The proposal attempts to displace a market leader by attacking its greatest strength: the integrated ecosystem. However, the plan contains a fatal internal contradiction. The company previously promoted a specific copy-protection standard to partners and device makers. This new proposal explicitly drops that standard to enable wireless sharing. This action alienates the very partners whose content and distribution are required to launch. Without label support, the store fails; without the established DRM, partner devices cannot interoperate, fracturing the network effect the "wireless sharing" feature relies on.

The "do nothing" alternative is superior because entering a winner-take-all market late, without a unique content advantage, and while actively antagonizing supply chain partners, guarantees capital destruction. The marketing budget cannot compensate for a product that violates existing industry agreements and lacks the licensed content to function. The risk of labels restricting sharing is not merely a possibility but a near certainty given the abandonment of the agreed-upon protection scheme.

Furthermore, the "larger screen" and "desktop client" are commoditized features that do not overcome the switching costs of a loyal user base tied to the dominant player's library. The proposal fails the "fit" test: it moves away from the industry standard the company helped create, isolating the product before it ships. There is no viable path to content acquisition if the company reneges on the security standards it championed. The pre-mortem is clear: the launch occurs with a limited catalog, no partner device support, and immediate legal or contractual pushback from labels, resulting in a stranded hardware platform.

This is not a case for revision; the core mechanism (dropping DRM) undermines the business prerequisite (content licensing). The cost of delay is irrelevant because the current plan is structurally unsound.

DECISION: NO-GO
