**Steelman:** A massive ad spend can overwrite the "kids' brand" perception, forcing adults to trial a premium burger that proves the food quality matches the price, thereby unlocking a new, higher-margin demographic.

**Attack:** The proposal fails the "fit" and "evidence" checks. First, brand architecture: a "child-centered image" creates a psychological barrier for adults seeking premium experiences that advertising alone rarely breaks; the signal (kids) contradicts the offer (premium). Second, unit economics: pricing above the core menu risks alienating the existing value-seeking base without a guaranteed conversion of the new adult segment. Third, and most critically, the risk of "heavy spending before demand is shown" is treated as a manageable risk rather than a fatal flaw. In 1996, without digital targeting or rapid A/B testing capabilities, committing the "largest budget the industry has seen" prior to validating demand in a limited market is not a strategy; it is gambling with the company's liquidity.

**Better Way:** The "do nothing" or "staged" alternative dominates. A regional pilot in a mature market with a modest budget would validate if adults will actually pay the premium and if the brand can stretch. If the pilot fails, the loss is contained. If it succeeds, the big budget follows. The current proposal skips the learning phase entirely.

**Pre-mortem:** Assume the launch failed. Why? The ads drove curiosity, but adults walked in, saw the playplaces and cheap toys, felt the brand wasn't "for them," tasted the burger, found it indistinguishable from the kids' fare, and left. The massive spend burned cash without changing long-term perception, while the price hike confused loyal customers.

**Kill Criteria:** This is a one-way door with irreversible financial damage if it fails. There is no tripwire mentioned to stop the spending if early sales metrics miss targets. The lack of a "prove demand first" step makes the risk unacceptably high. The proposal demands belief over evidence.

**Verdict:** The plan relies on a brute-force budget to solve a nuanced brand positioning problem without validating the core assumption: that adults want premium food from a kids' brand. The financial exposure is too great for an unproven hypothesis.

DECISION: NO-GO
