**Steelman:** A massive ad spend can overwrite the "kids' brand" perception, forcing adult trial of a premium burger that generates higher margins and diversifies revenue.

**Attack:** The proposal fails the "fit" and "evidence" checks. First, brand architecture: a "child-centered image" creates a psychological barrier for adults seeking premium dining; heavy advertising often amplifies the existing brand identity rather than replacing it, potentially confusing the core base without securing the new one. Second, the "largest budget in industry history" is an unsupported claim of sufficiency; no evidence is provided that ad spend alone overcomes product-brand mismatch (e.g., McDonald's Arch Deluxe later faced this exact failure). Third, the risk of deterring regular buyers is acknowledged but not mitigated; raising prices or shifting focus may erode the volume-dependent economics of fast food.

**Better Way:** Before an industry-record spend, test the product in a limited market with targeted, low-cost messaging to verify if adults actually perceive the burger as "premium" despite the brand. If the product fails to gain traction without massive subsidies, no amount of advertising will fix the fundamental brand-product disconnect. A "do nothing" or "small pilot" approach preserves capital while gathering real demand data.

**Pre-mortem:** Assume the launch failed. Why? Because adults refused to pay premium prices at a "kids' place," and the massive ad spend burned cash without changing deep-seated perception. The core value customers felt alienated by the shift in focus and pricing.

**Kill Criteria:** The proposal lacks a specific metric to stop the bleed. Without a defined pilot phase or a cap on spending tied to adoption rates, this is an irreversible bet on a hypothesis that contradicts the chain's established equity. The risk of brand dilution and financial loss from an unproven premium strategy outweighs the potential upside.

**Verdict:** The plan commits to maximum spend before validating demand or brand elasticity. It ignores the structural conflict between a "child-centered" image and "premium" positioning.

DECISION: NO-GO
