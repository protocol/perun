The claim is that a $1,500 head-worn display with a camera, launched via an invitation-only program in 2013, will succeed as an everyday consumer product. The steelman posits that exclusivity will drive desire among early adopters, masking the lack of clear utility until the ecosystem matures.

However, the attack reveals fatal flaws in the "everyday" positioning. First, the price ($1,500) is an order of magnitude higher than established everyday accessories (watches, glasses), restricting it to luxury novelty status rather than mass utility. Second, the explicit risks of privacy objections and social acceptance are not mere hurdles but fundamental blockers for an "everyday" device; a camera recording bystanders without clear indication creates immediate social friction and potential legal liability that an invitation-only launch cannot solve. Third, short battery life and voice control limitations in 2013 technology stacks suggest the device cannot sustain "everyday" usage patterns, relegating it to a short-duration gadget.

The "do nothing" alternative (waiting for battery density, social norms, and use cases to mature) dominates this proposal. Launching now forces the market to define the use case at a premium price point while actively antagonizing the public (privacy). The proposal assumes that "early adopter" enthusiasm will override deep-seated social and privacy concerns, a hypothesis contradicted by the nature of wearable cameras in public spaces. There is no kill criterion or pivot plan offered for when social backlash occurs, which is a near-certainty given the premises. The cost of delay is negligible compared to the reputational damage of launching a controversial, expensive product with no clear daily value.

The strongest attack is the mismatch between the "everyday consumer product" positioning and the reality of a $1,500 privacy-invasive prototype. The evidence provided (high price, privacy risks, no clear use case) directly contradicts the feasibility of mass adoption. This is not a go-if scenario; the foundational product-market fit for "everyday" use is missing.

DECISION: NO-GO
