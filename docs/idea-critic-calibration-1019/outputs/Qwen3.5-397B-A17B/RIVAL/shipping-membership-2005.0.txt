This proposal trades immediate margin compression for long-term customer lifetime value (LTV) expansion. The core friction—per-order shipping fees—is a proven barrier to frequency in retail economics. Removing it converts variable friction into a fixed sunk cost for the customer, psychologically unlocking higher order volume.

The risks cited are valid but manageable with specific guardrails:
1.  **Adverse Selection:** Heavy buyers will indeed cost more per unit. However, they are also the most loyal. The $79 fee acts as a screening mechanism; only customers confident in their future spend will opt in. This creates a self-selected cohort of high-LTV users.
2.  **Margin Hit:** The short-term hit is real. This must be modeled as a Customer Acquisition Cost (CAC) investment, not an operational loss. If membership increases annual order frequency by even 20%, the gross profit delta likely offsets the shipping subsidy within two orders.
3.  **Irreversibility:** Once launched, removing the benefit causes churn. Mitigation requires a "grandfathering" clause in the terms of service, allowing the retailer to raise prices for *new* members while honoring the $79 rate for existing ones. This preserves trust while allowing economic correction.
4.  **Unproven Economics:** No model is perfect before launch. The solution is a controlled geographic or invite-only beta. Launch to 5% of the email list, measure the ratio of (Membership Revenue + Incremental Gross Profit) vs. (Incremental Shipping Cost), and iterate the price or threshold before full rollout.

The strategic upside extends beyond shipping math. A membership creates a recurring revenue stream, smooths cash flow, and generates first-party data on committed buyers. It shifts the relationship from transactional to relational. In 2005, before widespread subscription fatigue, this is a differentiating moat against competitors charging per delivery.

The proposal lacks specific eligibility thresholds (e.g., minimum order size) and exclusion zones (e.g., oversized items), which are critical for unit economics. However, these are implementation details, not strategic blockers. The core thesis—monetize loyalty rather than logistics—is sound. The risk of inaction (losing high-frequency shoppers to future competitors) outweighs the risk of a controlled launch.

DECISION: GO
