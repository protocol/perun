This proposal represents a classic asymmetric strategy: refusing to fight a stronger competitor on their terms (raw horsepower and media features) and instead changing the battlefield entirely.

The rival is playing a "spec war," competing on graphics and media playback. This is a commodity trap where margins erode, and the incumbent with deeper pockets usually wins. By attempting to match their hardware, the company would release an inferior product at a similar price point, guaranteeing failure.

The dual-screen, touch-sensitive approach pivots the value proposition from "better graphics" to "new gameplay." This leverages the company's core strength: a library of creative IP and strong developer relationships, rather than silicon manufacturing. While the risks regarding developer confusion and the "gimmick" label are valid, they are manageable. Developers follow installed base and engaged users; if the hardware enables unique experiences (like a dedicated inventory map or touch-based puzzles), software will follow. The "gimmick" perception is a marketing challenge, not a product flaw, solvable by demonstrating killer apps that utilize the second screen meaningfully.

Financially, keeping the older model on sale is a masterstroke. It creates a price-tiered portfolio: the new device captures the early adopters and innovation-seeking mass market, while the legacy device serves the price-sensitive segment and extends the life of existing software libraries. This maximizes total addressable market while diluting the rival's singular focus on high-end graphics.

The lower price point is critical. It reduces the barrier to entry for the "broadened audience" (casual gamers, younger players) who are intimidated by expensive, complex consoles. This aligns perfectly with the goal of expanding beyond core players.

The risk of unproven touch mechanics is actually an opportunity for first-mover advantage in a new interaction paradigm. If successful, the company defines the next generation of gaming interfaces, forcing the rival to play catch-up in software innovation rather than hardware specs.

The proposal correctly identifies that winning requires differentiation, not imitation. It protects the core business while aggressively pursuing growth in a new direction. The risks are inherent to innovation but are outweighed by the certainty of losing a direct hardware shootout.

DECISION: GO
