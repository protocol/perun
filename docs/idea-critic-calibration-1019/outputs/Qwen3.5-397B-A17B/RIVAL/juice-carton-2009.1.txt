This proposal represents a high-risk, high-reward brand transformation that fails a critical stress test: **recognition latency**.

The core asset of a CPG (consumer packaged goods) brand on a crowded shelf is instant visual recognition. The "orange with a straw" is not merely decoration; it is a heuristic that allows loyal, older buyers to identify the product in under 200 milliseconds. Removing this cue to adopt a "minimalist" aesthetic prioritizes artistic modernization over behavioral reality.

The demographic risk is acute. An older, loyal base relies on established patterns. Changing the primary visual cue while keeping the recipe and price constant creates a "phantom stockout" scenario: the customer looks for their usual orange, sees a glass instead, assumes the brand is out of stock, and purchases a competitor. Once that habit loop is broken, reacquiring that customer is exponentially more expensive than retaining them.

While the new design elements (glass, orange cap, vertical text) may test well in a focus group or look superior in a boardroom presentation, they do not replicate the shelf impact of the existing asset. A nationwide, big-bang launch eliminates the ability to learn, iterate, or mitigate regional failures. If recognition drops by even 15% in the first month, the revenue hole will be immediate and deep, and the advertising budget will be spent educating confused customers rather than driving trial.

The proposal treats the brand identity as a static logo rather than a functional shelf tool. Minimalism is a valid strategy, but not when it severs the neural link between the shopper's need and the physical product, especially for a demographic resistant to change. The cost of redesign is sunk; the cost of lost shelf share is recurring.

To approve this is to gamble the core revenue stream on an unproven visual language. The safe path is evolution, not revolution: introduce the new design as a limited edition or phase it in slowly while retaining the orange cue. As proposed, the risk of alienating the core base outweighs the potential aesthetic gain.

DECISION: NO-GO
