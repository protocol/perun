The proposal demands a fundamental shift from selling hardware to operating a persistent service platform. In 2002, broadband penetration is critically low, likely under 5% of households. Mandating broadband immediately excludes the vast majority of the installed base, artificially capping the addressable market for every title.

The expectation that online play should be free is entrenched by PC gaming norms and rival console strategies. Introducing a mandatory annual subscription creates a double-barrier entry: high hardware cost plus recurring fees. This friction will severely dampen adoption rates during the crucial early lifecycle phase when network effects are most needed.

Operationally, the company lacks experience in running large-scale hosting and customer support for live services. Building this infrastructure from scratch while simultaneously marketing a new behavioral model (paid online) introduces execution risk that could destabilize the core hardware business. Failure in service reliability would damage the brand's reputation for quality.

However, the strategic upside of a unified identity, friends list, and matchmaking is transformative. It creates a "walled garden" that locks users into the ecosystem, driving long-term software attachment and recurring revenue. If successful, it establishes an insurmountable moat against competitors relying on fragmented, publisher-led solutions. The vision is correct for the long term, but the timing and mandatory nature of the rollout in 2002 are premature given the infrastructure and cultural constraints.

Launching now with these specific constraints (mandatory broadband, mandatory paid subscription for all) risks alienating the early adopter base and handing market share to rivals offering lower-friction alternatives. The service architecture is sound, but the go-to-market strategy requires adjustment to match current reality, perhaps starting with optional tiers or hybrid free/premium models to build the user base before enforcing full monetization.

As proposed, with strict mandates on connectivity and payment in an unready market, the risk of commercial failure outweighs the strategic potential at this specific moment.

DECISION: NO-GO
