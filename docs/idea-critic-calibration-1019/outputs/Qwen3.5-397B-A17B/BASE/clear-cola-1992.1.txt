The proposal to launch a clear cola nationwide in 1992 carries significant strategic vulnerabilities that outweigh the positive test market results. While the concept of a "purer" alternative resonates in controlled environments, the identified risks suggest a high probability of commercial failure upon full-scale deployment.

The primary concern is the fundamental disconnect between consumer expectations and the product offering. Cola flavor is psychologically linked to dark color; removing this visual cue may confuse shoppers rather than attract them. Test markets often inflate demand due to novelty effects, yet the proposal admits that repeat purchases are uncertain. If the product fails to retain customers after the initial trial, the substantial investment in a large marketing campaign will yield poor returns.

Furthermore, the threat of cannibalization poses a direct danger to the company's core revenue stream. As the second-largest maker, protecting the flagship brand is critical. Diverting existing customers to a new, potentially fleeting product without guaranteeing net new growth could erode overall market share. The risk is compounded by the likelihood of rapid imitation by rivals, who could launch copies quickly, diluting any first-mover advantage and turning the category into a price war.

Given the slowing growth in the soft-drink sector, resources should be allocated to initiatives with more predictable outcomes. Launching a major brand extension based on a novelty trait, while facing strong consumer skepticism regarding flavor-color association and high cannibalization potential, is imprudent. The current data does not support bypassing further refinement or additional validation steps. The risks of damaging the flagship brand and wasting marketing capital on a non-repeat purchase cycle are too great to proceed immediately as proposed.

DECISION: NO-GO
