The proposal introduces a paid, console-wide online service in 2002, requiring broadband and an annual subscription. This contrasts sharply with competitors who offer free online play or leave it to individual publishers. The plan includes advanced features like a shared identity, friends lists, voice chat, and universal matchmaking. However, significant risks threaten immediate viability.

First, broadband adoption in 2002 is extremely low. Mandating this connectivity excludes the vast majority of potential users who still rely on dial-up, drastically shrinking the addressable market before launch. Second, consumer expectations are firmly set on free online interactions. Introducing a mandatory fee on top of the console price creates a double-barrier to entry that rivals do not impose. This pricing strategy risks alienating the existing user base and stifling software sales, which are often the primary revenue driver for hardware manufacturers.

Furthermore, the company lacks any prior experience in running hosting and support operations. Building this infrastructure from scratch while simultaneously launching a consumer-facing service introduces massive operational uncertainty. Failure in server stability or customer support could damage the brand reputation irreparably. The combination of limited market readiness, hostile pricing perception, and internal capability gaps makes an immediate launch dangerous.

While the vision of a unified online ecosystem is forward-thinking, executing it now ignores critical market realities. The infrastructure is not widespread enough, the cost model fights established consumer norms, and the organization is unprepared for the operational load. Proceeding immediately would likely result in low adoption, high churn, and unsustainable costs. The proposal requires substantial revision to address connectivity barriers, reconsider the mandatory fee structure, and establish operational readiness before any launch can be considered.

DECISION: NO-GO
