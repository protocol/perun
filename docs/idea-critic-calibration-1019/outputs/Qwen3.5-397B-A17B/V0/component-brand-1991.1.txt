The claim is that a component manufacturer can successfully drive demand by branding directly to end-users, forcing retailers and OEMs to comply. The steelman: if consumers recognize and demand the logo, computer makers lose pricing power and must adopt the chip to sell units, justifying the ad subsidy despite channel friction.

However, critical assumptions remain unverified. First, it assumes consumers care about an invisible component enough to change purchasing behavior, despite zero precedent for B2B2C branding in this sector. Second, it presumes computer makers will accept reduced control over their own advertising narratives in exchange for partial funding, rather than viewing this as an intrusion or negotiating the subsidy without displaying the logo. Third, the proposal risks alienating major partners who may perceive the campaign as the supplier attempting to capture the brand equity of the final product.

The "do nothing" alternative preserves current margins and partner relationships. A "better way" might involve co-branding strictly on technical spec sheets or retailer training, avoiding the high cost and risk of mass media. The cost of delay is low; the chip market in 1991 is driven by OEM specifications, not consumer pull. If the campaign fails, the sunk marketing cost is unrecoverable, and damaged OEM relationships could lead to lost design wins—a one-way door with high reversal costs.

The strongest attack is that consumer demand cannot be manufactured for a product they cannot see or test, rendering the subsidy a pure cost with no leverage. The proposal lacks a pilot test with a single willing OEM to validate the "ask rate" before committing a large share of the total budget. Without evidence that consumers will actually ask for the chip by name, the entire strategy rests on a hypothetical behavioral shift. The risks of channel conflict and wasted capital outweigh the unproven potential benefits.

DECISION: NO-GO
