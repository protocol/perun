The claim is that a computer manufacturer should immediately launch a tablet running a newly acquired, niche mobile operating system, priced at parity with the market leader, despite a minimal app ecosystem and internal leadership transition.

The steelman: Leveraging existing hardware expertise and a fresh OS allows for a differentiated, integrated ecosystem that, if supported by aggressive developer incentives, can carve out a profitable niche despite late entry.

However, the attack reveals fatal flaws. First, pricing a device with a fraction of the competitor's apps at market parity ignores the primary value driver for tablets: software availability. Consumers will not pay a premium for an unproven platform with limited utility. Second, launching during leadership transition creates strategic drift; if the vision shifts mid-cycle, the product becomes orphaned. Third, the "small app catalog" is not a temporary hurdle but a structural barrier; developer outreach takes years to mature, not months. The cost of delay is negligible compared to the reputational damage and financial loss of a failed launch.

Better ways exist: License the OS to third-party hardware makers first to build market share without capital risk, or delay the hardware launch until the app ecosystem reaches a critical mass (e.g., top 100 apps). Alternatively, price the device significantly below the leader to compensate for the software deficit, accepting lower margins to gain users.

The pre-mortem: The device launches, sells poorly due to lack of apps, developers ignore the platform seeing low adoption, and the company writes off the investment while distracting from its core computer business. This is a one-way door with high sunk costs.

No kill criteria are defined to stop the bleed if early sales miss targets. The proposal assumes marketing spend can substitute for product-market fit, a historically false premise in platform businesses.

DECISION: NO-GO
