The claim is that a radical visual overhaul will refresh the brand without eroding recognition. The steelman: the current "orange with straw" cue is becoming dated, and a modern "glass + cap" aesthetic combined with heavy advertising will transfer equity to a sharper identity, securing relevance for the next decade.

However, the attack reveals a critical fragility. The proposal relies entirely on a "large advertising campaign" to bridge the gap between the old mental model and the new package. For a loyal, older demographic, shelf recognition is often subconscious and instantaneous. Removing the primary visual anchor (the orange with straw) creates a high probability of "shelf blindness," where loyalists simply walk past, assuming the product is out of stock or changed fundamentally. Advertising supports awareness, but it does not guarantee split-second visual retrieval in a crowded aisle.

The "do nothing" alternative or a hybrid evolution (keeping the orange cue while modernizing typography) offers lower risk. The proposed "all stores at once" launch is a one-way door with massive sunk costs; if recognition fails, the brand cannot easily revert without admitting defeat and confusing the market further. The cost of delay is low compared to the cost of a failed national rollout. There is no evidence provided that the older demographic responds positively to minimalism over familiarity, nor is there a pilot plan to test the new cue's visibility against the old.

The strongest attack survived is the assumption that advertising spend can override decades of conditioned visual habit instantly. This fails because it treats brand recognition as a fact to be communicated rather than a reflex to be triggered. Without a phased rollout to verify shelf grab-rate, the risk of catastrophic sales drop is unmitigated. The proposal lacks a kill criterion or a safe rollback path for a partial failure.

DECISION: NO-GO
