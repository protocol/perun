**Steelman:** Transform an invisible component into a consumer demand driver by subsidizing OEM ads, forcing buyers to pull the product through the channel.

**Attack & Alternatives:**
The primary failure mode is channel conflict: if major computer makers refuse the branding constraints, the budget burns with zero reach. However, the "clone" threat makes status quo ("do nothing") fatal; without differentiation, the product becomes a commodity. Alternatives like trade-only promotions fail to stop end-users from accepting inferior clones. The "unprecedented" nature of consumer ads for components is a feature, not a bug—it creates a monopoly on mindshare before competitors react.

**Fit & Pre-mortem:**
This aligns with the north star of escaping commodity pricing. If it fails, it will be due to weak OEM adoption, not consumer indifference. This is a two-way door: if sales lift doesn't materialize in two quarters, the subsidy stops immediately. The cost of delay is high; every month allows clones to cement "good enough" perceptions.

**Evidence & Metrics:**
The proposal relies on the verified fact that buyers currently cannot identify the chip. The mechanism (co-op advertising) is standard practice, merely applied to a new layer. The risk of OEM resentment is mitigated by the financial incentive (free ad spend).

**Kill Criteria:**
Launch only if at least two top-tier OEMs sign binding agreements within 60 days. Stop immediately if branded ad spend exceeds 15% of the marketing budget without a corresponding 5% increase in premium-tier sales volume within six months.

**Verdict:**
The skepticism regarding "unprecedented" tactics ignores that category creation requires breaking norms. The financial leverage over OEMs outweighs the friction of creative control. The risk of inaction (commoditization) vastly exceeds the risk of execution. The kill criteria provide a safe exit if the channel rebels or consumers remain indifferent.

DECISION: GO
