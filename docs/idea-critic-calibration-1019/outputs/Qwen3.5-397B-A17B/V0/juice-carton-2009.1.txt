The claim is that a radical visual overhaul will refresh the brand without eroding its core equity. The steelman: the current orange-with-straw icon is becoming dated, and a modern, minimalist aesthetic combined with aggressive advertising will attract new buyers while the cap shape preserves functional recognition.

However, the attack reveals a fatal flaw in the "do nothing" alternative analysis. The proposal admits the old image is the primary shelf cue for a loyal, older demographic. In a low-involvement category like juice, shoppers scan for patterns, not details. Removing the dominant pattern (the orange image) to rely on a "cap shape" and "vertical text" assumes a level of consumer attention that does not exist in a crowded aisle. The risk is not just confusion; it is invisibility. If the loyal base fails to recognize the product, they will not "learn" the new design; they will simply buy the competitor's familiar carton.

The "better way" analysis fails because the proposal rejects a phased approach. Launching nationwide simultaneously removes the ability to measure sales erosion in a controlled environment before committing the entire revenue stream. A regional pilot could validate if the advertising spend actually overcomes the loss of the visual cue. Without this, the company is betting the entire brand on an unproven hypothesis regarding shelf visibility.

The pre-mortem is clear: six months post-launch, sales have collapsed because the target demographic walked past the cartons, perceiving them as a new, unknown brand rather than their usual choice. The advertising budget was insufficient to retrain millions of habitual buyers instantly. The cost of delay (waiting to test) is negligible compared to the cost of a failed national rollout.

There is no kill criterion defined to stop the rollout if early regional data (had it existed) showed a dip. The proposal demands an irreversible, high-cost switch based on the assumption that "new design" equals "growth," ignoring the specific mechanism of shelf recognition. The evidence provided explicitly highlights the risk of losing the main cue, yet the plan proceeds as if advertising alone solves physical visibility.

DECISION: NO-GO
