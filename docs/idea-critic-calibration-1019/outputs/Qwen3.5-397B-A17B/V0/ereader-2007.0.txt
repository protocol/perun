The claim is that a non-hardware bookseller should launch a proprietary e-reader with free cellular connectivity to dominate the emerging digital market. The steelman: owning the device and distribution pipeline creates a defensible moat, removes friction for buyers, and secures publisher buy-in via DRM and controlled pricing, outweighing the risks of hardware inexperience.

Alternatives include licensing software to existing device makers (low risk, low control), waiting for the market to mature (misses first-mover advantage), or selling PDFs on generic devices (poor user experience). Doing nothing cedes the digital future to competitors.

However, critical failure modes exist. Hardware margins are notoriously thin; bundling free cellular data shifts costs to the retailer, requiring massive volume to break even. If adoption lags, the company bleeds cash on inventory and data plans without recouping via book sales. Publisher anxiety over piracy and margin erosion could lead to content strikes if the pricing model is too aggressive. Cannibalization of high-margin print sales is a real threat if the digital price point is too low.

Yet, the "paper-like" screen addresses the primary consumer complaint of earlier devices (eye strain), and the "no computer needed" feature solves the friction of sideloading. The integrated model (device + store + connectivity) is the only way to guarantee a seamless experience that drives volume. The risk of hardware inexperience is mitigated by partnering with established manufacturers rather than building factories. The risk of cannibalization is acceptable if the alternative is losing the customer entirely to a competitor's ecosystem.

The strongest attack is financial ruin from fixed costs (data plans, hardware R&D) before network effects kick in. This fails only if the company has deep enough cash reserves to subsidize the loss leader for years. Assuming they do, the strategic necessity of owning the customer relationship in the digital age overrides the operational risks. The kill criterion is clear: if device sales do not reach a specific threshold within 18 months to justify the data subsidies, the project must pivot to a software-only model.

DECISION: GO
