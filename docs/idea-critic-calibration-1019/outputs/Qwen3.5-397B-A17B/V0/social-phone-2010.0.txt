The proposal fails the "better-way" and "fit" tests immediately. Launching two phones on a new, unproven operating system without an application store in 2010 ignores the primary driver of smartphone adoption for teenagers: apps. Rivals already possess large catalogs; entering the market with zero apps is a non-starter for a demographic defined by social connectivity and third-party utility. The "social-networking" focus is insufficient without the ecosystem to support it.

The distribution strategy is equally flawed. Restricting sales to a single carrier with a mandatory data plan priced at full smartphone rates creates an insurmountable barrier for teenagers, who are highly price-sensitive and often rely on family plans or prepaid options. This pricing and distribution model contradicts the target audience's economic reality.

Furthermore, the decision to build a new in-house OS rather than leveraging the company's existing mobile platform fractures engineering resources and delays time-to-market, ignoring the "sunk cost" fallacy. Two years of development on a dead-end path should not dictate future strategy; it should trigger a pivot to the existing, supported platform. The lack of an app store at launch is a fatal omission, not a temporary gap, as network effects require immediate utility.

The pre-mortem is clear: these phones will launch to poor reviews citing the lack of apps and high cost, resulting in negligible market share and rapid discontinuation, wasting the acquisition cost of the messaging phone maker. The "do nothing" or "revise" options (launch one phone, on the existing OS, with an app store, across multiple carriers) are strictly superior. As proposed, this plan guarantees failure.

DECISION: NO-GO
