The claim is that a $1,500 head-worn display with a camera, launched via an invitation-only program in 2013, will succeed as an everyday consumer product. The steelman is that exclusivity creates hype, early adopters will tolerate flaws, and the "cool factor" overrides privacy concerns.

However, the attack reveals fatal flaws in the "everyday" positioning. First, the price ($1,500) is prohibitive for mass adoption, contradicting the goal of an "everyday consumer product." Second, the privacy objections regarding a always-on camera are not merely social friction; they represent a fundamental barrier to entry in public spaces (banks, gyms, theaters), effectively killing the "everyday" use case regardless of early adopter enthusiasm. Third, the lack of a clear everyday use for most people means the device solves no urgent problem, making it a luxury novelty rather than a necessity. The "invitation-only" strategy may generate short-term buzz, but it does not solve the core value proposition gap.

The "do nothing" alternative (waiting for battery tech to improve, prices to drop, and social norms to evolve) carries less risk than launching a product likely to be banned in public venues and ridiculed by the mainstream. The cost of delay is low compared to the reputational damage of a failed mainstream launch. The pre-mortem shows the product failing because it is socially unacceptable and too expensive, leading to a quick pivot or discontinuation.

There is no evidence provided that the privacy concerns can be mitigated technically or socially in 2013. The kill criteria for success (mass adoption) cannot be met with the current constraints. The strongest attack survived is the assumption that early adopters can bridge the gap to the mainstream; history suggests that without a clear, non-controversial utility, they cannot.

DECISION: NO-GO
