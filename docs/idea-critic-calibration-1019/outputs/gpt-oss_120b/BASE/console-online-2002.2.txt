The proposal aims to create a unified, subscription‑based online service that would give every player a single identity, friends list, voice chat and matchmaking across all titles. In 2002 the console is brand‑new, but two critical market conditions work against a mandatory paid model:

1. **Broadband availability** – In 2001‑02 only a minority of households have high‑speed Internet; most gamers still use dial‑up or no connection at all. Requiring broadband as a prerequisite will exclude a large segment of the console’s potential install base and could depress hardware sales.

2. **Consumer pricing expectations** – Competitors either provide online play for free or leave pricing to individual publishers. Early‑adopter gamers are accustomed to free multiplayer and may view a blanket subscription as a barrier, especially when the console itself already carries a premium price.

Operationally, the company has never run a large‑scale hosting or support operation. Building data‑centers, scaling bandwidth, handling account management and 24‑hour support would demand significant upfront capital and expertise, stretching resources away from core game development.

The upside—recurring revenue, a sticky ecosystem, and a differentiated service—remains attractive, but the current proposition is mismatched to the market’s readiness and the firm’s capabilities. A more viable path would be to launch a free tier with optional paid enhancements (e.g., premium voice chat, advanced matchmaking) while piloting the infrastructure in limited regions. This would gather usage data, test the technology stack and avoid alienating early adopters.

Given the high risk of low adoption and operational overload, the plan as written should not be approved now.

DECISION: NO-GO
