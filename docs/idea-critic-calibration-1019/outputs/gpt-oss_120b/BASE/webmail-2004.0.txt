The proposal leverages a strong differentiator— 1 GB of free storage and rapid, full‑text search— that could attract power users and early adopters. However, three concrete risk areas make the current plan untenable without mitigation.

**1. Storage and infrastructure cost**  
In 2004 a gigabyte of disk space still represented a significant expense. Even with invitation‑only rollout, the per‑user cost of hardware, power, cooling and redundancy would quickly outpace any early‑stage ad revenue. The proposal offers no cost‑control strategy (e.g., tiered storage, compression, or a clear break‑even user count). Without a clear path to amortize the storage expense, the service could become a financial drain.

**2. Privacy and ad‑targeting**  
Scanning users’ mail to serve text ads raises immediate privacy concerns. Users are accustomed to “spam‑free” experiences; a system that parses message content for commercial gain will likely encounter backlash, legal scrutiny, and high churn. The proposal does not include user consent mechanisms, opt‑out options, or a transparent privacy policy, all of which are essential to reduce reputational risk.

**3. Market dynamics**  
The free webmail market is dominated by entrenched players (e.g., Hotmail, Yahoo! Mail) and a new entrant (Google Gmail) is about to launch with the same 1 GB promise. Competing on storage alone is insufficient; the service must offer a compelling, differentiated experience that does not rely on invasive ad targeting. The lack of any immediate revenue stream further weakens the business case against well‑funded incumbents.

**Conclusion**  
While the technical ambition aligns with the company’s search expertise, the current plan leaves critical financial, legal and competitive gaps unaddressed. The executive team should require a revised proposal that:

* Defines a realistic cost model and break‑even user volume.  
* Introduces explicit user consent and privacy safeguards for ad targeting, or an alternative non‑content‑based revenue model.  
* Outlines a clear go‑to‑market strategy that differentiates beyond storage capacity.

Until those gaps are closed, approving the launch as described would expose the company to unsustainable costs and reputational damage.

DECISION: NO-GO
