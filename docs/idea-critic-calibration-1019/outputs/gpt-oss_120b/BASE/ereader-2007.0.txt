The catalog size gives the company a clear content advantage, but the proposal attempts to leap into a hardware market where it has no track record. Earlier dedicated e‑readers have shown limited consumer demand, and the added complexity of a “paper‑like” display plus a free cellular link raises development cost and supply‑chain risk. Without prior hardware experience, the company would need to build new design, manufacturing, quality‑control, and support capabilities—all of which would divert focus from its core retail and publishing relationships.

Pricing e‑books at a single low level could attract readers, yet it compresses margins that already depend on publisher‑set royalties. Publishers already fear piracy; a low‑price, always‑connected device may intensify that concern and jeopardize existing agreements, potentially limiting the catalog the company can offer. Moreover, a cheap, convenient digital format risks cannibalizing the higher‑margin print business that still drives a large portion of revenue.

A less risky path would be to leverage the company’s content strengths while avoiding hardware exposure: launch a software‑only e‑reader app for existing devices, negotiate bundled data plans with carriers, or partner with an established e‑reader manufacturer to embed the store. These alternatives preserve the brand’s entry into digital sales, protect margins, and give publishers confidence through familiar distribution channels.

Given the high development cost, lack of hardware expertise, margin pressure, and potential publisher resistance, the current proposal is not ready for immediate execution.

DECISION: NO-GO
