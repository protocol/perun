The compact model already proves reliable and inexpensive, giving the new coupe‑convertible a low‑cost mechanical base. Re‑using those parts can keep development outlays well below a clean‑sheet program, which is a strong financial advantage.

The target – post‑war baby‑boomers now reaching buying age – is documented to be looking for “sporty styling at a low price.” Competitors have shown that the combination of a long‑hood, short‑deck silhouette and a rich option list can generate strong margins when the vehicle is positioned as a lifestyle product rather than a pure economy car.

Key risks:

1. **Unproven volume demand for sporty small cars.** The market size is unknown, so committing to a full‑scale launch could leave excess inventory.
2. **Perception of cheapness.** Sharing parts with the economy model may dilute the premium feel required for a sport‑oriented image.
3. **Heavy advertising spend before sales are validated.** A “very large” campaign ties up cash that could be needed for inventory or dealer support if demand falls short.

Mitigation steps that keep the core concept intact:

* **Pilot the model in a limited geographic region** (e.g., two major metropolitan areas) for six months, measuring orders and dealer feedback before national rollout.
* **Phase advertising spend** – start with targeted, lower‑cost media (local radio, print, dealer events) and expand only after sales thresholds are met.
* **Differentiate styling and trim** – add unique exterior panels, interior finishes, and badging that are not shared with the economy car, preserving a distinct brand image.
* **Leverage the options list** to boost average transaction price, offsetting the low base price and covering the initial marketing outlay.

With these adjustments the project retains its upside while containing exposure. As written, the proposal’s unconditional large‑scale launch and marketing budget present an unacceptable risk profile.
