**Steelman:** Launch a fully integrated music ecosystem—hardware player with a larger screen, wireless song‑sharing, a desktop client, an online store and subscription service—backed by a sizable marketing push to win users from the incumbent.

**Attack:**  
1. **Ecosystem lock‑in:** The dominant player controls hardware, software, and DRM (e.g., FairPlay). Users are accustomed to seamless syncing; breaking that flow demands a compelling reason.  
2. **Copy‑protection conflict:** Dropping the company‑promoted DRM alienates existing partners and may breach licensing agreements, risking legal pushback and loss of goodwill.  
3. **Late entry:** By 2006 the market is already saturated; iPod sales are exploding and the iTunes Store dominates sales and discovery. A newcomer faces steep adoption curves.  
4. **Label restrictions:** Record companies may refuse to grant sharing rights or favorable revenue splits, especially for a model that undercuts their DRM‑controlled distribution.  
5. **Price parity:** Without a clear cost advantage, consumers have little incentive to switch, especially given the incumbent’s brand equity and ecosystem depth.

**Alternatives:**  
- **Do nothing** and preserve resources for core products.  
- **Partner** with the incumbent to offer a branded version of the player, leveraging existing distribution.  
- **Target a niche** (e.g., high‑resolution audio or integration with the company’s other hardware) where the incumbent is weak.

**Fit:** The proposal diverges from the company’s likely core competencies and does not advance the strategic north‑star of sustainable, differentiated growth.

**Pre‑mortem:** The venture would likely burn a large marketing budget, encounter legal disputes over DRM, suffer poor adoption, and erode partner relationships, leading to a costly failure.

Given the high market risk, lack of clear differentiation, and potential partner backlash, the proposal should not be approved in its current form.  

DECISION: NO-GO
