**Steelman:**  
Acquire the small OS‑vendor for a few hundred M USD, adopt its OO operating system as the core of the PC maker’s next platform, and reinstate the founder as an advisor to guide the transition.

**Attack & Findings**

1. **Financial mismatch** – The target’s revenue is minimal (few M USD). Paying hundreds of millions yields a > 20× price‑to‑sales multiple, far above typical software‑acquisition benchmarks, exposing the buyer to a large goodwill write‑off if integration fails.

2. **Integration risk** – Migrating legacy applications and developer tooling to an unproven OS core is a classic “one‑way‑door” move. Historical attempts have failed twice; the same internal obstacles (toolchain, driver ecosystem, OEM support) will reappear, magnified by the need to re‑engineer existing software.

3. **Founder volatility** – Prior difficult history raises a governance red flag. Even with an advisory title, the founder could clash with existing leadership, jeopardizing the already fragile integration effort.

4. **Better‑way alternative** – Licensing an established, market‑validated OS (e.g., Windows NT, Solaris) costs a fraction of the acquisition price, comes with a mature driver ecosystem, and avoids the massive migration effort. The licensing fee is predictable and can be scaled with market performance, providing a clear kill‑criterion (e.g., > 10 % cost overrun triggers renegotiation).

5. **Kill‑criteria** – If post‑acquisition integration exceeds 12 months or the combined OS fails to achieve ≥ 5 % market‑share lift within two years, the investment should be written off. No such contingency is outlined.

**Conclusion** – The proposal’s upside (control of a modern OS) is outweighed by financial overpayment, high integration risk, and governance concerns, especially when a cheaper, lower‑risk licensing path exists.

DECISION: NO-GO
