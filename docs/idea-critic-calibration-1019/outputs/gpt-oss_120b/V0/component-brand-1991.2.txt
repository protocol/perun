**Steelman claim**  
The chip maker will create a consumer‑facing logo and a subsidy program that pays a portion of computer‑makers’ advertising costs when their ads display the logo, with the aim of making end‑users ask for that chip by name and thereby lifting sales.

**Skeptic checks**  
- **Assumption 1:** End‑users care about the internal processor brand. No direct evidence is given; the only precedent is Intel’s later “Intel Inside” campaign.  
- **Assumption 2:** Computer makers will willingly incorporate a third‑party logo and accept subsidy payments. If makers feel the program dictates their creative direction, they may resist or drop participation.  
- **Assumption 3:** The subsidy will not consume an unsustainable share of the chip maker’s marketing budget. The proposal admits the program could take a “large share” but does not define a ceiling.

**Better‑way alternatives**  
1. **Co‑branding on packaging** – cheaper, gives OEM control over placement, and avoids direct ad‑creative interference.  
2. **Technical differentiation** – invest in performance/price advantages and let market signals arise organically.  
3. **Do‑nothing pilot** – run a limited‑scope test with a single OEM to validate consumer response before a full rollout.

**Kill‑criteria / pre‑mortem**  
- If after six months consumer awareness of the logo is <10 % or sales lift is <5 %, terminate the program.  
- If any OEM withdraws or publicly objects, cease subsidy payments to that partner.  
- Cap total subsidy spend at 10 % of the annual marketing budget; exceed → stop.

**Recommendation**  
Proceed with a controlled pilot: select one willing OEM, limit subsidy to 5 % of the marketing budget, and set the above awareness/sales thresholds as stop‑rules. This mitigates the biggest risks while testing the core hypothesis that brand‑level consumer demand can be generated for a component.

DECISION: GO
