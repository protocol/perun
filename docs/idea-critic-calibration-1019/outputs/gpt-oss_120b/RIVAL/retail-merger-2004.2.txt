The plan seeks to combine two financially stressed retailers in hopes of creating a larger, more competitive entity. The upside hinges on achieving cost savings from merged corporate functions and leveraging the discount‑store brand to sell the older chain’s products. However, every core assumption carries significant danger:

* **Parallel sales decline** – Both businesses are already losing market share, so the merged firm inherits double the revenue erosion rather than a stabilising base.
* **Aging, under‑invested assets** – Store footprints are old and likely require capital upgrades to meet modern shopper expectations; without a clear refurbishment budget, the combined network may continue to lose customers.
* **Uncertain synergies** – Merging back‑office operations can be complex, especially when systems, cultures and vendor contracts differ. Expected cost reductions are speculative without a detailed integration plan.
* **Customer mismatch** – Discount‑store shoppers and department‑store patrons have distinct purchasing habits and price sensitivities. A blanket brand‑selling strategy could alienate both groups rather than create cross‑selling opportunities.
* **External competitive pressure** – Big‑box rivals are expanding, offering broader assortments and stronger logistics. The merged entity would face intensified price wars and may lack the scale to compete effectively.

Given these compounded risks, the proposal lacks concrete mitigation tactics (e.g., store‑refresh funding, clear integration milestones, targeted market research on customer overlap). Approving now would commit resources to a venture whose financial upside is uncertain and whose execution challenges are sizable.

**Recommendation:** Defer approval until a detailed integration blueprint, realistic cost‑saving model, and a store‑modernisation plan are presented, with clear evidence that the combined brand can attract and retain both customer segments.

DECISION: NO-GO
